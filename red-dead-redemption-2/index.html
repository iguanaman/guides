Rockstar · Story Mode Strategy

Red Dead Redemption 2

Single-player strategy for Arthur's ride — how the honor, camp, economy and progression systems actually reward you, and how to play them well.

Plan the Run

RDR2 punishes an unplanned completion run harder than any other Rockstar game — a first attempt runs well past a hundred hours. Almost none of that is difficulty. It's revisiting the same corner of the map five times because five different tasks each sent you there separately, and it's chapter windows quietly closing behind you.

The three ways content is actually lost

A chapter window closes. Camp-bound content — companion activities, gang item requests, documents lying around the current camp — dies with the camp it belongs to, and each chapter has one story mission that shuts the door. Work backwards from that mission, not from the chapter number.

You didn't loot inside the mission. A large share of misses are documents and valuables sitting on a desk or a corpse during a mission, gone when it ends. Two letters in Cornwall's private carriage in the prologue set the tone for the whole game: search the room you're in before the objective marker pulls you out.

A random encounter passed you by. Most of the game's missable letters are one-shot loot on random-event bodies. Ride past a hanged corpse in Murfree or Night Folk country and you've skipped a document — shoot the rope, drop the body, loot it. Players see that event a dozen times and never realise it's interactive. Sweep a chapter's random encounters before advancing rather than trusting them to come round again.

The panic is mostly unwarranted

Rockstar built in safety nets. Any 100%-required quest that was Arthur's auto-completes if you reach the Epilogue without it. Guarma's unique animals are auto-documented precisely because you can't go back. And entering Chapter 4 from a maxed-out Chapter 3 locks out nothing at all — players who worried about that transition reach the 90s in completion percentage without missing anything. Track the short genuinely-missable list, and stop treating every chapter boundary as a cliff.

The timing spine: Chapter 6's end is the pivot

One boundary matters more than the rest, because it's the only one where content becomes unavailable, other content becomes available, and your assets get wiped. Sort every task into three buckets around it.

Prepare for the asset wipe

Ending Chapter 6 keeps your valuables and trinkets and takes your cash, consumables and owned horses. So before the final missions: buy every weapon you still don't own, buy enough premium cigarettes to finish the card sets, and hold gold bars rather than selling them — bars behave like valuables. Anything you were saving cash for, spend it now.

If you'd rather not lose the money at all, let the Aberdeen Pig Farm couple rob you shortly before the end; the cash is recoverable from behind the painting as John. Buy your ammo and tonics for the remaining missions first, and don't go back for it immediately.

Standing priorities to set before you start

Get the Legendary Buck and its trinket before you hunt anything. The Buck Antler Trinket raises pelt quality enough that a three-star animal usually still yields a perfect pelt despite one mistake — the wrong weapon, or a hit slightly off the vital spot. On small animals the difference is close to binary: high success with it, routine spoilage without. Every task that wants perfect pelts gets cheaper, so this is the first thing on the list, not a nice-to-have.

Craft the Legend of the East satchel early. It isn't required for completion, and it still pays for itself: carry capacity is what stops you running dry on supplies mid-trip, and it directly unblocks the herb-heavy challenge steps. It needs the ledger unlocked for Pearson's leatherworking tools — until then, donate every perfect pelt you take to Pearson anyway so nothing is wasted. If you decide to skip it, push the carry-heavy Herbalist steps to the Epilogue where the satchel can simply be bought.

Spread the exotics out; never grind them. Perfect pelts aren't needed, so Dead Eye and a repeater into a flock is fine. The constraint is that corpses persist for a long time and reloading a save doesn't clear them, and the rarest birds only spawn in a handful of places — which they won't do with bodies lying there. Take what a location gives, leave, come back several in-game days later.

Activate hunting requests early and stay opportunistic. Once the request list is live, every cross-map ride can fill it incidentally. That converts the worst part of the task — finding perfect small-animal pelts on demand — into a by-product of travel you were doing anyway.

Accept that one task is pure luck. Winning blackjack hands on five cards is low-probability and immune to skill; budget an hour or two for it in a session where you don't mind grinding, rather than letting it block the rest of your plan.

The ordering principle

What separates a 60-hour completion run from a 100-hour one isn't the task list — everyone has the same list. It's two habits.

Batch by location, not by category. Don't run "all cigarette cards", then "all carvings", then the challenges. Take a region and clear every collectible, challenge step and errand it holds in one visit. The point is never to see the same signpost twice.

Do challenge steps inside story missions. Missions hand you scripted conditions — cornered enemies, forced quickdraws, prepared setups — that make challenge requirements trivial compared to hunting them in free roam. Challenge progress counts during missions, so knowing which step a mission can knock out is worth more than any farming route.

Both habits compound with overlap. Rabbits are the classic example: a horse-related step, a hunting step and an item request all want them, and satisfied together they cost five kills instead of nine — fewer still if a story mission supplies one.

Also spend five minutes trimming the checklist before you plan. Several in-game entries complete themselves as a side effect of others — the fish and horse compendium entries fall out of their challenge lines, the plant entries out of Herbalist, the table games out of Gambler, the treasure chain out of Explorer. And a whole tier of entries needs no planning whatsoever: story missions, chance encounters, the ambient gangs, equipment given during the story, horse bonding. Approach every white dot you pass and that tier handles itself.

Chapter by chapter

Chapter 1 prologue — mission loot and one anchor save

The snow section is on rails: free roam is suppressed by the weather (your horse will collapse and die out there), so nothing here is about exploration. The only content at risk is what's inside the missions — the two Cornwall letters in his private carriage above all.

Mission order barely matters for completion, but it matters for medals: unlocking Dead Eye before the deer hunt makes the clean-kill objective substantially easier if you're chasing gold.

Chapter 1 also gives you very few chances to save manually. Push through, then make a manual save at the start of Chapter 2 and never overwrite it — that's your permanent post-prologue anchor for every future run.

Chapter 2 the widest sandbox — park here

Chapter 2 is the longest usable window in the game: the map is open, the camp hasn't moved, and nothing has been taken away yet. Players who want a loaded save park here deliberately and clear rock carvings, dreamcatchers, cigarette cards, every reachable dinosaur bone, every legendary animal currently available, the treasure maps encountered so far, and the trinkets and talismans those legendary parts allow.

Two things get skipped most often. Challenges — most lines are open in Chapter 2 and are far easier chipped at now than grouped later. And the satchel chain, which is pure hunting and crafting with no story gate on the hunting half.

Treasure is the chapter's money engine: gold bars accumulate faster than you need to spend, so sell what funds your shopping and hold the rest. Also tour the map for free world gear — the weapons and hats sitting in the world care nothing about story progress — and inspect every point of interest, including the ones Arthur never sketches, since a journal-based checklist under-counts them.

A few things are hard-gated and waiting won't help: fishing (and therefore legendary fish) until the story hands you a rod, the horse fence and so the crime challenges, and the tail of Herbalist.

New Austin is reachable this early — an NPC escort from a mission suppresses the enforcement that otherwise kills you — but be honest about the payoff. It makes one long ride trivial and lets you pat a husky. Do it for the novelty of Arthur standing somewhere he shouldn't, not for value.

Chapters 3-4 nothing locks; defer what's gated

The Chapter 3 → 4 transition takes nothing away, so a maxed Chapter 3 save doesn't need a farewell tour. What it can't finish, it can't finish at any length of stay: legendary animals and fish, dinosaur bones, the wildlife-art buyer who's out of town, the Gunslinger line's finale, hermit treasure maps you hold but can't use, and most of the Trapper's catalogue.

What is completable in Chapter 3, and worth clearing since you're there: cigarette cards, rock carvings, the wildlife photographer, dreamcatchers, and the camp and satchel upgrades.

On challenges, this is where deferral pays. The sharpshooting steps that ask for precise multi-target work can be brute-forced before the Dead Eye tier that lets you paint targets, and people do it — but they're a different task entirely once that tier lands. Leave them.

Two points of interest here are worth doing as two trips rather than one: the corpse and letter on the Grey house's back porch will get you spotted mid-way, so grab one, ride out when the shooting starts, and come back for the other.

Chapter 5 — Guarma budget for the boas

Guarma has a bad reputation it doesn't deserve: the unique animals are auto-documented, so you cannot be locked out of completion by it. What you can miss is the full study-track-skin set on the island's animals if you care about doing it properly, and one item nobody's checklist mentions — the Green Turtle journal sketch on the shoreline.

The three boas are the entire time cost. Their spawns are rare enough that farming them can take as long as everything else on the island combined, so start them early rather than saving them for last. The turtle is easier than it looks: study it with binoculars (which tracks it at the same time), and if it isn't rendering, zoom fully out and back in to re-trigger it. The roof of the nearby shack is a safe perch with no hostile trigger, so you can camp it as long as you need.

Everything here is achievable in one honest pass with no glitching, given patience on the snakes. The one snake that isn't Guarma-exclusive won't reappear on the mainland until the Epilogue, so missing it costs you a wait, not the entry.

Chapter 6 the genuinely Arthur-only list

The short list that actually vanishes: the follow-up to the Beau and Penelope stranger line, the first bounty errand out of the chapter's new board, the Saint Denis market missionaries, and the Annesburg stranger gated behind decent honor — that last one is simply unavailable on a low-honor run. Hunt the legendary alligator before the chapter ends too.

One chain loses a limb rather than the whole: the photographer's third outing is Chapter 6 or never, and doing his line later silently skips it. And one rewards sequencing — finish all three parts of the Brandywine Drop stranger during Chapter 6 and returning later unlocks a scene you don't otherwise see.

Everything else genuinely waits for the Epilogue, including the veteran strand — better saved for after the wipe, since its reward horse wouldn't survive it anyway.

Chapter 6 is also the honor window, since the cap comes off and every honor event counts for half again as much. If you want a specific ending or an honor-gated unlock, this is where you buy it.

Epilogue what crosses over with John

Money does not transfer. Neither do gold bars or gold nuggets, despite what a lot of guides claim — so convert or spend them before the end. Food doesn't carry, and tonics only carry as a small fixed allotment rather than your stockpile, so there's no point hoarding either.

What does cross: all plumes and legendary animal parts, every trinket, every pocket watch, keepsakes (with the odd exception), lures and baits, all documents — journals, letters, handbills, photographs, cigarette card sets — all orchids, and the named one-off valuables like Mary's ring, the meteorite and the Braithwaite brooch. Large jewellery bags survive; small ones don't.

Read that list as instructions: sell the fungible wealth, keep the unique items, and don't spend the last hours of Chapter 6 stocking a pantry John will never see.

Camp content: the part people actually miss

Companion activities and item requests are where completion runs quietly break, and the reason is a widespread misreading of how they're lost. You don't lose them by waiting — you lose them by advancing yellow story missions. White camp content is the losable class, and only story progress closes it. Time-of-day gating makes an unavailable activity look permanently missed when it will reappear later the same chapter.

So the technique is the opposite of hurrying. Hold off on story missions, and spend a full in-game day and night in camp before heading out, because different members are active at different hours. Keep the roster home: a companion away on a story mission can't offer you anything. Sleep in your own bed periodically — activities cluster around the start of each camp and sleeping is what surfaces them (you also wake with a gold stamina core).

Requests come out of conversation, not proximity, which is why players who never sit down at the camp games miss them. Play poker and Pearson will mention his lost compass; play dominoes with Tilly and she'll mention the necklace in the first round — you can leave the game immediately after. Those two are commonly swapped in advice; the dominoes one is not a poker trigger. Some requests also have state conditions rather than clock conditions: Pearson only asks for a rabbit for stew in the late morning and only when your cores are low, because the stew is meant to be a kindness.

Two honest caveats. A handful of camp quests spawn unreliably enough that a given playthrough may never offer them — a missables list can't help with something that never fires. And at least one request item sits in your inventory for a quest that some players never see triggered at all. The rewards are small dialogue beats, so treat this as content to enjoy rather than a checklist to force.

If you're chasing achievements rather than the in-game counter, note that the four genuinely missable ones all live here: complete every side mission, do one companion activity in each of the three main camps, deliver five requested items, and put $250 in the tithing box. All five item deliveries are available inside Chapter 2 alone.

Two quality-of-life systems worth unlocking early

Real fast travel exists. Upgrade Dutch's tent twice and you get the travel map at your own tent — that much is well known. The same upgrade also adds a fast travel option to any camp you pitch yourself, which turns the one-way camp map into a system you can use from almost anywhere. Set up a bedroll, and the menu offers travel alongside sleep and cooking.

Save manually and often. Quitting from the main menu does not reliably commit your session, and there's no save prompt — treat autosave as a backup and the manual save under the Story menu as the real one. Save when you quit, and save before any mission you might want to retry.

Hats: an undocumented collection with a glow you have to notice

Hats are tracked in two separate wardrobe categories — ones stolen off people's heads and ones found in the world — and filling a slot in one does nothing for the other. The stolen kind work on a mechanic the game never explains: knock a hat off an NPC by any means, and if it registers it will flash on the ground. That glow is the confirmation. Pick it up and it saves to the wardrobe permanently; no glow means picking it up gets you nothing.

The farming techniques all come from that one mechanic. At a gang camp or fort, leave one enemy alive — the rest respawn when you return, so the same hat pool can be rerolled indefinitely. For a hat that only exists inside a mission, die and reload the checkpoint until it drops, and again leave one enemy standing so the mission doesn't complete before you can grab it. And for a group of NPCs lined up together, dynamite blows every hat off at once, though they'll land wherever the blast throws them.

Half a dozen hats are genuinely missable because they're tied to one-time missions or single chapters — including one you get by having an NPC punch your own hat off during a mission and picking it back up. If hats matter to you, that's the class of thing worth looking up before the chapter that holds it, because no amount of later farming will produce them.

One last piece of planning that isn't about efficiency: the Gunslinger targets can be hunted in any order, and picking that order deliberately turns a checklist into the game's own arc in miniature. Take the outlaw still free and glamorous first, then the one who dies fighting, then the one who dies fleeing, then the one who traded his legend for a pig farm, and finish with the fraud whose reputation was a lie. Same rewards, much better story.

Recap: content is lost three ways — a chapter's closing mission, loot left behind inside a mission, and one-shot random encounters — and Rockstar's safety nets cover the rest, so plan around the short real list. Sort everything around the end of Chapter 6, which both takes content away and wipes your cash, consumables and horses. Get the Buck Antler Trinket before hunting anything, craft the big satchel early, spread the exotics out, and activate hunting requests as soon as they exist. Batch tasks by location and complete challenge steps inside story missions rather than free roam. Park in Chapter 2 to clear the widest window, don't fear the Chapter 3-4 transition, start Guarma's boas early, and use Chapter 6 for both the Arthur-only list and your honor push. Camp content is lost to story progress, not to waiting — so dwell in camp, sleep, and play the games.

Honor

Honor is the most misunderstood system in the game, because the two things that would explain it — your actual score and the chapter that gates it — are both hidden. Once you know the shape of it, the meter stops feeling random and becomes something you can steer deliberately in either direction.

The meter, and why it seems to ignore you

The bar runs eight notches either side of neutral, and you can only see it on demand — hold the honor-display input (left Alt on PC, the D-pad on controller). There is no numeric readout anywhere, which is why nobody can tell you exactly where a threshold sits; you learn your position from where the bar sat last time you checked.

Before Chapter 6, honor is capped at 8 of 10 in both directions. That single fact explains the complaint every player eventually posts: half an hour of greeting townsfolk and five donations to the tithing box produces no visible movement, then looting one corpse costs a whole level. Positive actions were pushing into a ceiling; the negative one had room to travel.

Chapter 6 removes the cap and applies a 1.5× multiplier to every honor event, in both directions. So the ending you get is decided by a period where honor moves half again as fast as it did all game. If you want an extreme — either extreme — Chapter 6 is where you go and get it, and any achievement asking for a maxed bar is only reachable there.

The practical read: don't fight the cap in Chapters 2-5. Play the character you want, keep honor roughly where you'd like it, and treat Chapter 6 as the correction window.

What actually moves the needle

Honor income comes in three very different sizes, and players who stall are almost always grinding the smallest one.

Scripted honor

Stranger missions and certain story encounters carry the largest single swings in the game — enough that one post-story encounter can take a player sitting stubbornly at ~85% straight to maximum. Mission choices where the game offers you mercy or murder are in this tier too.

If a threshold matters to you, mission and stranger content is the lever. Grinding cannot compete with it.

Repeatable actions

Greetings, catch-and-release fishing, camp chores done by hand rather than skipped, disarming instead of killing in a duel, stopping for white-blip encounters. Each is small, all are unlimited, and together they'll carry you across a level in a session.

The donation bump

Money into the tithing box pays a fixed honor bump that resets daily, and it is capped — around $20 clears it, and more is simply lost. A $500 lump behaves like a $20 one. Donating a single cheap item triggers the same bump, so value isn't what's being measured; the act is.

Rough exchange rate from player counting: on the order of a dozen capped donations to advance one level, i.e. a couple of hundred dollars and about as many in-game days. Fine as a passive top-up, hopeless as your main plan.

Losses are weighted differently from gains, and the asymmetry sharpens as you climb: near the top of the bar a good deed nudges and a casual bad act — one looted body, one spooked townsperson calling the law — drops a visible chunk. High honor is therefore a habit, not an achievement you bank. Keep a mental list of the cheap ways you leak honor (indiscriminate corpse looting is the big one) and stop doing them for the stretch that matters.

Farming honor efficiently

Every viable farm is a density problem: honor pays per action, so the winner is whatever puts the most qualifying actions per minute in front of you.

Greeting crowds is the fastest method available. Saint Denis is the only town with the pedestrian density to make it sing — the marketplace near the Trapper especially. Walk or slow-trot laps so fresh NPCs keep entering range, lock on and greet everything: men, women, beggars, even stray dogs and cats count. Every third unique person greeted is a tick, and a busy street will feed you dozens of greetings in the time one fish takes to land.

Catch and release pays a tick per fish returned to the water, and fish size is irrelevant — a twenty-pound catfish you fought for three minutes pays exactly what a bluegill pays. So the method only competes when you've found water where bites come in seconds; then deliberately target trash fish with bread bait and throw them straight back. Never spend the fight time on a big fish, and stay away from legendaries. As a bonus the release itself feeds health XP, so it isn't dead time.

Chores and duels are the passive layer. Do camp chores manually each day rather than skipping the animation. In any forced duel, shoot the hand — disarming pays roughly what a capped donation pays and costs you nothing you wanted. Divert for every white blip you pass; the time cost is seconds against one of the better payouts in the game.

The efficient run isn't one method. It's greetings when you're in a city anyway, a daily donation, chores whenever you're in camp, and never walking past a stranger.

Committing costs nothing permanently

Honor isn't locked in by how you played earlier. A bar sitting fully red — map tint and all — can be walked back to a high-honor ending by greetings alone, and Chapter 6's multiplier makes that recovery much faster than the climb that got you there. If you want to run most of the game as a bastard and still get the sunrise, that plan works.

Sequencing honor against the rest of the run

Crime-based challenge work tanks honor by design. If you want both a maxed honor bar and those challenges, take honor to its extreme first, collect what it unlocks, and do the criminal grind afterwards — the challenges don't care what your honor is when you finish them, but the honor unlocks won't wait.

Camp & Gang

Dutch will tell you the gang needs money until the credits roll no matter what you do, and that single unchanging line has convinced a lot of players the camp economy is either deeply important or completely fake. It's neither. It's a small, cheap system with two real outputs and a couple of genuinely worthwhile purchases hidden inside it.

What donating actually buys

Money into the tithing box produces exactly two things: an honor bump, and camp funds that pay for ledger upgrades. That's the whole return. There is no story consequence at any sum — players have modded in a quarter of a million dollars and Dutch still needs one more score. No hidden dialogue, no Tahiti cutscene, no branch.

More importantly, you never have to donate to buy an upgrade. If camp funds are empty, ledger purchases just come out of Arthur's own wallet. Donating first is bookkeeping, not a prerequisite. The one thing personal cash can't substitute for is the fast travel unlock, which is a camp upgrade or nothing.

Item donations are the efficient route when you do want funds: looted junk converts above face value, so a near-worthless oddity can come out as several dollars of camp money. Donating tusks, watches, rings and buckles funds the ledger without cash and saves the ride to a fence. Anything the game categorises as provisions gets cooked into the stew instead.

The other side of that coin: the box is also a place money can come back from. Camp funds pay to restock the supply wagons, and those supplies are lootable. Donate for the honor tick, restock with camp money, strip the medical wagon and sell what you don't want, wait for funds to rebuild, repeat. That loop runs net positive while still paying you the honor.

Stop the nagging for a penny a day

Grimshaw's and Dutch's complaints about contributions are recency-triggered, not amount-triggered. The check is "how long since Arthur last put something in the box", and nothing else. Donate two thousand dollars and then go away for a week and you'll still be scolded for not contributing.

Which means the fix is trivially cheap: drop a token amount in every time you're in camp. A few cents on a regular cadence silences it as effectively as a fortune. It's the same design as the "get out of camp" prompts, which fire on how long you've lingered rather than anything you did.

Worth knowing what the rest of the gang contributes, too — the ledger itemises it, and the answer is a couple of dollars each. Any meaningful camp fund is Arthur's alone, so nobody has standing to complain, and matching their trivial amounts is a perfectly defensible policy.

Which upgrades are worth the money

The ledger runs to roughly a thousand dollars to buy out entirely, which is easily affordable once treasure money starts arriving, and can be exhausted by early Chapter 3. But the upgrades are not equal, and two of them are the reason to engage with the system at all.

Leatherworking tools

Buy first. This unlocks Pearson's satchel line and therefore the Legend of the East satchel, and carry capacity is the single biggest quality-of-life change available to you — you stop rationing what you pick up, and looting stops having a ceiling. Everything else in the ledger is convenience; this one changes how you play.

Lodging / fast travel

The most-argued purchase in the game. It only travels you away from camp, so you still ride home — and skipping rides means skipping the random encounters that are a large part of what's good here.

The case for it is spawn-farming: hunting a specific three-star animal means repeated trips to the same spot waiting for a respawn, and there aren't enough roadside events to make that riding interesting. Buy it as an option you use sparingly, not a default. Upgrading Dutch's tent twice is what unlocks it, and it also enables travel from camps you pitch yourself.

The rest

The chicken coop improves the stew, which matters more than it sounds once you're chasing gold cores. The horse station lets you swap horses without riding to a stable. The boat is genuinely handy from Chapter 3 onward, though it's the most expensive single line item. The map upgrade is cheap and useful.

Pearson's crafted decorations aren't cosmetic

Don't conflate the two upgrade sources. Ledger upgrades unlock mechanics — stew quality, fast travel, horse swapping. Pearson's crafted decor unlocks morale, and morale is what the rest hangs off.

A higher-morale camp donates more money and goods of its own, so camp funds tick up noticeably more often once the decor is finished — the spend partially recoups itself. It also visibly changes camp life: more conversations, more campfire songs, more people doing things. If you like the camp as a place to be, this is the purchase that feeds it.

What it won't do is unlock a mechanic. Expect indirect payoff and atmosphere, not a new button.

Meat, trophies and the icon that never turns white

The camp's food indicator confuses everyone who tries to fix it by hunting, because meat donations don't move it. That icon tracks the stock level of Pearson's food wagon — red for depleted, then silver, then gold when fully stocked — and the only thing that raises it is spending camp funds through the ledger. Same for the other two supply icons, which track Arthur's ammunition wagon and Strauss's medical wagon. None of them will budge before story progress unlocks the ledger, so early frustration there is wasted effort.

What donated goods actually do splits cleanly. Uncooked meat and carcasses feed the stew: quantity and quality set how good that day's stew is, which sets the stat boost you get from eating it and the camp's mood for the day, plus a small honor bump for contributing enough. Over-donating inside the same window is wasted — more meat of equal or worse quality adds nothing. Trophies feed the funds: pelts, claws, horns and feathers each carry a cash value and behave exactly like putting money in the box.

Upgrading the food wagon also lets Pearson stock fruit, bread and vegetables rather than meat alone, which is what actually satisfies the camp's needs.

Pick a donation policy and stop thinking about it

Since the payoff is bounded, the sensible policies are all defensible: donate until the ledger is bought out and then stop; donate only looted items; cap yourself at a token amount on the grounds that everyone else gives pennies; or never donate and pay ledger costs from your own pocket, which works fine. The only bad policy is dumping large sums expecting something to happen.

If you're donating for honor rather than funds, remember the bump is capped and daily — small and frequent beats large and occasional, in both systems at once.

Two sequencing notes. The legendary animals — including the buck whose trinket you want before any serious hunting — are gated behind Hosea's hunting mission, which appears almost immediately in Chapter 2. So the natural opening is a mission or two, then Hosea's hunt, then the buck, then pelts toward the satchel. And an early windfall of a few hundred dollars is available in Chapter 2 to anyone who explores properly, which takes the pressure off camp upgrade costs entirely.

Finally, do the gang's item requests early. Members stop caring about them as the story darkens, and the requests are the cheapest content in the game — a conversation, a short errand, a small reward and a dialogue beat you otherwise never hear.

Recap: donations pay only honor and camp funds, and you can buy ledger upgrades straight from your own wallet anyway — fast travel being the one exception. Nagging is about how recently you donated, not how much, so a few cents per visit ends it. Buy leatherworking tools first for the satchel line, treat fast travel as a spawn-farming convenience rather than a default, and know that the chicken coop and horse station carry real utility while Pearson's decor buys morale, ambient camp life and faster gang contributions. Meat feeds the stew, trophies feed the funds, and the supply icons only move via the ledger. Item donations convert above face value, camp supplies can be looted back, and gang requests should be done early.

Money

The awkward truth about RDR2's economy is that a normal playthrough hands you more money than the game gives you to spend — plenty of players finish having bought every horse and outfit they wanted and still hold thousands. So the useful question isn't "how do I get rich", it's "which income streams are worth minutes of my life", and the answers are rarely the outlaw ones.

Where the money physically is

Loot is tiered steeply, and the top tier is worth an order of magnitude more than the bottom. A gold bar sells for around $500 and an ingot for $300, while a nugget is $25 and gold jewellery — teeth included — is under $10. Cash pickups are worse than they feel: a money clip is around $20, a sack is a few dollars, and loose coins are literally small change. Jewellery bags are the good mid-tier find at roughly $75 for a large one and $25 for a small, and they're sealed containers — you can't part out the contents.

The practical consequence is that fenced valuables beat found cash, so when a body or a drawer offers both, the trinket is the better pickup. Prioritise accordingly when your hands are full.

One inventory trap worth knowing: pocket watches sit under satchel Kits, not Valuables. Bulk-selling from the Valuables tab silently leaves your watches behind, and rummaging through Kits carelessly is how people sell cigarette cards by accident. Check both tabs.

Treasure: the top of the economy

Gold bars are where large sums come from, and nearly all of them come from treasure hunts. Every hunt is a chain — you must find and view each map before the next location becomes findable — so they can't be shortcut by knowing the destination. Payouts per hunt run from a single ingot up to six bars, and the New Austin hunts break the pattern by paying in something other than gold.

Selling those early is what funds your camp upgrades and first good weapons without any grinding at all.

The statue puzzle is a picture, not a maths riddle

Press the statues with 2, 3, 5 and 7 fingers. The universal explanation — "press the primes" — gets the right answer for the wrong reason, and that matters because the coincidence hides how the clue works. The painting at Window Rock shows eagles, some upright and some inverted; the upright ones carry 2, 3, 5 and 7 feathers. The key maps feather count to statue, and orientation tells you press-or-leave. Read the painting as a key and the puzzle is simple; read it as arithmetic and you'll never trust the answer.

The armless statue is the seven; its missing arm is in a rock pile in the corner of the cave. Start there and work round, skipping alternate statues, and you'll end on four presses.

Respawning loot: the actual money printer

Several containers in the world restock on a trivial condition — leave the area and come back. That's not a glitch, it's how their respawn timers work, and it makes them better per minute than any robbery.

The pattern is always the same. Loot the container, ride away far enough to unload the area (a short loop to a nearby landmark is usually enough), ride back, loot it again. A mountain shanty box in the north that pays platinum jewellery and a gold nugget pairs perfectly with a nearby landmark to loop around; a roadside stash west of Downes ranch holds a large jewellery bag, premium cigarettes and a tonic; an abandoned wagon chest in Big Valley restocks the same way; the cemetery behind Shady Belle reloads too. Use whichever is nearest to where your Arthur already is — the travel is the only cost.

What limits this isn't the loot, it's carry capacity, which is the real argument for rushing the Legend of the East satchel. With it you can hold 99 of most valuable types (watches cap far lower), so the routine becomes: fill every slot, sell down at the nearest fence, refill. Players who commit to this arrive in early Chapter 3 with tens of thousands of dollars in cash and satchel value, which is far more than the game asks of you.

If you're farming the mountains, do it by day. Wolves own those slopes at night and will end a loop that was going well.

Crime that's actually worth doing

Set expectations first: you cannot rob a bank alone in free roam. Stores can be held up — clerks respond to the prompt, and the back-room window behind the Valentine doctor is a well-known way in — but bank tellers have no robbery interaction outside missions, and no amount of dynamite makes the vault interactive. Pointing a gun at a teller only buys you a bounty. A special-edition mission robs a bank with Charles and Uncle, and PC mods add free-roam versions; on a stock game, that's the whole story.

More broadly, robbery is a roleplay choice rather than an economic one. Ten minutes of picking herbs for tonics or taking three-star pelts pays comparably to a hold-up, with no bounty, no ammunition cost and no honor loss. The game is quietly generous to the flower-picking outlaw. That said, three crimes are genuinely good, and all three work by removing the law from the equation rather than outshooting it.

Park a train where the law can't climb

Hijack a train and stop it halfway across a long river bridge. Lawmen spawn below and can only shoot upward at a man inside a rail carriage, which gives you effectively unlimited looting time. Stop near where the track meets land and the trick collapses — they simply walk on.

The prep matters more than the shooting. Stash your horse near the station with a stay command before boarding and take the guns you want off the saddle first, because saddle inventory is unreachable once you're aboard. Drive without stopping while guards shoot at you — they mostly miss and won't storm the driver — and fight only once you've parked.

For the safes, stick dynamite to the door and shoot it from outside the carriage rather than lighting it by hand; explosive rounds are better still, being faster with a much smaller blast. When you're done, creep the train forward until lawmen cross to your side, then reverse at speed back to the station, let it come to a full stop, and ride out.

Rob a train with no bounty at all

The key insight is that masks don't work on lawmen. They identify you through any disguise, so concealment can never prevent a bounty on a train job. The only defence is distance — stop the train so deep inside a tunnel that no lawman can reach or witness it. The game has exactly one tunnel long enough, on the line running west out of Annesburg, and the spot is its far end just before the turn.

Buy a ticket rather than stowing away (being caught riding free is its own bounty), mask up before you're seen, and whistle your horse to follow — otherwise you finish with no mount at the far end. Killing the conductor doesn't stop the train; it coasts until you brake, and overshooting costs nothing since you can just drive it back.

The first two carriages are manned and hold the safes, so that's where the fight is. Bring a lantern and a sidearm, since the lantern occupies your other hand. Rob the passengers, then deal with them and loot the bodies — people hold items back when robbed alive. Clear the cabinets for ammunition and tonics as well.

If your horse got lost, drive the train clear and steal a road wagon instead: wagons sprint indefinitely where stolen horses tire, the nearest ranch has a fence, the wagon itself sells there, and your horse gets recalled to you. A clean full-loot run pays a few hundred dollars, and the station nearby makes it a repeatable loop.

Stop a coach without firing

At least one stagecoach can be robbed with no kills, no witnesses and no wanted level, and the principle generalises: let the environment stop the coach for you instead of opening with gunfire.

On the road under the train tracks near the timber camp, a parked logging wagon can be released to roll down and block the road. Wait in the brush, let the coach halt at the blockage, then approach from directly behind — escorts don't spot you there — break the lock on the rear compartment, loot it and walk away. All it needs is a lock breaker.

Whether every coach has such a solution is unproven, but the habit of looking for a blockage, a chokepoint or a rear approach before drawing a weapon is what makes coach work profitable rather than expensive.

Homestead robberies are worth knowing as a route rather than individually: there are only seven in the game, and they cluster into two bands — a pair out west near Strawberry and the Dakota, and a run down the eastern corridor from Van Horn through Roanoke into Lemoyne, plus one outlier north of Annesburg. A single loop based around Annesburg picks up three of them, with a southern ride adding two more. Plan them as one trip, not seven.

Bounty laundering: let the wrong state arrest you

Your bounty is billed by the jurisdiction that arrests you, not the one you robbed. Ambarino has no jail, so an arrest there hauls you to Valentine but charges you Ambarino's trivial bounty — and clears the expensive one you built elsewhere without deducting it.

So: earn a large, profitable bounty in New Hanover (train work near Annesburg is the best ratio of payout to risk), ride into Ambarino, commit something petty — carrying a lassoed stranger around until a bystander runs off to report you does it — then surrender when the law arrives, taking care not to aim a weapon at them. A few days in jail later, visit a fence and sell everything. The income stays; the bill never arrives.

Two refinements: sell looted tonics to a doctor rather than a fence, and let a couple of bounty-hunter waves reach you before you leave, since they're worth looting. Shooting their horses lets you take the saddlebags too.

Low-risk income you'll collect anyway

The unglamorous streams are the ones that pay while you're doing something else, which is what makes them efficient.

Wagon and horse fencing. Park near a wagon fence, steal whatever passes, sell it. It's a few tens of dollars a time, needs no skill, and repeats forever. Horses fence the same way.

Bodies in mission shootouts. Story missions throw a lot of corpses and containers at you and then yank you forward with the next trigger. Loot before you advance — that's free money you're otherwise leaving on the floor, and the missions themselves pay well enough that they're the simplest route to solvency if you don't want to farm.

Pelts, fish and plants. Sell perfect pelts to a butcher or trapper if you're not feeding Pearson's crafting; donate the good-and-poor ones to camp funds. Herbs picked in passing become tonics you can sell or use. None of it is fast, all of it is free.

Collectible mail-ins. Keep every cigarette card you find and post completed sets to the collector; the rock-carving enthusiast and the dinosaur-bone scientist pay for coordinates the same way. You're finding these things anyway.

Rich towns and abandoned houses. The wealthy quarter on the edge of Saint Denis and the well-dressed citizens of Rhodes carry real money and jewellery, with a fence conveniently close. Search interiors properly with Eagle Eye — floorboards and drawers hide money clips, and the cemetery box behind Shady Belle is invisible without it.

Hold or sell?

Don't reflexively dump valuables at the first fence. Some are wanted later, and the early game's real bottleneck isn't cash at all — it's the perfect pelts the satchel line demands, a list heavy on deer and elk with one each of a dozen other species. Capacity is a permanent multiplier; money is replaceable.

The one hard deadline is the end of Chapter 6, which takes your cash — and, contrary to a lot of advice out there, your gold bars and nuggets too. Unique and named valuables survive; fungible wealth doesn't. Convert gold to cash and spend the cash before the story ends.

Exploits, and their expiry dates

Every money glitch is version-dependent, and the well-known ones have mostly been patched — the gold-bar duplication at the burnt town among them, though unpatched offline copies can still repeat it. Treat any exploit you read about as "worked on some build".

The durable one is a save-reload loop in the Van Horn mansion: clear the bandits, take the cash off the table, save, reload, and you respawn outside ready to repeat. It still functions years later. It's also slow — the loading screen, not the loop, sets your hourly rate, so it's only worthwhile if you were going to be away from the keyboard anyway. As a side benefit the bandits can spawn wearing a collectible hat, so the loop doubles as a reroll for it.

The money cheat code works and pays repeatedly, but enabling any cheat disables saving — so it can't fund a playthrough, only a sandbox. If your goal is money in a real save, gold bars are the honest exploit: they need no glitch, they're available the moment free roam opens, and fifteen to twenty of them across the map is several thousand dollars.

A word against farming

Money in this game buys weapons, clothes, horses and camp upgrades, and then stops buying anything. Players who optimise the economy early tend to report the same thing: with nothing left to want, the only remaining desire is a fresh save. Get a few hundred dollars, buy what's actually blocking you, and reassess — and keep a reserve for large bounties, ammunition you can't get free from camp, and the occasional expensive outfit. That's the whole budget the game needs.

Recap: loot value is steeply tiered, so fence valuables rather than hoarding cash, and remember watches hide under Kits. Treasure hunts are the top of the economy — chains must be found in order, six bars are available immediately in Chapter 2, and the statue puzzle is an upright-eagle key rather than a prime-number riddle. Respawning containers plus a big satchel out-earn every crime, since capacity is the true limiter. Banks can't be robbed solo, and robbery generally pays no better than herbs or pelts for the same time — but bridge-parked trains, the one long tunnel, and environmentally-stopped coaches all work by removing the law rather than fighting it. Bounties are billed by the arresting jurisdiction, which makes an Ambarino surrender a cheap wipe. Gold and cash die at the end of Chapter 6, so spend before then, and don't grind past the point where money still buys you something.

Gunplay

RDR2's combat looks like it's about weapon choice and is actually about three things: how you use Dead Eye, how clean your guns are, and how much you've fired the one in your hand. Get those right and the stat bars in the shop stop mattering — which is convenient, because they're the least reliable thing in the game.

Dead Eye has two upgrade tracks, and only one is grindable

This is the single most common source of confusion, and it explains why the tutorial seems to describe a mechanic you don't have. Dead Eye upgrades along two completely independent axes.

Tiers — what it can do

Unlocked only by specific story missions. No amount of XP touches them. There are five, and they land roughly one per chapter: automatic marking at the start; manual marking early in Chapter 2; staying in Dead Eye after unmarked shots in Chapter 4; heart and head areas highlighted in Chapter 5; lungs and stomach added in Chapter 6.

The Chapter 2 unlock is the one that matters — it's the point where Dead Eye becomes a precision tool instead of a shotgun blast of X marks. If you're planning a long early-game stay, know that the organ highlighting simply cannot be reached there.

Levels — how long it lasts

Pure meter duration, earned with XP, available at any point in the story. Levels 1-8 come from ordinary play. Nine and ten come from gear: buy one of the three relevant reinforced equipment sets at a Trapper for level 9, and all three for level 10.

XP rates are worth knowing because they're lopsided. Killing something without Dead Eye, cooking or crafting pays a single point. Camp chores and collectibles pay five. Skinning pays one to ten depending on the animal. Completing one of the relevant challenge lines pays thirty. Valerian root gives a chunk when consumed.

So cooking is the easiest grind and hunting is the productive one — you'd be doing it anyway, it pays more per action, and it feeds three other systems at once.

Trinkets and talismans layer on top, either increasing Dead Eye XP earned or slowing the meter's drain while active. And note the ceiling on every stat: eight is the wall from experience alone, and getting to ten requires challenge completion plus purchased gear. That's why maxed cores are structurally an end-of-run thing.

Using Dead Eye properly: toggle, don't linger

The marks aren't a tag list — they're a record of wherever your reticle has been. Leave the crosshair resting on one man's head and you'll stack four marks on that head, which is exactly the complaint everyone has on their first firefight. Sweep the reticle across your targets, one brief pass each, then fire.

But the technique that actually transforms Dead Eye is treating it as a sub-second correction rather than a mode you sit in. The meter drains continuously while active, including all the time you spend swinging the crosshair around looking for someone to shoot. So: let auto-aim lock the nearest enemy's chest, tap into Dead Eye, nudge up to the head, fire, tap straight back out, and re-lock the next target. Auto-aim does the target acquisition; Dead Eye does nothing but buy you the half-second to move from chest to skull.

Done that way a single charge covers five to ten men instead of two. Manual tagging, once you have it, is slower than this loop for ordinary fights — save it for legendary animals, where repeatedly marking the same spot lets you stack several shots into one place.

Things Dead Eye doesn't do

It doesn't make you invulnerable — that was the previous game. You take full damage in slow motion, so it's not an escape from a bad position.

A mark isn't a guaranteed hit either. A moving target or an object in the line of fire can eat the bullet, and the classic version of this is shooting from cover: the same cover protecting you also blocks some of your own shots.

And placing more than one mark switches Arthur from a shouldered stance to hip fire, which is much faster and noticeably less precise. That's a deliberate tradeoff, not a bug.

Two uses that aren't about aiming at all. Dead Eye brightens the scene, so it's a light source in caves and dark houses where you genuinely can't see the enemy ahead. And entering it reloads your weapon instantly, which makes it a free reload cancel in the middle of a fight. There's also a passive version — entered without a weapon drawn — that slows time without spending the meter, which is what you want while tracking an animal or chasing someone down.

Refill the meter by shooting without it, or with tonics. Since ordinary gunplay both refills it and feeds its XP, the natural habit is to fight normally and spend Dead Eye on the shots that matter.

Duels are a different system — unlearn Dead Eye

Quickdraw looks like Dead Eye and behaves nothing like it, which is why so many players never crack it and just empty the cylinder in every duel.

Do not fill the meter. The game's own prompt tells you to let it charge, and following that instruction gets you killed. Charge for about half a second, then fire the instant you see the opponent's arm move. The charge isn't power — it's how much slowed aiming time you get after the trigger goes down, and it's a straight tradeoff: draw too slowly and he shoots first, draw too fast and the slow-motion runs out before you're on target.

Don't tag. There is no marking in a duel; that reflex is a holdover from the previous game. Aim head or heart and fire once. If you can shoot straight, a single tap of charge plus a headshot ends it.

Mechanically it's a two-stage trigger: a light press gets Arthur's hand to the gun and starts the draw, then you release and press again to fire. With auto-aim on he goes to centre mass, so free-aim if you want the head — or a specific target, since a few missions want you to hit a man's shooting hand rather than kill him.

Free-roam duels have conditions: on foot, sidearm holstered, not already in combat. Once shooting has started you can't holster and re-draw into one. Forced duels inside missions behave differently from free-roam ones — some allow multiple shots, and the switch to regular Dead Eye that works in free roam doesn't work there. Expect to lose your first few, and expect the gunslinger questline's final opponent to beat you repeatedly even after it clicks.

One reason to learn it properly: you can shoot the weapon out of an opponent's hand rather than killing him, which is worth real honor and works outside duels too.

What actually determines how hard you hit

Rank the levers honestly and the shopping decisions get easy. Weapon class matters enormously; the specific gun within a class barely does. Handguns kill in one or two shots across the board — Cattleman, Schofield, a unique revolver, it makes no felt difference. Switch to a repeater or a shotgun and everything changes. Below class, the order runs ammunition type, then cleanliness, then the per-gun stat bars a long way back.

The reason the bars lie is familiarity, the sixth and least-known stat. Use a weapon and it gets better in your hands, and a maxed-familiarity gun outperforms any same-category weapon you've barely fired. Comparing base stats between two revolvers is therefore mostly academic — the one you've carried all game wins. You can check familiarity per weapon in the compendium.

The per-gun stat that is felt is how fast a weapon zeroes in: watch the reticle contract to the white dot. Fast-zeroing weapons are the reason the Schofield and Lancaster have the reputations they do. And if you play for headshots, damage is close to irrelevant — a bullet to the head settles the argument whatever fired it, so accuracy and speed are what you're actually buying.

Degradation: three causes, wildly different speeds

Soot is the only one you cause yourself, accumulating as you fire. Resistance varies by class — sidearms shrug it off, while high-firepower shotguns and rifles foul quickly and need regular attention.

Rust and dirt are environmental and much more brutal: water, snow and mud take a weapon to its worst condition in a startlingly short time. A single misjudged river crossing does more harm than an afternoon of shooting.

Which makes prevention mostly about routing and storage. Keep long arms in the horse's bag and only draw them when needed; a sidearm handles most trouble. If you must wade, draw dual sidearms first — Arthur carries them high enough that the water can't reach them.

Several items reduce the degradation rate and they stack: the reinforced and upgraded holsters, a per-weapon wrap for repeaters, rifles and shotguns, a talisman from a stranger mission, a trinket crafted from a legendary pelt, and the Legend of the East outfit. That last one requires every challenge in the game, by which point you're unlikely to still care — chase the cheap ones and ignore it.

Be honest about when this matters: early, when cleaning costs money you need elsewhere. Later you'll have more cash than uses for it and maintenance becomes a formality. Read condition from the bottom meter on the weapon wheel, or skip the menu entirely — a fouled gun looks dirty and its report changes audibly. Clean it yourself with gun oil, which enemies carry, so looting bodies keeps you stocked for free.

Building a loadout

Cover engagement bands rather than collecting favourites: a rifle for distance, a repeater for the middle, a sidearm you know intimately, and a small-game gun so hunting doesn't cost you pelts. Anything past that is redundancy you're carrying for nothing. Two class-wide facts shape the picks: repeaters and rifles are properly balanced across the story, so each unlock is a genuine upgrade — while shotguns are front-loaded, handing you most of the good ones early, so there's nothing to wait for.

Sidearms familiarity beats everything

Since you fire your sidearm constantly, you build familiarity with it whether you plan to or not, and that quietly locks in your choice — later unlocks rarely displace a revolver you've carried for thirty hours. Pick early, on handling rather than numbers, and stick with it.

The Schofield is the popular pick for good reason: fast zeroing and a good fire rate, and with split-point rounds it's the standard choice for stand-offs. The LeMat is the versatility option, carrying nine rounds plus an underbarrel shotgun blast — which can genuinely replace carrying a shotgun at all. Fast-firing options like the double-action are fine for hip-fire spam and weak at range; the Mauser fires quickly with little authority; the Volcanic hits hard and reloads slowly enough to be a liability outside duels, where its accuracy is exactly what you want.

Dual-wielding two sidearms is stronger than it looks — see the reload trick below.

Repeaters and rifles the mid and long bands

The Lancaster is the consensus best repeater on reload speed and rate of fire, and it's what most people mean by "the mid-range gun". The Litchfield trades handling for damage and magazine size if you fight aggressively. The early carbine is perfectly serviceable and builds familiarity fast, which is a real argument for keeping it longer than the upgrade path suggests.

For rifles, understand that top-end damage converges: with full customisation and good ammunition, the main hunting rifles all land in the same place. Unmodified there's a whisker between them. So the choice isn't damage, it's role — a bolt action's five rounds buy you fast follow-up shots, a rolling block is for the deliberate very-long-range shot, and a Springfield with express ammunition is the general-purpose answer that's available absurdly early. The scoped rifles are the setup-time option: superb when you have a vantage point and time, useless when something is charging you.

The varmint rifle deserves a mention as a combat weapon, not just a hunting tool: it's accurate at distance and one-shots people in the head, which makes it quietly excellent against crowds.

Shotguns pump for reliability, slugs for everything else

The pump action is the all-rounder and the community default: five shots, a tight enough spread to work at medium range, faster than the repeating version and more accurate than the semi-auto. The per-shot pump barely costs you anything because close range is a one-shot proposition anyway.

The semi-auto's case is fire rate and magazine — hip-firing into a crowded room is where it shines, and that margin is sometimes the difference between winning and reloading. It loses to the pump the moment distance opens up.

The break-action guns hit hardest per shell, and a double barrel with the long-barrel upgrade will one-shot at any range a shotgun is sensible at. Two shells is the catch. The classic compromise is a pump as your long arm with a sawed-off in the off-hand holster for point-blank work — the sawed-off is a backup, not a primary, and dual-wielding two of them is fun in a cramped house and worse than dual pistols anywhere else.

The single most useful shotgun upgrade isn't a gun, it's slug ammunition, which fixes the reliability problem the whole class has — and it's what makes a shotgun viable from horseback. For animal defence, incendiary buckshot in a pump will stop a charging bear in one hit; the pelt is ruined and you're alive, which is the correct trade when you're walking in bear country.

The bow and arrow types quiet, and best-in-class on legendaries

Arrow type is identifiable at a glance while the bow is drawn, which matters when you're switching under pressure: a bare knapped point is a regular arrow, a blunt brass tip is small game, a green-stained head is poison, and a lit bulb behind the head is dynamite.

Dynamite arrows are the answer to legendary animals — one shot, done — and they sidestep the real cost of rifle hunting, which is that a miss sends you back to re-tracking the animal. Since kill quality doesn't affect a legendary pelt, there's no reason to be delicate.

Small game arrows are for the smallest animals only — snakes and little birds — and this is where the weapon-versus-size rule bites. A varmint rifle destroys those carcasses whether or not you use Dead Eye; conversely, rabbits and squirrels need the varmint rifle and won't yield a perfect pelt to anything else. Dead Eye above the manual-marking tier makes both cases dramatically easier by showing you where the clean kill is.

Recipes for the specialty arrows come from pamphlets scattered in the world — near the reservation for dynamite, a couple of locations north and east of Emerald Ranch and Saint Denis for poison, with several pamphlets often sharing a spot so check them all. If hunting them down sounds tedious, a fence will sell you the recipes instead.

Small techniques that change fights

Dive to dodge. The dive is a far better evasion than it looks — enemies effectively can't hit you during it. It's the right answer to being caught in the open, not just a stylish way to reach cover.

Swap guns instead of reloading. Reloads run on the holstered weapon, so dual-wielding two sidearms and switching when one empties gives continuous fire — by the time the second cylinder is dry, the first is loaded. This is the strongest argument for dual-wielding, and it's especially good with a high-capacity revolver.

Walk into a revolver execution. Closing on an enemy with a revolver triggers a point-blank instant kill, which skips the whole aim-and-trade exchange. Aggression is cheap here.

Stealth is a real option even when nothing asks for it. Missions rarely require the loud approach, and an entire guard roster can be cleared before anyone raises an alarm. Go at night — darkness is what makes it work — and carry the full silent kit: hunting knife for melee, throwing knives and tomahawks for short range, bow for distance. The detection AI is fair rather than psychic, so a failure is almost always a specific execution error; fix it and retry rather than abandoning the plan. Expect a couple of attempts.

Fist fighting is burst-then-defend. Mashing punch is just agreeing to trade damage. Land two or three, then block or dodge a few of theirs, then repeat — and read the opponent instead of committing to the full burst every time, because overextending is what earns you the counter. As they tire you can extend the burst. Blocking is the core, and dodging is the same button plus movement away, which is your only answer to a knife since those strikes can't be blocked. In a grapple, holding one stick and flicking the other throws them to the ground. And if someone has you in a choke while a third man punches you, that incoming punch is your escape — it kicks the choker off. Clean wins with no damage taken are entirely possible; if a frail-looking townsman is knocking you down, that's timing, not his stats.

The lasso has no auto-target. Because throw distance is charged by swinging the rope over your head, aiming is fully manual — so use Dead Eye for any throw that matters. It can't tag, but the slow motion is what you need, and a red crosshair confirms a valid target in range. Hold to keep charging and release to throw. All livestock except chickens can be roped, mid-size game like bucks can, and predators can't. Two practical uses: dragging a carcass out of water before it sinks so you can skin it, and rope-plus-knife kills on livestock, which are quiet and produce reliable perfect pelts without drawing witnesses.

Recap: Dead Eye's abilities come from story missions and its duration from XP, so no amount of grinding unlocks organ highlighting — and levels 9-10 need Trapper gear on top of challenges. Use it as a toggled half-second correction after auto-aim acquires the target, and remember it reloads instantly, lights dark rooms, and does not make you invulnerable. Duels are a separate system: half a second of charge, no tagging, fire when his arm moves. Weapon class, ammunition and cleanliness decide your damage, with familiarity outweighing base stats inside a class and zeroing speed the only per-gun stat you really feel; grey bars are potential filled by upgrades and ammo, red is reversible neglect. Rust and mud degrade guns far faster than firing them, so store long arms on the horse. Build to cover four engagement bands, use slugs to fix shotguns and dynamite arrows on legendaries, and lean on the cheap techniques — dive-dodging, gun-swapping instead of reloading, night stealth, and burst-then-block melee.

Horses

More words have been written about the best horse in RDR2 than about any other decision in the game, and almost all of them are arguing about numbers that barely move. The stats matter far less than bonding, saddle choice and how you handle a spooked animal — and one widely-discussed attribute doesn't exist at all.

There is no bravery stat

The compendium's courage ratings don't predict behaviour, and datamining the game's own AI conditions finds no bravery or breed term anywhere. What the code keys on is bonding level, fear state and threat proximity. Bonding is the only thing that changes how brave your horse is.

What actually decides whether you get bucked into a grizzly: whether the horse is fed and its cores are full, the type of threat and how close and how numerous it is, whether you're riding toward the danger or away from it, and how consistently you're calming — including in the seconds just before and after the threat appears.

That explains why player experience diverges so wildly. Someone riding an under-fed Arabian into alligator country without touching the calm button has a genuinely awful time and concludes the breed is high-strung; someone else keeps cores topped and calls out to the horse on a rhythm and reports Arabians are fine in gunfights and around predators. Both are describing their own habits.

It's also true that panic isn't one behaviour. Horses react differently to human gunfights, to wild predators, to heavy town traffic, and to deep water — a horse that's steady in a shootout can still lose its nerve near a gator, exactly as a real one might. So if a specific context keeps throwing you, the answer isn't a different breed, it's calming more aggressively in that context and keeping the horse fed before you get there.

The practical liberation in all this: ride the horse you like the look of. You'll spend more of this game staring at your horse's neck and tail than at any other object in it, and that's a perfectly legitimate basis for choosing one.

Beating the same NPC racer twice grants a permanent +1 speed to every horse you own. It's the only speed upgrade that follows you across mounts, it costs nothing, and the races are easy to skip past without realising what they're worth.

Bonding: the only progression that matters

Bonding actions are not worth the same, and the popular tricks are often the bad ones. Leading the horse on foot pays the most per action, soothing while riding is close behind, brushing is middling, and feeding, patting, hitching and unloading cargo are worth almost nothing. Since a single mid-tier level costs several hundred points, the "unload a carcass repeatedly" trick is close to useless — you're farming one point a time against a 750-point wall.

Two habits do the work. If you want to grind, lead on foot along a long road — a rail line makes a good route — and level four arrives in something like twenty minutes for a starter horse, half an hour for a mid-tier one, and under an hour for an elite one. If you'd rather not grind at all, brush the horse every time you mount and call out to it every eight to ten seconds while riding; it costs no dedicated time and keeps stamina topped as a side effect.

Respect the cooldowns, because they're what makes spam useless. Brushing has a several-second gap, patting has its own, and mashing either registers once. The tested loop that beats spamming is to rotate: brush, pat, lead two or three steps, stop, brush again. Keep the leading segment tiny — its value is in refreshing the other actions, not in the walking. You can also brush mid-ride by alternating between slowing and speeding up, brushing the neck as the horse drops pace.

Feed by hand from foraged berries rather than buying feed, pat after every jump you land cleanly, and get the bonding-boost trinket from the wrecked ship on the far north-east shore before you start grinding rather than after.

What level four buys is worth the effort: the stamina economy above, plus the calmer threat responses that people mistake for bravery.

Choosing and acquiring

Acquisition timing shapes this more than stats do. The white Arabian is free and wild in Chapter 2 once the story opens that area, which puts an elite horse in your hands long before you could buy a comparable one. A black Arabian and a gold Turkoman go on sale in Saint Denis around Chapter 4, and the remaining Turkomans don't appear until the Epilogue — though one can simply be stolen in Chapter 3 if you know where to find it.

The free Arabian

Elite handling makes it genuinely responsive — it weaves through obstacles at speed in a way heavier horses don't — and its stats are good enough to win NPC races before it's even bonded. It's free, it's early, and catching it requires a very slow approach; rush and you'll lose it, sometimes off a cliff.

The honest caveat is that it reacts to threats early and dramatically, sometimes before a predator has even appeared on your minimap. If you hunt on foot and keep it away from gunfights that's a non-issue. The risk is the fight you didn't plan for, where a bolting horse means no getaway — though bolting is also what keeps it alive.

The mid-game favourites

The two most-compared horses differ by one point of speed and stamina against two of health, which is close enough to nothing that you should pick on looks and feel. If you fight mounted, take the health. If you value nimbleness, the other steers noticeably more sharply where its rival feels stiff through direction changes.

Coat and saddle pairing is a real consideration for the same reason looks are: you're going to be looking at it constantly. And note that a specific coat can carry different stats from its breed-mates, so a coat you like isn't automatically a compromise.

Keeping more than one

Since panic is contextual, a small stable covers more ground than one perfect horse: something steady and heavy for swamp and predator country, something fast for open road and races, and a workhorse you don't mind risking. Rotating also spreads the emotional cost of the permadeath rule below.

Saddles and stirrups: the actual upgrade

Saddles don't change raw stats — they change three drain rates: how fast horse health drops, how fast stamina drops, and how fast stamina regenerates. And the tiering here is brutally clear. Trapper saddles beat every stable saddle on all three numbers, while base stable saddles are close to worthless with single-digit reductions. If you're hunting anyway, the pelts a Trapper saddle costs are trivial.

Among the good ones, the totals are similar and the split differs, so pick by what kills you. The panther saddle is weighted toward stamina economy; the beaver is the best at slowing health loss. As a rule of thumb the trail and vaquero patterns lean toward stamina and the roping and ranch-cutter patterns lean toward health — choose based on whether your horse tends to gas out or to get shot.

The stirrup slot is a hidden freebie: hooded stirrups carry an unlisted bonus to both speed and acceleration, so they should be on every horse. The exception is Trapper saddles, which can't take them because that bonus is already built in.

Permadeath is the harshest penalty in the game — and it's beatable

Your death costs a fraction of your money and puts you back on your feet where you fell. Your horse's death is permanent and takes hours of bonding with it. That asymmetry produces a genuinely useful and completely counterintuitive tactic: if you're both about to die, make sure you go first. Dying is cheap, and if you hit the ground before the horse does, the horse lives.

Don't count on reloading out of it. The autosave fires immediately after your death, so the most recent save is already past the point where the horse died. Manual saves are the only reliable insurance, which is another argument for saving before anything dangerous.

Horse revivers don't cover falls and tumbles, only wounds — and in a firefight they create a perverse choice, where reviving the downed horse is worth more than saving yourself even if the attempt will probably kill you. The cleanest mitigation is prevention: when you expect trouble, leave the horse somewhere out of it. Mounted shootouts are the one genuinely high-stakes activity in a game that is otherwise very forgiving.

Recap: there is no bravery stat — bonding, cores, threat type and proximity, direction of travel, and how consistently you calm are what decide whether your horse bolts, and panic is context-specific rather than a blanket trait. Speed is nearly irrelevant and caps out, stamina becomes a non-issue at bonding level 4, health only matters for mounted combat, and handling is something you adapt to. Beat the same race twice for a permanent +1 speed on every horse. Grind bonding by leading on foot, or fold it into riding with a brush-and-soothe habit, rotating actions to beat the cooldowns and grabbing the bonding trinket first. Trapper saddles outclass everything from stables — pick the split by whether you gas out or get shot — and hooded stirrups are a free speed bonus everywhere else. Finally, if you and your horse are both going down, die first.

Law & Crime

The law system has one rule underneath all its moving parts: a bounty happens when a lawman lays eyes on you, and nothing else reliably causes one. Every technique in this section is a different way of arranging for that not to happen — which is why masks, outfits and body-hiding get so much more attention than they deserve, and why distance and timing get so much less.

Witnesses: read the eye icons

The "murder witnessed" banner is the most misread message in the game. It means somebody has witnessed that a murder happened here — not that anybody saw you do it. A stranger stumbling across a corpse triggers the same banner as a man watching you pull the trigger, which is why it can fire minutes later while you're a mile away on a fast horse and feel like omniscient AI.

Three states, three icons on the minimap:

  • White eye — investigating. They've noticed something is wrong but don't know what happened.
  • Dark red eye — found the body, didn't see the killing. They can report a murder but not a murderer.
  • Bright red eye — saw you, or knows it was you. This is the one that turns into a named bounty if they reach the law.

Treat the banner as intelligence, not a sentence. There's no bounty unless the law actually sees you at or near the scene — so the response to a witness banner is to stay off the nearby roads, not to reload your save. And the reason it doesn't fire more often is mundane: missions, cutscenes and zone repopulation quietly despawn the bodies you left behind before anyone finds them.

Where a witness has identified you, you have a window. Kill or threaten them before they reach the law and there's no report, so no bounty and no penalty. Threatening works, and it's the honor-cheap option — though the chase behaviour is janky enough that a cowed witness sometimes runs the wrong way while the game still tells you to catch them.

Masks stop civilians, never lawmen

Two facts do most of the work here. A mask must be on before anyone sees you — equipping it after you've been spotted is worthless. And lawmen identify you through it regardless, so no disguise ever protects you from a lawman's line of sight.

Which reframes the whole system. A masked crime witnessed by civilians opens a wanted state against an "unidentified assailant" — no name, no bounty on Arthur. Your job isn't to be unrecognisable, it's to be somewhere lawmen aren't while that plays out.

Clothing, hair and horse don't factor into recognition; the game shipped without the wardrobe-disguise system some manuals still describe, so swapping outfits and mounts to sneak around is wasted effort. The exception is narrow and worth knowing: keep committing crimes in the same clothes in an area where you already earned a bounty in those clothes and witnesses eventually start handing you instant bounties. Change clothes and that stops. So outfit swapping is a repeat-offender countermeasure, not a disguise.

Finally, don't unmask while the law is still searching — pulling it off during an active search is exactly how an anonymous report becomes your bounty. And if a witness flashes the bright red icon while you're in an unfamiliar outfit, that's a known visual bug; no bounty follows as long as the law never sees you.

The quiet-robbery procedure, and why each step is there

Mask up before you approach, so no witness ever sees your face. Walk right up next to the clerk before drawing, because close range keeps the crime quiet and doesn't pull a crowd from down the street. Leave immediately and route around lawmen, since their eyes are the only real failure state. Keep the mask on until the search ends. Done in that order, stores can be robbed indefinitely without a bounty.

Being wanted: two phases

Active pursuit is the loud phase. Lawmen are hunting you, and the red circle on the radar tracks you while they have eyes on you — the important detail is that when they lose sight, the circle stays at your last known position. That's your escape: break line of sight and the search area stops following you. Resist and kill lawmen and the response escalates beyond ordinary deputies into wagons, dogs, town mobs and marshals, with the mix depending on where you are.

Passively wanted begins once you're out of the circle and unseen long enough for the gauge to empty. The law stops actively hunting, but while the bounty stands you'll meet extra lawmen and bounty hunters on that state's roads and checkpoints, and after half a day a posse will occasionally come looking for you in the wilderness if your crimes were severe enough.

Two boundary rules matter for planning. Crossing a state line ends an active pursuit but not the passive phase, and each state tracks its own wanted level and bounty separately. And there's a hard escalation: commit a dozen serious crimes inside an hour and towns go into lockdown — shops closed, interactions stripped, any lawman who sees you resuming the chase, with a dashed recognition ring on the map that's considerably bigger than the town itself. It lasts for hours of in-game time, so a rampage costs you access to a region for a long while.

Pay it, serve it, or live with it

Paying at a post office clears the state cleanly and — the reason most people do it — restores fast travel, which a live bounty blocks.

Surrendering to a lawman is often cheaper, but not reliably so: some players report the full amount taken anyway, and you serve jail time on top. Its real use isn't economy, it's jurisdiction. Because the arresting state sets the bill, surrendering in a state where you've done nothing wipes the expensive bounty elsewhere for pennies — the trick covered under Money, and the reason a lassoed stranger in Ambarino is worth more than a payment at a post office window.

Living with it is a genuine strategy rather than negligence. Bounty hunters are combat content that comes to you when you're bored, they're worth looting, and shooting their horses gets you the gold nuggets in the saddlebags — so they fund their own eventual clearing. Wounded ones crawl and need finishing. The cost is that they interrupt hunting, which is the worst possible timing, and that maintaining a maxed bounty pins your honor at the bottom.

A detail for anyone deliberately running hot: some regions send three waves of lawmen and then stop. To get more, you need a fresh crime with a fresh witness. And if you're planning to keep offending, paying off early is simply wasteful — at maximum bounty there's no further penalty to accrue, which turns a repeatable train robbery into free money.

Working the other side: bounty hunting

The alive-versus-dead question has a clean answer. Read the poster. A single "dead or alive" price is what you get either way — bringing the target in breathing adds nothing. Only when the poster lists a separate, higher alive price does mercy pay. Claims that alive always pays more, or doubles the payout, are wrong, and so is the belief that your own bounty gets deducted from your fee.

Take them alive for the other reasons: it's the honor-consistent choice, you get conversation on the ride in that you'd otherwise never hear, and some targets tie into side plots — deliver certain gang leaders alive and you can witness them hanged later as a world event.

Know also that bounty hunts are scripted mini-missions rather than free roam. Ambient plants and predators don't spawn, enemy placement is fixed, and the weather is set for you — so don't plan to combine a hunt with foraging. The mission ends the instant you collect the money, and if you're carrying a bounty of your own, don't loiter in the sheriff's office afterwards; that's a good way to be recognised.

Method matters more than concealment

The system isn't a blanket punishment script — outcomes depend on whether you're masked, what you do to a witness, and how you kill. Gunshot wounds read unmistakably as murder, so shooting someone while unmasked near anyone who might find the body is the reliable way to earn a bounty.

Indirect deaths largely slip through. An antagonised townswoman who throws the first punch, gets hogtied, and is left where a train will find her produces no wanted level and no bounty — no mask, plenty of onlookers, nobody cares. Environmental accidents are the loophole the system leaves open, and it's a broad one. It does still glitch occasionally, so don't treat any method as guaranteed.

Recap: bounties come from lawmen seeing you, not from witnesses knowing what happened — so read the eye icons, silence or scare off the bright-red ones, and treat the murder banner as information rather than a verdict. Masks must go on before you're seen, work only on civilians, and stay on until the search ends; clothing changes matter only as a repeat-offender countermeasure. Break line of sight and the search circle stays where you left it; crossing a state line ends the chase but not the bounty, and a dozen serious crimes in an hour locks a region down for hours. The petty crime tier costs $5, which is what makes deliberate arrest in a clean state the cheapest way to wipe an expensive bounty elsewhere. Paying restores fast travel; living with the bounty turns hunters into income. When collecting bounties, the poster tells you whether alive pays more — usually it doesn't, so take them alive for the story instead. And indirect, accidental-looking kills dodge consequences that gunfire never will.

Hunting & Crafting

Hunting is the load-bearing system in RDR2 — it feeds your satchel, your trinkets, your provisions, your saddles and half your wardrobe — and it's easy to reach Chapter 4 having barely touched it. The important thing to understand early is that perfect pelts are won before you pull the trigger. Preparation is the skill; the shot is a formality.

The rules that decide pelt quality

The animal's rating is a ceiling. Anything under a pristine three stars cannot produce a perfect pelt no matter how cleanly you kill it. The rating shows bottom-right when you aim, look through binoculars, or simply get close, so checking costs nothing — and it means most animals you see are not worth shooting for crafting purposes.

One clean hit to a vital organ. A perfect pelt needs an instant kill from a single shot to the head, upper neck or heart. A second shot on a three-star animal ruins it, and so does bumping the carcass with your horse before you skin it.

Learn the three hit types. Fatal kills instantly. Critical drops the animal after a stagger, and in story mode you can finish a critically-wounded animal with your knife and still keep perfect quality. A normal hit ruins the pelt outright. On anything medium-sized and up, only critical or fatal will do.

Study before you commit. Binoculars both study and track, so glassing an animal from a distance tells you the exact weapon and ammunition it wants along with its quality. Some animals' study text is more specific than any general rule — asking for a long-scoped rifle rather than any rifle, for instance — so study whenever you're unsure rather than trusting a chart.

Dead Eye's weak-point tier is the practical enabler here, because the sweet spot on most animals is small and near-impossible to hit freehand on a sprinting target. Some players still find plain headshots more reliable than organ shots; either works, and both are far easier with the marks on.

Weapon by size class — and the counterintuitive part

Here's the fact that saves you the most agonising: headshots from any non-explosive rifle round are equally effective on every medium, large and massive animal. A basic round from a bolt action does the same job as an express round from a scoped rifle, on a fox as on a grizzly. The most powerful round won't spoil a fox and the weakest will drop a bison. Nominal damage differences simply don't enter into it.

What does matter is matching the weapon to the size class — and the classes don't always match apparent size or how the carcass handles. A beaver is fox-sized and needs the small-calibre rifle; several large birds handle like mid-size animals but need small game arrows.

Get the buck trinket before you hunt anything else

The trinket crafted from the legendary buck's antler gives skinned parts a chance to come out a grade higher, which in practice rounds good pelts up to perfect. Since good pelts are far easier to get than perfect ones, it cuts the difficulty of every hunting task in the game — especially on small animals, where success without it is patchy and with it is routine.

It isn't a guarantee. Reports of mixed results are consistent with a hidden numeric quality value sitting under the visible star rating, so treat it as a large thumb on the scale rather than an auto-perfect button. It also can't rescue a pelt you destroyed — dynamite is still dynamite.

Legendary animals: stop being careful

Legendary pelts are always legendary quality regardless of how you kill the animal. That single fact inverts everything above: bring the most overpowered weapon you own with the best scope and the heaviest ammunition, and ignore clean-kill discipline entirely. Explosive rounds, incendiary shells and dynamite arrows are all fine.

The hunts are structured like small side quests. Entering the territory announces itself, then three clues — visible as yellow clouds under Eagle Eye — lead you to the animal. The map you're given early is a rough placemat that gets you to the right region, not the right rock.

Availability is gated in ways worth knowing before you plan a trip. Most are reachable early; one waits on a Chapter 4 mission; one only spawns once you've reached the highest hunting challenge rank; and a few live in the late-game region you can't reach as Arthur.

Two failure modes have specific fixes. "Too much activity in the area" means a side event — a stranger calling for help, a chase — is blocking the hunt; camp and sleep a few hours, or go resolve it. And a natural predator nearby will also suppress the legendary, so kill the predator first.

The famous one is the bear you hunt with a companion in Chapter 2, which is a scripted failure — you can't kill it during the mission. The trick is to not ride back with him: the mission ending doesn't despawn the animal, so staying in the area lets you track and kill it immediately. Expect a multi-shot fight where it charges and mauls you; break free at the struggle prompt and finish it once it disengages.

You can't permanently lose a legendary

Any legendary part you can't carry — or lose outright — is routed to the Trapper automatically, and selling a pelt to the Trapper or donating to Pearson functions as storage: they hold the material until you're ready to craft. So the counterintuitive correct flow is to sell the legendary pelt and then "buy it back" later as clothing. Nobody buys the whole carcass, so don't haul it across the map.

One real caveat: a long-standing unpatched bug can make a legendary pelt vanish on skinning. The tells are immediate — no pickup notification after skinning, and the carried item labelled a generic "pelt" rather than by name. If you see either, you're already affected, and the pelt won't be at the Trapper. It has been reported to strip previously-crafted legendary clothing too, so a manual save before each legendary hunt is cheap insurance.

Making spawns behave

Almost every "this animal doesn't exist" complaint comes down to one mechanic: the game won't respawn an animal at a spot while it still remembers the carcass you left there. Everything else follows from that.

Remove the evidence. Sell the carcass to a nearby Trapper, or walk into water until Arthur drops it — dropped in water it despawns much faster. Then the spot is free to repopulate.

Sleep to reroll. Making camp and sleeping cycles the spawns and the time of day, which is the general fix for an empty area. Several species are markedly more common at night.

Save at the spawn, not at home. For rare predators, camp and save at the spawn zone, then do a short patrol loop. Nothing there, or a two-star? Reload and go again. That turns a two-or-three in-game day wait into a few seconds per attempt, and it's the difference between a cougar taking twenty minutes and taking an evening.

Alternate two distant spots. Fast-travelling between two known spawn areas and sleeping at both ends burns enough in-game days per round trip to reset each one. Skip the sleeping and the loop doesn't work.

Compress the kill so the horse can't ruin it. Predators aggro instantly and your horse panics, so the whole engagement has to fit inside slowed time. On the growl, call to the horse once, drop straight into Eagle Eye — which buys time to swap to the bow and locate the cat — and as drawing the bow kicks you out of it, go into Dead Eye and take the shot. Redraw and re-enter immediately as insurance. Done properly the entire fight is two or three real seconds, so your first calming word hasn't finished and the horse never throws you.

Two smaller habits: learn the audio cues, since animal calls tell you which species are currently spawned so you know whether to wait or move on, and remember that legendary sites double as good ordinary farms for the same species afterwards.

The satchel chain

The big satchel is the single biggest quality-of-life upgrade available, holding ninety-nine of nearly everything, and it's earned by donating a fixed list of perfect pelts to Pearson — about twenty pelts across fourteen species, with deer and elk the only multiples. It's all-or-nothing: no partial credit until the whole list is in.

Plan it as one loop, because travel is the real cost rather than killing. Carry limits are what shape the route — you can hold multiple small and medium pelts but only one large at a time, so the biggest animals need dedicated trips. A neat trick is to save the two largest for last and bring a second horse to haul both at once.

Donate perfect pelts as you get them rather than banking them, because you lose carried pelts when you or your horse dies. And never sell a perfect pelt while the list is open — sell the good and poor ones and donate the rest.

The bottleneck is always predators. Prey species are everywhere; the cats are rare, and with the late-game region still locked there are only a couple of reliable spawn points for them. That's precisely where the save-at-the-spawn technique earns its keep.

You'll need a rifle (a repeater is not a rifle), a bow, a varmint rifle and the ability to craft small game arrows. All arrow types need flight feathers, which come from shooting birds — auto-aim makes hitting them mid-flight easy — and you can craft several at once kneeling in the field without setting up camp. Poison arrows additionally want a specific riverside herb from the southern swamps, and a lap to collect it doubles as good hunting country.

Where the materials go: Trapper and Fence

Two shops turn hunting into permanent upgrades, and they don't overlap. The Trapper takes pelts and hides and makes clothing, garment sets built around legendary pelts, and saddles. The Fence takes the non-pelt legendary parts — claws, teeth, horns — and makes trinkets and talismans. Trinkets need only the legendary trophy; talismans additionally need rare world items and ordinary jewellery.

That last detail has a planning consequence people miss: talismans chain off collection lines that have nothing to do with hunting. The rewards for mailing in your first cigarette card set and your first dinosaur bone coordinates are talisman components, as are a couple of items in hidden chests. If you want the core-drain reductions, start those collections early rather than treating them as end-of-run filler.

This is also the gear that gates the last two ranks of health, stamina and Dead Eye, which is why maxed cores are structurally the last thing you finish.

Fishing: position, not patience

The rod arrives from a camp companion activity rather than a shop, and it greys out in the item wheel unless you're standing at the edge of water deep enough to fish — if it won't equip, step closer to the shore.

The key insight for legendary fish is that the exact spot you stand is the controlling variable, not the weather or the time of day. Players handed nothing but screenshots of the correct standing positions have caught legendaries first cast, repeatedly. So don't wait for conditions; find the right rock, the right bridge ledge, the right patch of ice, and cast from there.

Legendary fish bite only special lures, which are sold at a single bait shop in the southern swamp and only unlock after you meet the fisherman who sets the whole quest going. Drawing your rod tells you which water type you're in, so match the lure to that and you're done thinking about tackle. Ordinary lures beat organic bait for anything big because they're reusable, at the cost of having to work them by reeling slowly to imitate a swimming fish.

When you're fighting one, don't spam the line-hold: use it no more than once every three pulls or you lose progress. Pull down on the stick while reeling, and expect about three pulls per window while the fish isn't fighting back.

Three legendaries live in the southern regions and are simply uncatchable as Arthur — don't waste an afternoon on them. The mail-in rewards are tiered, with cash and cooking ingredients partway through and, after all thirteen, a final quest that unlocks a master lure which works in every water type at once and shortens time-to-bite permanently.

Herbs, tonics and what's actually worth picking

Judge a herb on two axes: how many useful recipes it feeds and how strong those are, against how easy it is to find — spawn zone size, whether it's visible without Eagle Eye, and whether it's locked behind the Epilogue's regions.

That framing sorts the list quickly. Anything whose only recipe is bait is junk, because bait barely works in this game — which condemns most of the mushrooms and berries regardless of how common they are. The sages are the best herbs in the game on utility alone, feeding six worthwhile recipes, but two of the three variants are Epilogue-locked. The currants are worth learning to spot for the horse meal alone, which makes your horse immune to core drain and effectively ends brushing-and-feeding maintenance on long rides. One mushroom earns its place purely because it crafts a horse reviver you can make in the field when you're stranded with a crippled horse and none in stock.

The three seasoning herbs — thyme, oregano and mint — cover nearly the whole map in clusters of three or four and feed the food buffs that raise core gain, with mint's temperature protection the most universally useful of them. They're also what the herb-doubling trinket applies to.

On tonics, be honest: story mode is easy enough that they're near-optional. The default correct play is to loot them, keep a few, and sell the rest for satchel space. The exceptions are the moments where the game imposes a hard ceiling you can't play around — a timed endurance challenge, a genuinely sustained firefight — and those are worth stocking for deliberately rather than hoarding permanently.

One trap worth naming: tonic tier names don't reliably indicate effect size. The naming ladder implies a straight upgrade path, and at least one line breaks it — two differently-named versions restore identically, with the "better"-sounding one winning only on buff duration and easier ingredients. Read the item description text, which states the actual restore and fortify wording, rather than trusting the label.

Recap: a perfect pelt needs a three-star animal and one fatal hit to a vital organ, so study first and skip anything under pristine. Match the weapon to the size class rather than to power — headshots from any non-explosive rifle round are equally lethal on everything medium and up, small game arrows are the only option on the smallest animals, and the varmint rifle is the only one on moderate. Never use explosives, tomahawks, handguns or shotguns except slugs on the largest game. Get the buck trinket before hunting anything. Legendary quality can't be spoiled, so bring overkill, and their materials can't be permanently lost because the Trapper holds them for you. Beat spawns by removing carcasses, sleeping to reroll, saving at the spawn point and alternating two distant spots; compress predator kills inside stacked slow-motion so your horse doesn't throw you. Plan the satchel list as one loop and donate as you go. Trapper for clothing, saddles and the free challenge-gated reinforced gear; Fence for trinkets and talismans, whose components chain off collectible lines you should start early. For legendary fish, the standing spot is everything and special lures are mandatory. Ignore bait-only herbs, learn the sages and currants, and treat tonics as loot to sell rather than a resource to manage.

Cores & Condition

Arthur's condition is tracked by four systems the game barely explains — stat levels, cores and rings, weight, and temperature — and all four are hidden behind menus or invisible entirely. Once you know what each one is actually measuring, the maintenance stops feeling like guesswork and reduces to a couple of habits.

Stat levels: eight from playing, ten from shopping

Health, stamina and Dead Eye each have ten bars. Bars one to eight come from using that stat — ordinary play, plus the consumables tied to each one. That's the whole XP route, and it stops dead at eight.

Bars nine and ten aren't XP-gated at all. They come from completing all ninety challenges and then buying the reinforced gear each challenge line unlocks at the Trapper — four pieces per line. Finishing the challenges isn't sufficient; the purchase is what raises the stats. This is why maxed stats are structurally the last thing in a completion run and why nothing you drink will get you there.

The cost sounds alarming and isn't: the full set of gear runs to a couple of thousand dollars, against a map holding well over ten thousand in free gold bars — a large share of it collectible in Chapter 2 alone. In a game with almost nothing else to buy, this is the one purchase worth banking for.

The blocker is a challenge, not the money

The herb-picking challenge asks for dozens of unique species and gives you no way to see which ones you're missing. The compendium is not a valid checker — it counts herbs you picked before the challenge started, and those don't count toward it. So keep a written list and tick species off while the challenge is active. If you're already stuck, the only fix is re-picking against a list one at a time.

The usual culprits are the exotic orchids, since each species counts as its own unique herb — partial orchid coverage stalls the count silently while everything else looks complete.

Cores, rings and the gold state

Each stat has an outer ring you spend moment to moment and an inner core that feeds it. Getting a core to gold does four separate things, which is why it's worth more than it sounds: food yields more calories, the ring takes longer to start emptying, the ring refills faster, and there's a further delay before the core itself begins to drain. Net effect, you take more damage, sprint longer and hold Dead Eye longer.

Gold cores come from big game meat cooked with the matching seasoning herb — one herb per core, and the pairing is fixed: oregano for stamina, mint for health, thyme for Dead Eye. Alligator is the most convenient big-game source; the predators all work. A seasoned steak holds the gold state for roughly a full in-game day.

If you'd rather not cook, a saloon meal or the camp stew will do it, and upgrading the camp's chicken coop hands you two gold cores outright — a quiet argument for that particular ledger purchase.

Don't confuse a gold core with a gold ring: the ring is the temporary fortified state certain tonics grant. They stack, and the ring drains first, so a fortified gold-core Arthur absorbs a meaningful number of extra hits. That's what you want before a hard challenge or a long firefight — and it's exactly why the three seasoning herbs are worth picking every time you ride past them.

Weight: a hidden number you can actually read

Weight is a hidden scale from zero to twenty, where ten is peak condition. Sitting a point or two above the middle doesn't change Arthur's appearance, so you don't need to hit the exact number.

The mechanics underneath are simpler than the folklore. Every consumable carries a small calorie value, ten calories equals one weight point, and you lose a point roughly every twelve in-game hours — so maintenance is about twenty calories a day, ideally split across the day rather than eaten in one sitting. A few pieces of salted beef and a vegetable covers a tick. And crucially, weight changes only resolve on a screen fade — sleeping, fast travel, a mission ending. Eating with no fade afterwards hasn't registered yet, which is the source of most "I ate three steaks and I'm still underweight" confusion.

You can read your exact weight without menus-diving guesswork: the damage-taken stat in Arthur's general stats moves by three quarters of a percent per weight point, so a reading of minus two and a quarter percent means three points above the middle. Overweight genuinely reduces damage taken, which makes "slightly heavy" a defensible build rather than a cosmetic failure.

Listen while eating, too. A loud chime is a core filling, lower chimes are the rest topping up, and a distinct crunch means you've eaten enough to cover the naturally-lost point. It's quiet, but it's the only real-time feedback the system gives you.

Why diet advice never transfers

Calorie burn scales with activity. The same diet that holds perfect weight during a week of sitting in camp will leave you underweight across a week of missions and long rides — so someone else's steak-count prescription is calibrated to their play, not yours.

Two more reasons not to over-manage it: weight takes a couple of in-game days to update, which makes corrective eating a guess between waiting and overshooting; and the appearance thresholds are hair-trigger, snapping to gaunt or jowly around three percent either side while a fraction of a percent of drift can pass unnoticed. The sane approach is a steady habit — a real meal a day, the stew when you're in camp — rather than chasing the number.

Temperature: warmth is a point total

Health core drain depends heavily on whether your outfit suits the local climate, and the game never explains how it decides. Each garment carries a hidden warmth value, and the sum across what you're wearing puts you in a band: nothing to a couple of points is hot-weather dress, three points is temperate, four or more is cold-weather.

Where the points live is not intuitive. Every coat contributes, from one point up to four. Ponchos and gloves are worth one each — and gloves don't care about style, so fingerless gloves count exactly as much as thick winter ones. A handful of shirts and sweaters give one. Some vests contribute, sometimes varying by colour and fabric. A few boots give one or two. Trousers contribute nothing at all, and a few hats are the only headwear that counts.

The practical shortcut is that one heavy duster is worth the entire cold-weather requirement on its own, so a single coat stowed on your horse solves the snow without rebuilding an outfit. If you dislike how warm boots look, half chaps hide part of them. And one accessory that advertises warmth in its description actually has a value of zero — a labelling oversight, so don't count it as a layer.

Arthur's illness

The tuberculosis is not a Chapter 6 switch. It becomes a mechanic only after the collapse and diagnosis, which triggers on the way to a specific mission in Saint Denis — visual signs like reddened eyes appear earlier, but cosmetic onset isn't mechanical onset.

What it does is reduce maximum health and stamina cores, and the practical impact is modest — comparable to walking around underweight, which plenty of players do accidentally for most of the game. Some also report shooting feeling degraded on top of the smaller bars. Either way, it is not a reason to avoid free roam in Chapter 6; the reasons people find that chapter bleak are tonal, not mechanical.

If you want a save where Arthur shows no symptoms — for photographs, or for a permanently healthy sandbox — the anchor point is earlier than most people assume. Coughing turns up in ambient animations well before the diagnosis, so pick a stopping point before the audio starts rather than before the mission.

Recap: stat bars one to eight come from play and nothing more; nine and ten require every challenge and buying all the Trapper's reinforced gear, which is cheap against the free gold on the map — with the herb challenge the real blocker, so track species on paper while it's active. Gold cores make food more nutritious, delay drain and speed ring refill, and come from big game meat plus the matching herb, from stew, from a saloon meal, or free from the chicken coop; gold rings from tonics stack on top. Weight is a hidden 0-20 scale where ten calories is a point, a point drains every twelve in-game hours, changes only resolve on a screen fade, and the damage-taken stat is your exact readout — being slightly heavy is a small damage reduction. Outfit warmth is a point total where coats do the heavy lifting and trousers do nothing, and one heavy duster covers cold weather by itself. Arthur's illness starts at the diagnosis, not the chapter, and costs less than its reputation suggests.

Gold Medals

Gold medals are the one part of RDR2 that's genuinely hard, and most of the difficulty is bookkeeping rather than shooting. Half the runs that "should" have passed were already dead before the shooting started, because of two rules nobody tells you about.

The rules that decide whether a run is already lost

Every objective must be satisfied in one single run. Partial progress doesn't bank — replaying a mission resets you to zero on all of it, so three of five objectives from a previous attempt count for nothing.

"No health items" really means "no checkpoint reloads." Reloading a checkpoint restores your health, and the game counts that as using a healing item. On a twenty-minute mission with a long ride in the middle, this is the rule that ends runs: one mistake fifteen minutes in and you're finished whether you notice or not. Dying voids it too.

Accuracy objectives break on checkpoint reloads as well. If you've reloaded a checkpoint, the accuracy requirement has already failed — which is the real answer to "I only missed one shot and still didn't get 80%". For any medal attempt, restart the whole mission rather than continuing from a checkpoint.

You don't need them all. Only about two thirds of the 104 medals are required, so you can skip the three dozen hardest outright. The Epilogue has several very short missions that are cheap golds — collect those before you start grinding the brutal ones.

Check your progress mid-mission

Holding left on the D-pad during a mission shows your gold requirements and their current state — headshot count, whether a timed objective credited. Learning this early removes the single most demoralising part of the process: spending fifteen minutes on a run you already failed. If an objective is gone, abandon immediately and restart.

Replays: what you get and what you lose

The replay menu hands you a base-spec Arthur. You don't pick your horse, your weapons or your items — the mission decides, so your bonded elite horse and your favourite rifle are irrelevant. The allotted kit is always technically sufficient, which is not the same as comfortable.

A patch made this substantially better than its reputation: health, stamina and Dead Eye now scale to the mission's point in the story rather than starting from scratch, and your weapons and inventory approximate what you'd normally have unlocked by then. Older guides' difficulty ratings are pessimistic by a notch or two as a result. A couple of missions are still effectively impossible on replay because they demand more Dead Eye than the mission-appropriate allotment provides, so those are ones to get on the natural playthrough.

The other replay annoyance is structural: there's no restart-mission option, only restart-checkpoint. Once you're past a checkpoint, recovering from a failed run means dying or cancelling out, waiting for the open world to load, and re-entering from the menu — which roughly doubles the time per attempt. That's the argument for the recommended workflow: a dedicated save, autosave off, a manual save before each mission, reload on failure.

Accuracy is a fraction you control

Accuracy is hits over shots fired, which means the denominator is a resource. In a mission with only eight killable enemies, one shot per head gives you two misses at 80%. Deliberately padding with body shots — two or three per enemy, a dozen shots overall — buys you a third miss. So if your headshots without Dead Eye are unreliable, adding shots is a legitimate way to widen the margin.

The tension is that fast headshot kills protect the other objectives, especially the no-damage and no-healing ones. So headshots remain the better default and body-shot padding is the fallback for when your consistency is the bottleneck.

Two habits reduce waste. Stop firing the instant a red X appears on the reticle — that target is already dead and every extra round inflates your denominator. And don't shoot the man running away; he's usually far enough that you'll miss, and sparing him occasionally pays off in a later mission.

One trap specific to shotguns: accuracy is counted per pellet, not per shot. If half a buckshot spread connects, that shot scores about half. That forces you to let enemies close before firing, which collides directly with any no-damage requirement in the same mission.

Transferable tricks

Steal the aid the mission hands you. Several missions put consumables in reach — a stolen bottle of aim-steadying snake oil, brandy and a cigar in a train's baggage car, chewing tobacco on a robbed passenger. On replay those items are frequently the difference between having enough Dead Eye for an objective and not, and the aim-steadying one specifically fixes the reticle sway that makes some missions feel broken.

Get ahead of the objective. Where a mission asks you to do something within a time limit that starts on a prompt, check whether you can begin before the prompt fires. In at least one notorious mission you can walk into the target area early and be a couple of steps into the task before the timer even starts — which converts an impossible limit into a comfortable one. Look for this pattern whenever a timer feels miscalibrated.

Feed the stock horse. Replay races put you on a basic horse, and hand-feeding it a stamina herb when its core drops is the standard answer. Combine with route shortcuts — cutting corners past bridges and through tree lines — and races that "can't be done on the stock horse" become routine.

Save-scum the coin flips. Where a mission has a checkpoint immediately before a random-feeling segment — a knife fight whose opponent opens with an unavoidable stab, a struggle minigame whose duration varies — reload that checkpoint until the dice fall your way, provided the mission has no accuracy or no-healing objective to protect. When it does, the whole run is the unit and you restart from the top.

Melee opponents with knives can't be blocked. Blocked knife strikes still deal damage, so in a no-damage knife fight the working approach is aggression: close and keep swinging until they drop it. Defensive play loses.

Some of this is jank, not difficulty

A few missions are notorious because of inconsistency rather than design: shots that pass through stone pillars, allies who die for no reason, prompts that fail to appear, Dead Eye refusing to mark a target it marked last attempt, identical inputs producing different NPC behaviour. With reliable aim and reliable triggers, those same missions are short and easy.

The practical consequence is emotional rather than tactical — when a run fails in a way that makes no sense, that's usually what happened. Don't rebuild your whole approach around it; take the aim-steadying consumable if one is available, and go again.

Recap: every objective must land in one unbroken run, and reloading a checkpoint silently voids both the no-healing and accuracy requirements — so restart missions from the top and check progress with the D-pad rather than hoping. You only need about two thirds of the medals, so bank the cheap Epilogue ones and skip the worst. Replays give you a base-spec Arthur with story-appropriate stats, no restart-mission option, and a couple of medals that are effectively impossible that way. Treat accuracy as a fraction whose denominator you control, stop firing at dead targets, and remember shotguns are scored per pellet. Expect no-damage objectives to take several attempts, dread sidearms-only combined with headshots and a timer, and lean on the transferable tricks: steal the mission's own consumables, start timed tasks before the prompt, feed the stock horse in races, and save-scum random segments when no accuracy objective is in play.

Challenges

Ninety challenges across nine branches, and they are the longest single block of work in a completion run — comfortably thirty hours if you approach them badly, and the gate on your last two stat bars. Almost all of the time saved comes from three ideas: farm respawns instead of hunting spawns, hogtie anything that needs to hold still, and know which challenges let you walk away without losing progress.

How the system works

Each branch has ten steps, and finishing a branch unlocks a four-piece reinforced equipment set at the Trapper — which you then have to buy. Completing all nine also unlocks a bonus set. The sets are what raise your stats past eight: three branches feed each stat, one complete set gives the first bonus bar and all three of that stat's sets give the second. Across the whole set of ninety you'll also collect a few hundred dollars and enough XP to reach level eight on each stat naturally.

A branch starts either when the story offers it or the moment you satisfy its first challenge — so you can force-start any branch by simply doing step one. Progress counts in free roam, inside story missions, and in mission replays, which makes replays a legitimate farming tool. One caveat there: finish the replayed mission, because abandoning back to free roam can roll the progress back.

The trap to internalise is that many challenges reset on a save load or on standing up from a table, while others explicitly don't. Which is which decides your whole approach to a step, so check before you grind — a challenge that survives standing up can be farmed in short safe attempts, and one that doesn't must be done in a single sitting.

The four techniques that do most of the work

Farm a de-aggro respawn loop. This is the single best trick in the challenge system. Ride up to an outlaw camp with no weapon drawn and they'll merely warn you off rather than opening fire. Ride away until the hostile marker clears the radar edge, note that spot, and ride back — the camp is now repopulated with men sitting passively around the fire. You can then walk up and execute whichever kill type the challenge wants with no combat pressure at all, and repeat from the same turn-around point indefinitely. It's weapon-agnostic, which means one camp clears several steps across different branches. The same respawn logic works at proper hideouts for stealth-kill requirements: kill quietly until the alarm, leave until the red zone clears, return and the same men are standing in the same places.

Hogtie for anything that needs a stationary target. A tied victim is the universal solution to distance and grouping requirements — a long tomahawk throw wants someone who won't move, a multi-kill explosive wants four men piled together, and a very long rifle shot wants a target left in an open field while you climb a hill. It also covers the challenge that literally asks you to leave people on railway tracks, which needs no train at all: tie, place, untie, move on.

Stand up rather than lose. In the gambling branches, leaving a table forfeits the bet but does not lose challenge progress on several steps. So the correct play when a hand or a game is clearly going badly is to get up, which means you never bank a loss — check per challenge, because a couple of them do reset.

Sleep and save at the spawn. Anything gated on a rare animal is really gated on respawn timers. Camp beside the spawn, save, patrol, and reload when nothing useful appears — and where a respawn needs a couple of in-game days, sleeping twice with a manual save in between both passes the time and protects you from the animal killing you and despawning.

Layered on top: combine steps with travel you were doing anyway, and pick single dense locations rather than roaming. A branch done in one well-chosen valley beats the same branch done across the map.

Branch by branch

Bandit cheap in honor if you route it right

The whole branch can be done in an evening for a few percent of honor, because most requirements have an honor-neutral route. Robbing pedestrians is the one unavoidable hit; everything else can be arranged around.

Coach theft is the backbone. Take a stagecoach ride to a ranch with a wagon fence, steal a coach on arrival and drive it to the fence — that's the ride and a theft in one trip. Loot the coach's rear box before delivering, since delivery forfeits the contents, and breaking the lock rather than shooting your way in costs no honor. Camping near the station lets you force a fresh coach to spawn by opening the sleep prompt and cancelling it, which turns the whole thing into a loop. That single loop covers the coach and wagon steps and, by unharnessing a team at the horse fence, most of the horse-selling step too.

Carry your bounty forward. The $250-bounty step is nearly free if you simply haven't paid off the bounties you accrued doing the rest of the branch. Starting from clean means deliberately causing trouble in a town and then paying it off afterwards. Note that bounty hunters aren't lawmen — killing them adds nothing.

Use targets whose deaths don't cost honor. Hogtieing outlaws for the railway-track step is honor-free, and their cave hideout is a reliable supply. Similarly, recovering your own money from a pickpocket counts toward the robbery steps at no honor cost at all.

Speed is what prevents bounties. Shop robberies want a route — two towns close together, hit and ride, don't waste time intimidating witnesses, just leave the search area. For the train steps, only one robbery per train counts and you need five separate trains, so rob a single passenger and get off; the ride back to the previous station roughly matches the pursuit, so you arrive clean.

Explorer the easiest branch in the game

Every step after the first is simply "find treasure", with no skill, timing or technique involved. The only thing to know is that random chests in houses and camps don't count — only map-driven treasure hunts do.

That reduces the branch to a route: the three main multi-map hunts yield nine treasures between them, which clears the entire category. The maps chain into each other, so the hunts self-feed once started. The smaller one-map hunts also count if one of the big ones is inaccessible.

For the first step you only need a map, not a treasure — the easiest is bought from (or taken off) a treasure hunter on a river ridge in the middle of the map, and any fence sells the same map later for a little more.

Gambler two genuine grinds, everything else is a formality

Blackjack. Double instead of hitting whenever you're at 19 or less and the double-down step completes itself. Beating a dealer in two specific towns is the simplest challenge in the game. The three-hits step is the notorious one — it's pure chance with nothing you can influence, so optimise the attempt rate instead: rent a room, sleep to morning, then save and reload to reset the saloon so no other players are at the table and you're playing the dealer alone. Bet the minimum, always hit, and when your starting hand is high enough that three cards would obviously bust, stand up and immediately sit back down — you forfeit a tiny bet and save a lot of time. Loading a save doesn't reset this one's progress, so it's safe to save after a success. Budget anywhere from a quarter hour to a few hours and do something else while you grind.

Dominoes. Play the variant where end values must sum to multiples of five if you can — it's much easier to count and to see who's ahead — and lead with a double-five for an immediate score. Play doubles early generally. Wins against three opponents don't count for the no-draw step, because that game has no draw pile. Standing up when you're behind saves the streak.

Five finger fillet. It's one long two-handed input sequence, and the winner is whoever completes more laps. Hold the aim button during your opponent's turn to preview the sequence, then don't watch the screen while you play — the on-screen prompts lag behind your inputs, so go by the controller or from memory and you'll go far faster. Two thumbs is the fastest input; one thumb alternating is slower but immune to the double-presses that fail a lap. The key exploit: the first game at a table is always the easy two-button pattern, and no challenge requires beating a chain of challengers, so after each win stand up and sit down again to reset to that easy pattern. That's also a repeatable money loop.

Poker. Play the opponents, not the cards. RDR2's poker hands out surprise wins on bad hands and surprise losses on good ones, so card-strength-first play is unreliable. Early on, call small raises to see cards and fold to big ones — with more than two players any large raise can spiral toward an all-in you don't want. Once you have a good flop, raise several times the small blind to fold out the short stacks and bad hands; that's how you build the cushion the rest of the strategy needs. Then use it: opponents' willingness to fold scales with how far ahead or behind they are, so a player near busting folds to almost anything while a player up on the night may only fold to an all-in. Raising hard when a genuinely scary card lands — a high card, or one completing a flush or straight, but never a board pair — is what folds the folders. And if you're stuck at a table with an unbeatably lucky player and nothing but short stacks, leave and rejoin: chip stacks persist but personalities are re-rolled.

Herbalist the branch that decides when your run ends

The final step wants one of every herb species in the game, and it is the reason all ninety challenges can't be finished before the Epilogue — a handful only grow in the late-game region. Two rules make it much worse than it looks: it isn't retroactive, so every species must be picked again after the challenge starts even if your compendium already has it, and plants looted from corpses don't count.

Since there's no in-game way to see which species you're missing, keep a written checklist and tick species off while the challenge is live. Orchids are the usual silent blocker because each species counts individually — which is why it pairs naturally with the exotics collection quest that sends you after all of them anyway.

Elsewhere in the branch, the crafting steps are trivial once you know which recipes need no pamphlet — the sage-based bitters and horse stimulants are available from the start of Chapter 2. The one heavy step is the batch of special tonics, which needs herbs by the dozen; do it after the big satchel or split it across several trips. The cooking step wants every meat type seasoned, so it's really a hunting checklist, and the herb-doubling trinket makes gathering the three seasonings twice as fast.

Horseman two long rides, both won by route planning

The cross-country rides fail instantly if you touch water, dismount or fall — which is the whole design problem, because the game's auto-path routes you straight through rivers. The answer is manual waypoint chaining: place waypoints deliberately so every crossing is a bridge or a railway bridge, including one placed carefully enough to send you over a bridge in a town's centre rather than around it. The town speed penalty is worth the guaranteed dry crossing.

Railway tracks are the backbone of both rides — straight, dry and obstacle-free. Prepare before you start: feed and brush the horse, eat, and carry stimulants, because feeding mid-ride is a needless risk. Tap the calm button periodically to keep stamina up, which works best on a well-bonded horse. Pausing stops the timer, so pausing to think is free. Ignore every stranger who tries to interrupt you; engaging risks a dismount.

The time limits are more generous than their reputation — a mediocre horse and one stimulant clears the long one with minutes to spare on a good route. Do the longest ride after the Epilogue opens the destination, or you'll be arriving into a town full of lawmen who want you.

Elsewhere: the lasso-drag step needs the victim alive when you start (they'll die en route, which is fine) and resets if you dismount or release, corpses and carcasses don't count. The trample step accepts anything, so flightless birds or fish in shallow water are the fast route. And the break-every-breed step has an in-game checklist and doesn't require the unique horses.

Master Hunter cougars are the whole branch

Killing five cougars with a bow is the hardest step, and there's a technique that transforms it: in Dead Eye, don't pull the bowstring — hold aim, activate Dead Eye, and the bow behaves like a single-shot rifle, letting you mark and fire almost instantly. That same trick is excellent for birds. Poison arrows kill on any body hit, so precision stops mattering. Stay mounted for the visibility and the extra aiming time, and if you're thrown, the cougar turns around before attacking again, which buys you the moment you need to stand and shoot.

Then it's respawn management: a cougar needs a couple of in-game days, so camp nearby, sleep twice, save, and check. Saving also insures you against the cat killing you and then despawning. Removing the carcass — selling it to a nearby Trapper, or dropping it in water — is what frees the spot to repopulate.

Two steps hinge on a fact people don't know: bait doesn't spawn animals, it only lures ones already nearby, so a bait step fails silently in an empty area. Pick somewhere with both predators and prey already in it. And tracking requires studying an unknown animal first before its tracks become trackable, which also makes finding perfect-quality specimens far easier because studied tracks show quality.

The rest is arranging travel: the tracking step is free on a long ride through changing terrain, the call-and-clean-kill step wants abundant herbivores in one field, and only the rabbit step cares about pelt quality — which forces the varmint rifle. The opossum is found by ear (a loud pig-like squeal with no boar in sight) at night, and either grabbing or killing it counts. The legendary panther only exists after the ninth step is done, and unlike every other legendary it hunts you.

Sharpshooter fiddly rules, cheap setups

The headshot streak is a gift with one quirk: a headshot that doesn't kill neither adds to nor resets your count, so if a kill seems to have reset you, you hit the neck or another critical spot rather than the head.

The disarm step is the one that punishes ignorance. Mark all three enemies' hands in a single Dead Eye activation and take all three weapons at once — because exiting Dead Eye auto-reloads your gun, which resets the challenge. Doing it manually means never reloading mid-streak, so bring something with capacity: a carbine or dual sidearms. Single-shot rifles and break-action shotguns literally cannot complete it.

Shooting hats off is a buckshot trick — fire from medium range with marks on heads and the spread lifts hats without killing; if people die, back up further. Once free-aim Dead Eye is unlocked you can simply aim above their heads instead.

For the extreme-distance shots, the game's render distance is the real obstacle, so use a guaranteed setup rather than pacing out a field: a tower target at a hideout viewed from across water gives you the distance reliably. For the thrown-weapon distance check, back away while aiming — when the reticle turns from white to red you're comfortably past the requirement. And the bird steps are all easiest against a mixed flock, which also satisfies the two-species-in-one-Dead-Eye step.

Survivalist the small game step is a definition problem

Eight consecutive one-shot small game arrow kills sounds like a marksmanship test and is really a taxonomy test: "small game" means the animals Arthur stows whole in his satchel — rats, bats, squirrels, frogs, snakes and the smaller birds. A rabbit is a size class up and silently breaks the streak. Verify by aiming and checking the info prompt, which must specifically recommend a small game arrow. The streak resets if anything takes more than one hit, is killed by another weapon, or wasn't small game.

The right venue makes it easy: a cave full of rats and bats, small birds perched on rocks in daylight, or a recurring saloon rat-clearing event. Farmyard chickens are the fastest option and cost you honor and possibly a cruelty bounty, which is a fair trade only if you're not mid-honor-push.

The scavenger step needs the animal to start eating before you shoot, so leave a corpse and be patient. The heavy-fish step is easiest in a cold northern lake full of big fish, and legendary fish count toward both fish steps. The crafting steps mostly need pamphlets you can buy from a fence rather than hunt for, and the fishing-from-a-boat step just needs a borrowed rowboat in the southern swamps.

Weapons Expert one camp, four challenges

This branch is where the de-aggro respawn loop pays off most: several of the late steps can be cleared at a single outlaw camp, riding out to the turn-around point and back between batches, executing whatever kill type the current step demands on men who are sitting down and not reacting.

The tomahawk-retrieval step has a rule that wastes hours if you don't know it: the counter only starts once the tomahawk has been retrieved, meaning dropped and picked up. Throw it at your first enemy and the count stays at zero. So throw it into a tree or the ground and pick it up first, and your first kill registers. It resets if you fire any weapon, if anything dies to something else, or if you throw a different tomahawk — so carry only one type and discard the rest.

Elsewhere the requirements are mostly shopping and crafting problems. The long-barrel step needs the barrel modification fitted at a gunsmith, not a particular gun. The crafted-shotgun-ammo step wants ten kills with either craftable shell, both of which use materials you're already looting. Poisoned throwing knives make the mounted-kill step trivial because they kill on any body hit — pair it with the bandit bounty step, since a live bounty is what generates mounted hunters to throw at. And the no-damage grizzly step is simply two poisoned knives: one to the head, then chase and finish it.

For the early knife and throwing-knife steps, note that the weapons themselves are gated behind a couple of Chapter 2 missions before fences and trappers stock them — until then there's a cabin in the north that has them lying around.

Recap: ninety challenges across nine branches, each branch unlocking reinforced gear you must then buy — and that purchase is what raises your stats to ten. Progress counts in missions and replays, but many steps reset on a save load or on standing up, so check before grinding. Most challenges are available in Chapter 2 and all ninety only in the Epilogue, because the final herb step needs the late-game region. The techniques that matter: farm de-aggro respawn loops at outlaw camps, hogtie anything that must hold still, stand up rather than bank a loss at the tables, and sleep-and-save at animal spawns. Beyond that it's knowing the rules that silently void progress — reloading in Dead Eye resetting the disarm streak, tomahawks needing to be retrieved before they count, rabbits not being small game, bait only luring animals already present, and the herb challenge ignoring everything you picked before it started.

Play It Harder

RDR2 ships with no difficulty setting, and its free roam is tuned generously enough that the protagonist absorbs a startling number of bullets. So every source of challenge is something you impose — either by rule or by mod. The useful way to approach it is to identify which of the game's systems have gone slack, and target those rather than blanket-nerfing yourself.

What's actually gone slack

Supplies are cheaper and faster than hunting. Buying food always beats stalking a deer, so the entire survival layer — hunting, fishing, cooking, cores — becomes optional flavour rather than a requirement. Any rule that limits shopping puts those systems back in play.

The economy has a huge spike in it. A handful of treasure maps and the free gold lying around the map produce more money than the game has uses for. Everything downstream — camp upgrades, weapons, ammunition — stops being a decision.

Aim assistance is doubled up. Auto-aim and Dead Eye are interchangeable crutches doing the same job. Keeping both means combat can barely be lost; the sharpest single change is to drop one.

Restoratives remove consequences. Health cures and tonics mid-fight are what make gunfights unloseable rather than tense, and refusing them is the highest-impact rule available without mods.

The rule toolbox

Treat these as a menu to build from rather than a list to adopt whole. The strongest combinations pair a combat restriction with a resource restriction, because either alone is easy to play around.

Combat

No Dead Eye for the whole run, allowing it only where a mission forces it and perhaps against dangerous game. Free aim with no reticle and no auto-aim. HUD off entirely — which does more than look clean, because your spare ammunition and Dead Eye meter then drain unseen and force genuine caution.

No restorative items during combat. No dual-wielding. And for a much larger jump, encounter-level restrictions: melee-only or bow-only fights, which people who've tried them describe as both brutal and the most fun they've had with the game.

One honest warning: if your raw aim is weak, dropping Dead Eye and auto-aim doesn't make the game harder, it makes it unplayable. Pick one to keep.

Ammunition scarcity

The most developed rule in circulation, and the one that changes moment-to-moment play the most. Buy one box of special ammunition per gun and no more; when a gun runs dry, switch to another rather than resupplying, and only restock in town when you're genuinely empty rather than topping up a half-used box.

The crucial refinement is to avoid regular ammunition — it's looted off enemies far too readily and quietly undoes the whole rule. For the same reason, main a pistol, rifle and shotgun rather than revolvers and repeaters, whose ammunition early enemies drop constantly.

Cap arrows at around forty across all types combined, and only rebuy once you're out of the last one. That single rule teaches you to walk over and recover spent arrows, which is exactly the behaviour the game's fiction implies and its economy never requires.

The effect is dramatic: a wolf attack is genuinely tense with two rifle rounds and three shells, and every miss costs you something. Note the counterpoint that "regular ammunition only" on its own is roughly how most people already play — it needs the rest of the scheme to bite.

Poverty

Donate the large majority of money and items to camp, keep only what you loot and earn from missions, and don't hunt treasure or grind money at all. Buy nothing, or hold to a couple of narrow exceptions — saddlebags for outfit storage, one good saddle, one horse reviver.

Skip the satchel upgrades, or cap your carry stacks near the starting amount, and note the inversion that catches people out: upgrading your lodging increases the money accumulating in the gang's savings box, so skipping camp investment is what keeps you poor.

Survival and routine

Eat at least twice a day with plausible meals and sleep every night, napping in the day if a mission ran long. Skip the camp stew specifically, because its day-long core boost is exactly the buffer you're trying to remove. Only walk when a bar hits zero — and hold the horse to the same rule, no galloping when it's tired or underweight.

Limit armament to one long gun and one sidearm per outing, change loadout only at camp, skip weapon upgrades beyond a scope, and clean guns only with oil you found rather than bought.

Turn the minimap off and never open the map — or use the gentler version where you may consult it only where you could plausibly read one or ask directions: at camp, in towns, at stations and signposts. This is the rule players report changing the game most, because it forces you to plan a route before riding out instead of following a line.

Law and death

Keep a maxed bounty in every state and never pay it off or surrender, so the pressure never lifts. No fast travel, no trains, no stagecoaches.

For death penalties, the fiction writes itself. One horse only: if it dies, carry the saddle to a stable on foot before buying a replacement, and never steal an NPC horse as a substitute. If Arthur dies, sell the horse afterwards — the killers took it — and donate everything you own to camp, restarting from nothing.

The enforcement problem

Self-imposed rules only work if you're the kind of player who keeps them, and plenty of people know they aren't. If your rules keep quietly slackening at the moment they'd cost you something, the mods below are the honest answer — they make the restriction mechanical rather than moral.

Mods, if you're on PC

The full overhaul. The established difficulty mod ports the older game's hardcore mode wholesale: max health halved, regeneration slower and later, Dead Eye capped lower, carry and ammunition limits cut, most prices doubled and sale values halved, mission payouts halved, loot and drops reduced, XP gains cut, cores draining twice as fast, and fortifying tonics made rare while ordinary ones only restore partially. The joins are thought through — cosmetics are deliberately left alone so it's a difficulty change and not a fashion tax, and the reduced XP means your small health pool stays small far longer, which is what keeps the combat dangerous deep into the run. An optional module also cuts honor gains, so a maxed bar becomes genuine work. It needs a mod loader and script hook, and it conflicts with anything else touching core drain.

The surgical option. A scaling script instead adjusts player and AI weapon damage, melee damage and health regeneration independently, which lets you pick your asymmetry — lethal enemy bullets while your own damage stays stock, or heavy damage both ways for fast mutual-kill shootouts, with the regeneration cut as the counterweight that sustains lethality between fights. Its best feature is a single hotkey that flips the whole thing back to vanilla values instantly, so a story mission that's become impossible under scaled damage can be done at stock difficulty and the mod switched back on afterwards. Two practical notes: edited config values only take effect after toggling off and on, and the script has been abandoned for years, which for script-hook mods means patch breakage is likely.

Targeted system mods. A needs mod plus a money-reduction mod, both with adjustable settings, are the direct fix for the two slack systems above — they re-couple hunting to eating and make robbery worth doing. If you'd rather build a broader immersion stack, the two lessons from people who've done it are that mods touching the same system collide — damage mods needing hand-patching against each other, movement mods conflicting — and that big compilation mods are best cherry-picked file by file rather than installed whole. Rebalance mods also expose their values in config, so raising crime prices or adjusting an economy curve to taste is usually a text edit rather than a search for a different mod.

Recap: there's no difficulty setting, so pick your own — and target the systems that have gone slack rather than nerfing everything. Shopping being cheaper than hunting, the treasure-and-gold money spike, doubled-up aim assistance and mid-combat restoratives are the four culprits. The highest-impact rules are refusing restoratives in a fight, one box of special ammunition per gun with regular ammo banned outright, poverty rules that route your income into the camp, and turning the map off. Keep either Dead Eye or auto-aim if your aim is shaky, and remember that regular-ammo-only alone isn't a challenge. If you won't enforce rules on yourself, the hardcore overhaul makes them mechanical, while the scaling script offers per-encounter control and an instant vanilla toggle for missions that stop being possible.

PC Setup

RDR2 on PC has an unusually lopsided settings menu: most options are effectively free and a small handful eat your entire frame budget. Hand-tuning beats every preset — a custom configuration outperforms even a mid preset and comes close to doubling the frame rate of a maxed-out one, with a visual difference most people can't spot in motion.

The handful of settings that actually cost frames

These are where your budget goes, roughly in order of expense. Everything else is noise by comparison.

  • Lighting quality — one of the most demanding settings in the game; dropping it to medium is worth up to about 30% of your frame rate.
  • Reflection quality — around 20% for the same move to medium.
  • Water quality — roughly 15%, with only a slight visual cost.
  • Tree tessellation — up to 15%, and worse than benchmarks suggest: the hit spikes when you're actually looking at trees, which in this game is constantly. Turn it off.
  • Water physics — the gain from stepping this down is described as massive, and it's CPU-sensitive. Never run it maxed.
  • MSAA — brutal, costing roughly a sixth of your frames at 2×, a third at 4× and half at 8×. Use the temporal or post-process anti-aliasing instead.
  • Volumetrics and shadow quality — genuinely expensive but with real visual payoff; these are the first things to spend spare headroom on.
  • Grass and geometry level of detail — both worth several percent, and geometry detail in particular leans on the CPU, so drop it further on a weaker one.

Textures are the exception that proves the rule: their cost is VRAM rather than GPU speed, and anything below the top setting looks noticeably worse. Set them as high as your card's memory allows regardless.

What to leave maxed

Anisotropic filtering, global illumination, far shadow quality, ambient occlusion, mirror quality, particle quality and lighting, tessellation, long shadows, tree quality, parallax occlusion, decal quality and soft shadows are all close to free — going from lowest to highest across the whole group costs only around 10-15% combined. Max them and stop thinking about them.

A few small wins hide in the advanced list: full-resolution ambient occlusion off is worth a few percent for a difference you won't see, fur quality at medium is worth several, and the unlocked volumetric raymarch option is negligible visually — though it's not recommended on older cards.

Two settings people get wrong in the other direction: the post-process anti-aliasing option is a matter of taste rather than performance and some find it actively hurts the image, and reflection anti-aliasing should stay on its lowest step rather than off, because reflections look genuinely bad without it.

API, upscaling and the config file

Test both graphics APIs. Vulkan is generally faster, but the result is hardware-dependent and DX12 is also the fix to try first if you're getting crashes or graphics errors. Run each and keep the faster one.

Update the upscaler. The game ships an old DLSS version; swapping in a current DLL is a straight performance win without the old version's artefacts, and it works on the mainstream store versions. Gains scale with output resolution — modest at 1080p, substantial at 4K — so tune your settings first, start with upscaling off, and step down through the modes only until the artefacting bothers you. Keep its sharpening low, and don't stack the game's own sharpening on top. On AMD hardware the equivalent upscaler works but has been associated with a specific graphics-state crash and some ghosting during camera movement, so treat it as the option that needs watching.

Turn the in-game vertical sync off. It makes the game choppy; force it externally in your driver control panel instead.

The game is GPU-bound to an extreme degree. Underclocking a high-end CPU to a fraction of its speed and cutting it to four cores produced identical frame rates in testing — so don't chase CPU upgrades for this game, and don't blame your processor. The exceptions are the couple of CPU-sensitive settings noted above.

Settings live in a config file under your documents folder, which also holds a hidden asynchronous-compute option that's disabled by default. Some players report less stuttering with it enabled on Vulkan; it's hidden and off for a reason, so treat it as an experiment rather than a fix.

Known annoyances with file-level fixes

Ultrawide letterboxing. Parts of the game letterbox and pillarbox on wide displays. A script-hook-based mod fixes the letterboxing permanently with no per-session step; a more comprehensive tool also fixes pillarboxing and field of view but must be run before every session, and may trip a false antivirus flag. Either can introduce minor graphical oddities, so pick by how much the framing bothers you.

The intro sequence. Skipping the legal videos is a simple file drop; a more aggressive version skips the main menu too and loads straight into your save.

Driver-related crashing. If you hit a pattern of freezes followed by lost display output and a driver that needs reinstalling, suspect a recent graphics driver rather than the game. Rolling back to an earlier driver version, stepping back one release at a time until stability returns, is the fix people land on. Fullscreen-windowed mode has also been implicated in lingering instability.

On a handheld. The game runs at 720p and 30 frames per second with a preset that trades volumetrics, global illumination and filtering down hard while keeping textures, trees and mirrors high — which is the right shape of compromise, since the cheap settings stay cheap even on weak hardware.

Recap: a hand-tuned configuration beats every preset by a wide margin. Spend your budget on textures (VRAM-limited, always max), shadows and volumetrics; cut lighting, reflections, water quality, water physics, tree tessellation and MSAA, which between them account for nearly all the cost. Leave the dozen-odd cheap settings maxed. Test Vulkan against DX12 and keep the winner, replace the shipped upscaler DLL, disable in-game vertical sync in favour of your driver's, and don't blame your CPU — the game barely notices it. If you're on an ultrawide or sick of the intro, both have file-level fixes, and a crash-and-lost-display pattern usually means a bad graphics driver rather than a broken game.

Undocumented

RDR2 is full of mechanics it never mentions — verbs that exist but are never prompted, systems that quietly track things, and interactions that only turn up if you try something the game didn't ask for. These are the ones that change how you play rather than just being trivia.

Verbs the game never tells you about

Making people surrender no shot, no witness, no honor cost

NPCs can simply give up. Aim a weapon at a stranger and hold it — the interaction wheel switches to defuse, antagonise or rob, they'll talk back, and then their hands go up. Hostile encounters don't need a kill, and a broken will costs you no honor and generates no crime.

Grapple with a shotgun equipped and let go and the release animation reads as a threat, so they surrender with hands raised instead of resuming the fight. A point-blank surrender with no ammunition spent.

You can shoot the gun out of someone's hand. They're left unarmed and either flee or come at you with fists, so you can holster and settle it as a brawl — winning an armed confrontation with nobody dead. It works in duels and out of them.

And it works both ways. An NPC can knock your gun away mid-fight, at which point your sidearm slot reads empty and tells you to take weapons from your horse. It isn't a soft-lock — walk up to your horse and handguns are restored automatically, since the horse carries spares. Recovery means returning to the horse, not hunting for the dropped gun.

Grapples have vertical verbs. A grabbed or hanging NPC can be hauled up onto a ledge or shoved off it, which is a non-shooting way to move bodies and live people around level geometry. In a fistfight, grappling a weakened opponent and crouching takes him to the ground, the same input pulls him back up, and holding a direction with block throws him hard that way — into objects, or out of windows.

Hogtie everything. Melee-attacking with the lasso drawn is an instant hogtie. Leaving people tied rather than knocking them out or killing them avoids honor loss when you rob campers, and hogtying a shopkeeper means they can't report you once you're out the door.

Environmental and melee kills quiet, and often consequence-free

Water drowns people automatically. Tackle someone in a river or lake and the animation transitions into holding them under. Grappling someone into shallow water does the same. It's a free silent kill — though it still registers as a crime if anybody sees it, so the witness rules apply as normal.

Guns are melee weapons. Press the melee button with a gun in hand and Arthur pistol-whips or rifle-butts — the rifle butt also disarms. A second strike is an instant knockout, and the attack button instead gives a close-quarters execution. Every weapon type has several execution animations from the front and from behind, including with a bow. All throwing weapons work in melee too.

Crouching makes every strike lethal. While crouched, melee strikes are instant knockouts even head-on. And when an enemy charges to tackle you, mashing melee throws him back instead — occasionally that even works on a big cat, though the window is tight.

Draw straight into Dead Eye. Opening a confrontation via the draw prompt on a hostile puts you into Dead Eye from the very first shot, so you get the tagging window without building up to it — enough to tag a whole camp before anyone reacts. You can also dive while aiming and keep painting targets mid-jump, with the shots resolving as you get up.

Watch the crosshair colour. Hits to the neck and upper body can trigger bleed-out deaths, and the tell is the crosshair flashing dark red on impact — that's the critical-hit indicator.

Two small ones: explosive rounds open train safes like dynamite with a smaller blast, and you can shoot or melee certain windows to get into buildings that otherwise have no entrance.

Horse handling infinite stamina, swimming, and ground-hitching

Patting refills stamina, and the cooldown is ten seconds. Calling out to your horse while galloping tops up its stamina, and with full bonding that's effectively unlimited running with no stimulants at all. The cooldown is invisible, which is why mashing does nothing — cadence, not spam.

Your horse swims like a boat across deep water for as long as its stamina lasts, and running out means being thrown off. Patting works while swimming; feed and stimulants don't. So on a fully-bonded horse you can hold still in the water to minimise drain and pat every ten seconds to keep it topped up indefinitely.

Ground-hitching exists. Hold the dismount input for a second or two and you get a hitch prompt with no post in sight — Arthur ties the horse to bare ground. It's fussy about conditions: reasonably flat grass, and the horse standing completely still. Perfect for foraging trips off-road.

Assorted handling: mount fastest by pressing run and mount together while approaching from behind; you can mount from a height as long as you land on the horse's back; double-tap-and-hold the brake to walk backwards; and charged horseback melee lets you pick a striking side and time a real cavalry charge with a hatchet or tomahawk.

On wild horses, calming isn't strictly needed — you can lasso them or leap across from your own horse. When breaking one, pulling the stick consistently back and to one side is far more reliable than reacting.

Two conveniences: refreshing your outfit from the horse cleans Arthur up anywhere, replacing a bath and erasing visible wounds from the model. And keeping a warm hat on the horse converts a temperate outfit into a cold-weather one without carrying a whole second wardrobe.

Hunting shortcuts time savers and quality protection

Park your horse almost on top of the carcass before skinning and the animation is significantly shorter. Careful not to trample the animal, which damages it — but over hundreds of skinnings this is the biggest single time saver in hunting.

Lasso-dragging an unskinned animal doesn't hurt the pelt. That makes the rope the right tool for repositioning a kill: dragging one off your horse, hauling one out of water before it sinks, or pulling a mid-sized animal close enough to finish with a knife — which keeps the pelt perfect and draws no attention.

An unskinned carcass sells for more than the skin and carcass sold separately — but carcass capacity is tiny even across two horses, while skins stack deep. So skin when you're loading up for volume, and carry whole animals only when you're heading straight to a buyer. Selling a pelt far from where you hunted it also pays more.

Small game that fits in the satchel never spoils, so keep a handful of pristine birds, rats and bats on you permanently. They cover hunting requests and the Trapper items whose animals are annoying to find on demand.

Eagle Eye highlights every interactable object, not just animals, tracks and plants — which makes it a looting tool indoors and in camps, not only a tracking one. Aiming at an animal you've already studied also shows its quality, so you never waste a stalk.

Binocular watching fills in Arthur's journal drawings progressively. Each viewing fires a generic new-entry notification, but usually it's updating an older sketch — and it stops once that animal's drawing is complete. You can milk a flock or a swarm by panning across it repeatedly.

Two behaviour notes: standing up from a crouch is enough to scare off some animals, and a charging grizzly can be faced down by not moving at all — done right it stops short, roars, and leaves. Done wrong it kills you.

One quiet quirk: an animal that shows no star rating at all despite being studied is very likely perfect and will yield a three-star skin — those tend to be script-spawned or encounter-related.

And on arrows: always collect poison arrows back off the corpse. They're expensive in herbs and capped in capacity, so re-crafting is real work you can avoid by walking twenty feet.

Law tricks cheaper surrenders and free clears

Surrendering can be cheaper than paying. Handing yourself to a lawman means jail time but a discount on the bill and a small honor bump for doing the right thing. If you change your mind mid-surrender, reaching for your gun behaves like a duel.

A bounty you can't pay can be cleared for nothing. Spend or donate everything you own, then surrender — you serve the time, get a short scene, and walk out with the bounty gone because there was nothing to take.

Defuse the investigating lawman. Where a crime has been reported but no bounty has landed yet, defusing often ends with being told to move along and no bounty at all. Repeated defusing has Arthur protesting his innocence; lingering after being told to leave undoes it.

Insults count as attacks. Antagonise a citizen and if it escalates, witnesses treat you as the aggressor. The reverse also holds: if they insult you first, self-defence applies and roughing them up without killing usually costs no honor and isn't reported.

You can turn in fugitives you find. Obvious criminals from encounters can be delivered to a sheriff, and you can steal a bounty target off the bounty hunters escorting them. Gang members don't count. One trap: if that sheriff has an open bounty mission, walking in may play that mission's dialogue instead of acknowledging your prisoner, leaving you stuck with them.

Gang hideouts can be raided early and again later. They're accessible long before the story sends you and re-populate roughly weekly, which makes them a renewable source of targets, loot and horses. Enemy-owned horses are also worth taking — their saddlebags often hold gold nuggets, and killing a horse flagged as enemy-owned is less likely to cost honor.

Free resources and travel the camp is a shop you already own

Camp gives away more than the supply wagons. There are crates of whisky and beer, a few cigars, and an unlimited coffee pot beside Pearson's stew — which is why gang members loiter there. It may be deducted from camp supplies like anything else you take, so it isn't quite free, but it's yours.

Outside camp there's free stew at a couple of town entrances and free drinks in crates scattered around the map. Some locals report you for taking them and some don't care. There are also wilderness spots and houses where Arthur can sleep for free with no NPCs ever spawning — effectively private property.

Buying a train ticket spawns the train immediately, so you never have to wait for one. Once aboard you can ride for the scenery or rob it. Hijacking a locomotive and switching to cinematic mode can trigger proper travelling music.

Cinematic mode is more capable than it looks. While auto-riding you can still use the weapon wheel, eat, drink a tonic, pat or brush the horse, use Eagle Eye, open the map and set a waypoint, and control speed across eight steps by tapping run or brake. The horse follows roads even with no waypoint set. Stay alert, because predators and bounty hunters don't respect the camera mode.

Two more: call out to NPCs driving a wagon and you can tag along for a free ride, and a wagon you're driving stays on the road automatically while you're aiming a gun. Steamboats ply the big river and never dock, but reach one and you can hijack it — with the warning that an invisible boundary rings the playable water and crossing it sinks you.

Menus, shops and quality of life small frictions with fixes

Hold the loot button where drawers or lootable items cluster and Arthur works through all of them in sequence rather than one prompt at a time.

Hold the back input deep inside a catalogue or a pause menu to exit all the way out in one action instead of stepping back through every level.

You can abandon a mission from the log — select it and choose abandon, which takes you to the failure screen with restart and checkpoint options. No need to die on purpose.

Every grocery store that sells clothing has a wardrobe room, usually behind a door into a tiny adjacent space, where you can access and rearrange all your outfits as if at home. One town's is outside down an alley instead.

Watches are equipment, not just loot. Equip one from the kit section and pull it out from the item wheel to check the time — it shares a slot with binoculars and the camera, and doesn't work mounted. Hair pomade likewise applies straight from the satchel with no trip to a barber.

Turn on hold-to-reel in the accessibility controls if fishing is straining your hand — it swaps the mashing for a held button and costs nothing.

Iron sights exist. In first person with an unscoped weapon you can zoom further into proper iron sights, and once toggled it persists across guns until you switch to a scoped rifle. It never applies on horseback.

Two cosmetic oddities: double-tapping the weapon-wheel button with a handgun drawn holsters it with a gunslinger spin, and long weapons can be removed from your horse's flank by swapping the equipped weapon to an empty slot and then swapping a second weapon before closing the wheel — the switch animation plays and the visible gun disappears.

One inventory workaround: large fish normally ride only on the horse's back, but taking one to a campfire, entering the cook menu and choosing to stow rather than eat puts the whole uncooked fish into your satchel. Get close enough that only the cook prompt appears, and repeat with the fire still up.

World quirks and collectibles things that only exist if you try them

Cut down hanged animals. Carcasses and fish strung on drying racks at NPC camps have a cut-down prompt and behave as a normal harvest — including firing compendium entries. Other people's camps are a free source of pelts and fish.

Shoot the rope at a gallows to save whoever is about to hang. The same input rescues hanged victims in outlaw country, which is how their documents are looted.

Dogs defend their owners, and can kill you. Attack a homestead resident and the household dog charges — and if you're unarmed or mid-brawl, a farm dog mauling can genuinely kill you. Treat one as a second combatant, not scenery.

Premium cigarettes contain cigarette cards. Every pack bought in a store includes one, weighted toward cards you don't have — which turns the collection from a scavenger hunt into a shopping trip. If your inventory caps out, discard the cigarette stack to keep buying. You can keep mailing in complete sets for rewards after the quest is finished.

Stolen hats are the most overlooked collectible in the game — around forty NPC-only designs that no shop sells, with a wardrobe section of their own but no completion credit, no trophy and no mention in the official guide.

Every fence but one is usable early. The mission people think unlocks fences only opens the one at the ranch; the others work before it.

Hair and beard growth, and weight change, only apply after a cutscene. The last beard stages take an age, so stack hair tonics and then repeatedly trigger time-skips — camping, sleeping, fast travel, coach rides, waiting outside a shop until morning.

Arthur's mood is rendered. Well-rested with full cores he looks cheerful and may sing to himself while riding; empty cores and no sleep show as visible exhaustion, up to badly shaking hands in Dead Eye.

Two social oddities: greeting immediately after antagonising someone (or the reverse) produces special dialogue, and at the theatre you can antagonise the performers right before a dramatic climax to make them fumble it.

One economy note worth repeating here because it's easy to lose forever: several unremarkable-looking valuables are talisman components — a quartz chunk, a couple of bracelets, vintage handcuffs, petrified wood, an abalone shell, and specific earrings. Fencing them can make them very hard to reacquire, so hold anything that looks oddly specific.

Recap: the game hides a whole non-lethal toolkit — aiming alone makes people surrender, guns can be shot out of hands (yours included, with your horse holding the spares), grapples move people vertically, and water drowns anyone you tackle into it. Guns work as melee weapons, crouched strikes knock out instantly, and drawing from a confrontation prompt hands you a free Dead Eye window. Patting refills horse stamina on a ten-second cadence, which makes stamina effectively infinite and lets a bonded horse swim indefinitely. Park your horse beside a carcass to skin faster, lasso-drag without hurting pelts, and keep satchel-sized small game permanently since it never spoils. Surrendering beats paying a bounty, an empty wallet clears one for free, and defusing an investigating lawman often prevents it entirely. Camp gives away drink and coffee, train tickets spawn trains on demand, cinematic riding allows almost everything, and cigarette packs contain the cards you're missing.
