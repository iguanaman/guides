Strategy Guide

Fields of Arle

A solo strategy guide — worker placement, land reclamation, and the building engine

How to Think About Arle (Solo)

Solo Arle is the full game at full weight: same board, same nine half-years, same four workers per season. The only rule change from multiplayer is that the cross-season special action is gone — no borrowing a Winter action space during Summer or vice versa. Nothing else is bolted on: no automa, no bot, no artificial blocker. The base system already supplies all the tension the solo game needs, and this guide is written entirely around that constraint.

What actually beats you

There's no starvation spiral here and no external threat. Feeding is present but forgiving — a steady economic nudge, not the punishing pressure of Agricola or Caverna — and there's no event deck, no bad-luck draw, no rival stealing your spot. With roughly 30 action spaces and 18 buildings on the board at once and minimal rules steering beyond the food requirement, the game gives you far more good options each turn than you have workers to spend on them. That openness is disorienting on a first play — reading every option's full text before committing to a move feels like the only way to choose well, and it can freeze you into real analysis-paralysis for a session or two. It eases fast: by the end of a first play most people already want another, and the sense of being overwhelmed doesn't survive a second sitting.

Because the game gives so many reasonably good options rather than one obviously correct move each turn, it plays lower-stress than Agricola-style worker placement — no move feels clearly wrong, so the tension is quiet rather than sharp. Losses still trace back to decisions, though, not variance. Post-game reflection on a disappointing score almost never turns up "bad luck" — it turns up a specific, nameable choice: a tool upgrade delayed a round too long, a vehicle bought late, investment spread too wide instead of committed deep. That's the good news for a strategy guide — causes are legible and fixable next time — but it also means there's no shortcut. You have to understand why a play is strong, not just copy a move list, because the choice space is too large to memorize or solve outright: worker actions, vehicle loadouts, and trade-for-food choices compound into a combinatorial space nobody's calculated the objectively optimal line through. That size is a feature — it's what keeps the game from going stale even after a dozen-plus plays, because you genuinely can't converge on one "solved" opening and repeat it.

Breadth vs. depth: the central tension

Every game hands you the same open menu: expand land (peat-cutting vs. dike-building), breed animals for offspring and byproducts, upgrade tools for stronger actions, process raw goods into higher-value products, buy or build vehicles for efficiency, trade with the neighboring towns. You cannot run all of it at once. Nine half-years and four workers a season is not enough action economy to max livestock, goods processing, and infrastructure simultaneously — choosing what not to do is the actual game, more than any individual action choice.

This is a full point-salad scorer — unreclaimed land penalties, merchant contracts, accumulated commodities and goods, animals, building tiles all contribute — and that breadth is deliberate: farming-heavy, crafting-heavy, wood-production-heavy, merchant-contract-heavy, and animal-husbandry-heavy builds are all viable end states, along with straightforward generalist play. None is mechanically dominant. But breadth of viable openings cuts both ways: it's exactly what makes the early board feel like a sprawling tableau of possibility on a first look, and for players who want a single preferred asymmetric starting plan handed to them, that open-endedness can read as directionless rather than exciting. If you're the type who wants to commit to a plan and see it through, pick your two reinforcing pillars early (livestock feeding a goods chain, or infrastructure feeding a build-heavy finish) and treat the third as filler — trying to run three independent engines with the same four workers a season is the single most common way to end up with a mediocre score despite a full board of half-finished ideas.

Payoff is gradual, not explosive

There's no single engine-explosion turn here the way there is in Terraforming Mars or Ark Nova. The payoff is subtler and slower: around half-year 6-7 of 9 the farm "matures" — tool upgrades, vehicles, and processing chains started early begin to compound together and production starts humming. It never fully runs itself, though; hard choices continue all the way to the final half-year. That gradual curve is why early passivity is expensive: a half-year spent undecided in half-years 1-3 is a half-year of compounding you don't get back by half-year 7-9. There's no catch-up mechanic and no late-game multiplier that rewards a delayed pivot, so the early game's real job is getting generators — forests, fields, animal pairs, tool upgrades, vehicles — online, not scoring points directly.

Scoring starts negative — don't panic mid-game

A messy mid-game board — half-upgraded resources, unreclaimed land, an incomplete dike line — is normal, not a sign you're behind. Unreclaimed land actively costs points until it's dealt with, so an early scoring snapshot reads deceptively low; the correction comes in the back half through big buildings and space-clearing (cutting the last peat off a moor, extending dikes to remove the penalty). Treat your mid-game board as under construction, not behind schedule — the players who panic and chase quick fixes mid-game are usually the ones abandoning a compounding plan for an isolated bonus that doesn't pay back what it costs.

Where the real decisions live

There's no rival in solo, so worker-placement conflict in the literal sense — someone else taking the spot you wanted — doesn't exist. What replaces it is the building pool. Buildings are the game's scarcity lever: their powers are subtle but strong, you can't build them indiscriminately and expect points, and the specific mix available at setup meaningfully gates which strategies are even open to you that game. A few differences in the building lineup between setups can butterfly-effect into a completely different viable plan, so it's worth reading the full available pool before committing rather than assuming your favorite combo will be sitting there. This is also where the variable-building-pool replay lever is weaker than it first looks — the pool is fixed per copy of the game, so most of Arle's long-term replayability comes from the sheer size of the combinatorial action space rather than from setup variance alone, though setup variance still meaningfully reshapes each individual game's plan.

The other real decision, specific to solo, is what the missing cross-season action costs you. In multiplayer, each player gets one action per round from the season that isn't currently live — a release valve that opens up an option when the live season's row doesn't have what you need. Solo removes it entirely: you're locked to whichever season's row is active, full stop. That's not a minor rules footnote — it's a genuine restriction relative to the multiplayer game, and it means solo planning has to be built around the season you're actually in, not around "I'll grab that Winter action space during Summer if I need to." Any plan that depends on borrowing across seasons needs a same-season substitute built in, because in solo that safety valve simply doesn't exist.

Together these two constraints — a building pool you don't control and a hard season lock with no cross-season escape hatch — are what make solo Arle a puzzle of prioritization rather than execution. There's no explosive climax and no external disaster to react to, just constant quiet tension from knowing you can't optimize everything, and a final score that reflects how well you read the board and committed, not how the dice fell.

Opening Principles

Arle is a sandbox — the building pool is different every setup, so a memorized move order breaks the first time your favorite building isn't on the board. What travels between games instead is a resource-priority framework and a sense of what an opening move actually needs to accomplish.

Resource priority: wood > clay > peat > board space

When a flexible action lets you choose what to collect, default to this order. Wood and clay are the scarce inputs that everything else is built from — clay especially, since outside rare setups only the (Driver) Clay Worker space produces more than 1-2 clay per action, making it the tightest resource in the game. Wood is generally more abundant, but grabbing forests early is still one of the strongest lines available, since only one board space ever produces forests and only five exist total. Peat matters next: it pays for feasts, upgrades into brick, and feeds the peat boat trade, but it's a secondary currency rather than a primary building block. Board space — clearing moors, moving dikes — matters least of the four early on, because open space is only worth something once you have goods to put on it; there's also only one space on the board that plows fields, which makes it a scarcer bottleneck than its popularity suggests, though less contested in practice than forests.

Vehicles are infrastructure, not upgrades

A cart is close to mandatory, not optional. Actions can produce timber and brick directly, but at too thin a rate to fund a real building plan — the vehicle-based upgrade path is what actually supplies volume at scale. Beginner consensus converges on getting a cart online by round 2: it opens up food acquisition through Destinations and lets you upgrade wood and clay whenever you want, and every round it's delayed is a round of worse conversions banked. Build a second cart soon after the first.

Garage capacity exists for a reason — don't stop at one large vehicle. Each additional large vehicle functions as a free resource-upgrade action every single round from the moment it's built, and that advantage compounds the earlier it comes online. Vehicle construction itself only has one board space, so it's worth treating as a contested spot: if you're not there first, be ready to imitate, take the action out of season, or grab it early in the turn even in a slightly worse form rather than let it slip to later in the round.

On tool upgrades specifically, there's real disagreement about their place in the opening. One school sequences a workbench/tool upgrade (axes and shovels/spades together, since shovels double as clay help and make it easy to open board space by moving dikes) as the very first move, before collecting anything — betting that an efficiency multiplier is worth buying before you've gathered a single resource. The opposing, more strongly argued view: spending scarce starting wood and clay on tool upgrades early is weaker than it looks, because a multiplier does nothing until you already have an engine running to apply it to — better to sequence resources into vehicles and buildings first, and treat tool upgrades as something to revisit once you're already repeating the action they'd boost. If you're unsure which applies, lean toward the second: an upgrade sitting unused for several rounds while you catch up on raw materials is lost value, while raw materials converted into a cart immediately start paying off.

Front-load generators, not one-off goals

Split what you can build or acquire into two categories. Generators — animals, fields, forests, vehicles, tool upgrades — accumulate resources or value every round once they exist. Goals — finished clothing, high-end buildings, cleared dikes or moors, Travel — are one-off point or advantage payoffs. Neither category alone wins: an all-Generators game never converts its engine into points by the final scoring, while an all-Goals game stays too small to compete. The opening favors generators specifically because of when they pay off: a breeding pair, a productive forest, or a milk/wool producer built in round 2 earns for the rest of the game, while the same asset built in round 8 barely returns its own cost. Prioritize productive assets — a breeding pair of animals into your starting stall, fields, forests, wool/milk producers — ahead of one-off scoring buildings early, then let the balance shift toward goals as the game gets shorter.

Stables are worth flagging inside this framework: animals themselves are easy to acquire, but only one board space builds stables and only three exist total, so a space-efficient animal-ranching plan is bottlenecked by stable availability specifically, not by animals.

Don't skip the weak version of an action

Even when an action's unupgraded output looks small — two wool off a base Loom, for instance — it's still usually worth taking over passing. Output compounds across only nine rounds total, and there's no reward for waiting until an action's yield looks impressive before using it. This is really the same logic as favoring resources over tools in the opening: a small steady producer running from round 2 outperforms a bigger producer that only comes online once you've spent several rounds upgrading it first.

That logic also argues against chasing only glamorous lines. The most fixated-on plays — special-power buildings, the Master (tool-upgrade) action, becoming a huge flax or wool producer — aren't reliably the strongest use of an action; some of the best actions on the board are unglamorous ones like Grocer, Cattle Trader, and Sailmaker. A broad spread of small, repeatable gains across several sources can outscore committing everything to one flashy engine. A single well-timed action can also matter beyond its own output — building a peat boat off a Wainwright action as early as year 2, for example, is not exciting on its own, but it secures a vehicle line early and sets up every subsequent turn that depends on it.

Pick 1-2 target buildings before round 1, then adapt

The building market is the one thing that changes setup to setup, so scan what's actually available before the game starts and pick one or two buildings to anchor your plan around, rather than assuming a favorite combo will show up. Aim across the game to complete at least a couple of high-value buildings plus a handful of smaller ones you can actually extract value from before the game ends — a building you can't use in the rounds remaining isn't worth its cost no matter how strong it looks on paper. Beyond that anchor, experiment freely; treat the anchor buildings as a target to steer toward, not a fixed script that ignores what the board actually offers this game.

Common beginner trap: over-investing in Destinations/travelling. Travelling for food feels productive — it delivers food, minor goods, and a sense of progress every time you take it — but it's a support action, not a strategy in itself. It's really just a food-generation engine, and food's only job is to convert into the cost of buildings later. Leaning on it as a primary line starves the vehicle-upgrade pipeline that big buildings actually need, and by the closing rounds the math flips entirely: selling goods for food at that stage costs more points than the food is worth, so stop travelling once you're converting stock into final buildings. Use Destinations to plug a specific food shortfall, not as a default whenever nothing else looks urgent.

None of this replaces reading your actual board. The moves above are a starting framework for the choices every setup forces on you — what to collect, when a vehicle pays for itself, which building to anchor on — not a checklist to execute blind. The VP-on-Home-Board scoring category starts at roughly -16, and a good final score needs to climb well clear of that hole, which is the real reason a slow, tool-heavy, goal-light opening tends to underperform: there simply isn't time later to make up for rounds spent on anything that doesn't eventually turn into resources, food, or building progress.

The Building Engine

Setup determines which buildings are even on your board — some are removed at random before the game starts — so this section is a framework for evaluating whatever you're dealt, organized by the game's six real tiers. The recurring lens across every tier: a building earns its keep in proportion to how many worker-actions it lets you skip, not its raw point value in isolation. A cheap building that quietly removes a recurring action from your rotation every round is worth more over a full game than an expensive one-off payout — because a one-off effect, however large, can't generate the repeated critical mass a recurring conversion does. That's also the trap of chasing too many narrow, single-use buildings: each one demands its own action and board space to build, and if its effect only fires once, the action economy spent building it rarely gets paid back. A handful of buildings you'll use every round beats a shelf of buildings you used once each.

Starter Houses (Light Green)

The rulebook frames these as newbie-only, meant for a first learning game and then set aside. In practice they hold up fine mixed into any game, even for an experienced player — a few plays with them is enough to learn what they do, and there's no real reason to stop using them afterward.

  • Farmer's House — rated very good: it removes the need for separate Clay Worker and Peat Cutter actions, and that action-skipping is consistently worth more than its low cost suggests.
  • Plow Maker's Workshop — good: it removes the need for the Farmer action. Valuable specifically if you're trying not to overuse Farmer, since Farmer competes for Wood and Horses you'd rather spend on Vehicles, and Cows you'd rather spend on Food production.
  • Novice's Hut — merely okay. It removes the need for Farmer plus Dike Builder/Warden actions, which sounds strong on paper, but if dikes aren't actually your bottleneck and you'd rather keep those action spaces for their other values, the benefit goes mostly unused. Grain scarcity is rarely the problem it solves either.
  • Workshop — the standout of the tier, rated as close to overpowered. It removes the need for Wood Cutter/Trader and Farmer actions, and it enhances the Master action directly. Its real strength is that it's exploitable: it scales with your Workbench, Oven, and Weaving Loom upgrades rather than paying out once, so its value compounds across the whole game far beyond what the other Light Green buildings offer. If it's available, it's usually the pick of the tier.

Across the tier, the spread in quality is real and consistent — Workshop is a clear step above the rest, and Novice's Hut is a clear step below unless dikes happen to be your specific problem. Take Workshop on sight; treat the others as fine but replaceable.

Small Houses (Dark Green)

Very cheap relative to every other color — a Small House costs only a Worker's Action and a homeboard space, and only returns 1 VP directly. Their real value isn't the point, it's the extra actions they let you execute over the course of the game.

  • Weaving Parlour — okay, but only if you're not already running the Linen Weaver action, which does the same conversion better; on its own, the extra peat it yields is welcome but not a reason to build it.
  • Colonist's House — good: it mirrors Farmer's House by removing the need for Clay Worker/Peat Cutter actions, but the cost is paid in Wool. That makes it situational — skip it if you need that wool for a clothing strategy elsewhere, take it if wool is sitting idle.
  • Carpenter's Workshop — good, and specifically important for an animal-focused build: it gives a second route to upgrade a Stall into a Stable without spending the Brick you'd rather keep for other buildings.
  • Schnapps Distillery — weak. Food has too many alternate sources in this game for a narrow single conversion like this to matter once your economy is actually running; it's hard to build a strategy around it.
  • Loading Station — okay: it works specifically for a Peat-focused strategy chasing Resource Track score, but if you're loading vehicles with Travel Destinations or Building Materials instead, it doesn't contribute.
  • Litter Storage — weak: an expensive trade with a restrictive requirement, rarely worth building. The one situation where it earns its keep is trading Flax for a Horse when you're specifically short on horses for a Carriage — otherwise it's close to dead weight, and only worth considering very late if it can add animal VP without costing you Resource Track VP elsewhere.
  • Wood Trader — very good and frequently used: it removes the need for Wood Cutter/Trader plus Farmer actions. Its one real requirement is that you already have vehicles moving to support using it, so it's gated by board state rather than standalone.

Two of these — Schnapps Distillery and Litter Storage — are near-useless outside a narrow edge case, and skipping them costs little. The rest are all reliable enough that a couple of well-chosen Small Houses, picked to match resources you're not already using elsewhere, is worth more than trying to own several.

Minor Craft Buildings (Yellow)

Not very expensive, but each one really wants a specific strategy behind it and a building from another color to combo with — this is the tier where "does this fit my plan" matters more than "is this good in a vacuum." The tradeoff to watch: they're built using upgraded materials you might need for something else, so an off-strategy Yellow building is a real opportunity cost, not free value.

  • Turnery — very good: it removes the need for a Peat Cutter action and returns extra Wood on top. Combines well with Farmer's Inn.
  • Smokehouse — good: removes the need for a Peat Cutter action. Combines well with Sluice Yard Inn or Waterfront House.
  • Smithy — good, and cheap for its category, but its real value only shows up if you build around it: a small vehicle fleet (Hand Carts, Peat Boats) that on its own is worth only a few VP can be converted through Smithy into a much larger point total. Combines with Joinery and the Farmer action.
  • Cooperage — excellent for an animal-heavy strategy chasing Resource Track score, and essentially dead if you're not running one. A clean example of a building whose rating depends entirely on which pillar you've committed to — don't judge it outside that context.
  • Bakehouse — weak, close to a trap: food has too many alternate sources, and the trade is expensive with an awkward restriction (limited by how many Ovens you've upgraded, which is itself an expensive investment). Rarely worth building.

Bakehouse is the clear weak point of the tier; the rest reward being matched to a strategy you're already running rather than picked cold.

Major Craft Buildings (Orange)

All six have unique costs and are available every game with no setup variance — the only question is whether your board state makes the cost worth it. Like the Yellow tier, these complement other buildings and strategies rather than standing alone.

  • Mill — good and not expensive: it only needs one extra Field beyond what you're already running to pay off, and the more Fields you own beyond that, the lower its effective Grain/Flax cost gets. Combines with Smithy and the Farmer action.
  • Weaving Mill — good, arguably held back only by its cost design. Its benefits add up to a large VP total, but it costs 10 Flax or 10 Wool, which directly conflicts with a clothing strategy that wants those same resources for itself — its main reward (Weaving Looms) only synergizes with either Wool Weaver or Linen Weaver, never both at once, so it can undercut the very engine it's meant to support.
  • Textile House — okay: second-highest raw VP in the tier, but paying for it in clothing-material resources feels wrong if you're actually running a clothing strategy, since it drains the pipeline it should be complementing. It fits much more comfortably alongside a Vehicles strategy instead, where the clothing-resource cost isn't competing with anything.
  • Saddlery — good: removes the need for a Peat Cutter action. Pricier than most in this tier, but pays for itself once you can upgrade it toward Fleshing Beams. Combines with Joinery.
  • Joinery — good and not overly expensive since Grain is usually easy to get, but it needs a specific strategy — Peat Boats and/or Plows — to actually pay off. Combines with Smithy and Saddlery.
  • Waterfront House — very good, the highest raw VP of the Orange tier: it removes the need for Dike Builder/Warden actions and nearly completes your Fish Trap tools in one move. Its cost is Food-heavy, but a good chunk of what it returns can be spent straight back on an Inn.

None of these is a bad building if you can afford it, but none is a must-take either — the deciding factor every time is whether the specific strategy it wants is one you're already running. Weaving Mill and Textile House in particular are worth weighing carefully against a clothing strategy: their cost can eat the very resources that strategy needs, so the immediate one-time payout has to be worth temporarily draining your own pipeline.

Inns (Blue)

A big structural advantage over other tiers: Inns don't require upgraded building materials to construct, just base resources or goods, yet they still deliver strong benefits, good synergy, and plenty of VP. That combination of low material cost and high payout is why this tier reads as uniformly strong — build at least one or two whenever they're on the board.

  • Potter's Inn — okay: it provides animals of any type, smoothing out uneven animal stocks. Rarely worth building on its own — other Inns are simply better picks — and it only really shines with several Pottery Wheel upgrades already in place. Combines with Cooperage.
  • Farmer's Inn — very good despite having the lowest raw VP in the tier, because it delivers 3 Forests, which is a genuinely useful resource conversion. The real cost is that it needs plowed fields to compensate for what you're giving up. Combines with Smithy and the Farmer action.
  • Junk Dealer's Inn — very good: substantial VP from its benefits, plus a choice of utility between a Peat Boat and a Hand Cart.
  • Gulf House Inn — very good: free Bricks and Timber add up fast. It needs a specific, fairly expensive strategy (Stalls/Stables aren't cheap to build toward) to really shine, but if you have the Food for it, using it on the final turn to complete a Large Building is a genuine highlight play. Combines with the Carpenter action.
  • Milk House Inn — very good: removes the need for a Peat Cutter action, and doubles as a way to equalize your animal stock counts and clear remaining Peat from Moors in the same move. Combines with Cooperage.
  • Sluice Yard Inn — good: removes the need for Wood Cutter/Trader plus Farmer actions, and can meaningfully boost Wood income. Combines with Smokehouse or Waterfront House.

Potter's Inn is the one soft spot; every other Inn is strong enough that owning two or more across a game is close to free value rather than a real tradeoff — this is the tier to be least stingy with.

Large Buildings (Red) — solo-specific guidance

Hugely expensive, so they need to be worth plenty of VP and deliver excellent benefits to justify the cost — and in solo they typically only get built in the last round, once the resources have finally accumulated. Building even one Red building in a game is common; two is a stretch goal; building all three in a single solo game is rare enough that even experienced players report never having pulled it off.

There's a genuine open question about how strong Red buildings really are relative to their cost. One read is that they're worth it regardless of when you get them, since tool upgrades and free tile flips hold their value right up to final scoring. A separate, more speculative observation from watching who tends to win is that the top scorer in a group typically holds 2 of the 3 Red buildings — read as anecdotal evidence they're unusually strong engines, though that pattern comes with no real statistical backing behind it. Treat Red buildings as strong when you can afford them without warping your action economy to chase them, not as a mandatory pillar of every plan.

  • Village Church (grants a Carriage) — the weakest of the three, and specifically timing-sensitive: a Carriage is a genuinely valuable, useful vehicle, but its utility is diminished if you only obtain it in the final round, since there's no time left to actually use it. The typical solo pattern of building Red buildings last actively works against this one. One counter-read worth weighing: in a build that isn't prioritizing tools, taking Village Church earlier — well before the final round — can deliver a large point swing (15+3 VP) plus real vehicle utility across the remaining rounds, arguably outweighing the minor tool upgrades the Castles offer. If you can afford it early and aren't leaning on tool upgrades, it's worth reconsidering the "always last" default.
  • Lütestburg Castle (free Forest + move a Spade/Pottery Wheel) — very good: its tool upgrades combine well with the Master action, and a free Forest is always welcome, even when picked up in the last round, since you can still upgrade it via the Warden action or spend the wood to pay an end-of-season cost.
  • Berum Castle (move an Oven/Loom + free tile flip) — very good: tool upgrades again combine well with the Master action, and the Warden-action synergy is always useful, especially in the last round where upgrading a Forest or Stall for extra VP is pure value with no downside.

Once you weigh their build costs against what they return, it's hard to call any of the three anything but strong — the real decision is sequencing, not whether to build them. Village Church specifically rewards being reconsidered outside the default last-round slot if your build can use a Carriage early; the two Castles lose nothing by waiting.

Balance vs. specialize — an open question, weight accordingly

Whether to spread building investment evenly across VP categories or concentrate hard into one or two is a genuinely unresolved question, not one with a clean answer. Some of the strongest scores on record came from games played with a deliberately balanced approach across all VP areas rather than a specialized one — enough to raise real doubt about whether specializing is actually the stronger route, even though it's the more common instinct.

The risk on the specialized side is concrete: leaning hard into one or two VP routes through buildings alone, turn after turn, can produce serious analysis-paralysis, and one-off ability buildings — Blue buildings especially — often don't generate enough repeated value to justify the action-economy cost of chasing them. A single-use effect, however good, can't compete with a route that pays off every round.

Two specific combos are worth testing if you want to push past an average solo score: a textile strategy built around the full wool-to-cloth conversion chain, and a heavier animal-husbandry focus than most builds attempt. Both are flagged as promising but under-explored — avenues worth deliberately trying rather than assuming they're already priced into the buildings above.

Land & Resources

Home board tiles are permanent — plan placement ahead

Built tiles on your home board — fields, pastures, stalls, stables, buildings — cannot be voluntarily demolished or returned to the supply to free up space. That's a rule that catches new players by surprise, since loose pieces (animals, vehicles, equipment) can be returned to the supply anytime; the general "return anything to the supply" convenience simply doesn't extend to anything you've built onto your board. Once a tile is down, it's down for the rest of the game.

Only three narrow exceptions ever remove a tile from your home board once built, and none of them are a general "make room" tool: a Moor tile comes off once it's been fully dehydrated and cut (the normal end of moor processing, not a space-clearing action), a single Moor tile can be removed by visiting Bremen, and a single field can be removed by visiting Hage. Outside those three specific cases, the only way to open new space is expanding your board outward via your dikes. Treat every build placement as a permanent commitment: a suboptimal tile position can't be undone later, only worked around at the cost of further actions — plan the shape of your board ahead of time rather than placing opportunistically.

Advancing dikes

Since built tiles can't be removed to make room, dikes are effectively your only general-purpose way to open new home-board space. Push them forward when space becomes your binding constraint, but don't treat dike-advancing as free — it still costs a worker action, so weigh it against whatever else that action could have done.

Moors: dehydrate, then cut, then (eventually) clear

Moor tiles follow a fixed processing sequence, and the flip that starts it is the same free-flip mechanic used to upgrade vehicles: a Warden, Dike Warden, or (per the game's own reference card) equivalent flip action drains the moor by flipping the tile and placing peat cubes on it. From there you cut peat off the tile over one or more actions, and only once the tile is fully cut can it be removed from your board entirely — one of the three narrow exceptions to the no-removal rule above. Because dehydrating and clearing are separate steps years apart in practice, don't expect to reclaim moor land quickly; budget it as a multi-action project, not a one-time flip.

Flipping tiles: build-side-choice vs. free-flip are different mechanisms

Vehicles and tools print a cost on both the cheap front side and the upgraded back side, and there are exactly two distinct paths to owning the upgraded version — they are not the same action at different prices. The first path is a free flip: only the Warden action and the Dike Warden action (not Dike Builder — that action does not grant a flip) let you flip a tile you already own to its upgraded back side at no cost, marked on the action spaces by the curly/circular arrow symbol; Berum Castle grants this same flip ability as a building effect. The second path is building the upgraded side outright: when you take the vehicle- or peat-boat-build action, you're free to pay the higher printed back-side cost immediately and build the upgraded version directly, skipping the cheap front side entirely — you are never required to buy the cheap side first.

These two paths are mechanically distinct, not a discount on the same action: building the upgraded version directly is really returning the (unbuilt) tile and building a fresh one that happens to show its upgraded stats — it is not a "flip" in the Warden sense. And there is no hybrid: once you've built a tile at its cheap front-side cost, you cannot later pay the difference to top it up to the back side. The only route from an already-built cheap tile to its upgrade is a genuine flip action (Warden, Dike Warden, or Berum Castle) — there is no partial-payment shortcut.

The printed back-side cost matters mainly for that direct-build choice early on, when you have full freedom to build either side outright — not as a deferred upgrade price you pay down over time. Weigh the two paths as a real timing tradeoff: building the upgraded side immediately costs more resources up front (commonly a couple of extra wood) but saves you an action later, while building the cheap side now and flipping it later via a Warden-type action saves resources today but spends an extra action (or ties up a Warden/Dike Warden turn) down the line. Which is better depends on whether you can afford the resource cost now or would rather bank on a flip action being available when you need it.

This same free-flip mechanism is also what dehydrates a Moor tile — flipping it to place its peat cubes uses the identical curly-arrow flip action as upgrading a vehicle, not a separate moor-specific mechanic. Keep that in mind when deciding what to spend a Warden or Dike Warden turn on: it's competing to do double duty between your vehicle upgrades and your moor-draining schedule.

Fields, forests, and the crop-rotation gap

Fields and animals live in genuinely separate systems here — there's no in-game mechanism linking a field of grain to the animals sharing your board, no bonus for grazing sheep near your wheat, nothing resembling real-world crop rotation. That's worth stating plainly because the two feel like they should interact thematically, and new players sometimes hold off on committing to animals or fields while waiting to find the synergy between them. There isn't one to find. Each pays off on its own terms: a Field returns Grain or Flax every harvest it's active for, a Forest returns Wood whenever you cut it, and neither cares what's sitting in your stalls.

The upshot is that you can plan fields and forests purely around their direct output — what you need Grain, Flax, and Wood for in your building and goods pipeline — without budgeting any part of that plan around feeding animals or worrying that an idle field is "wasting" potential animal synergy. Animal-heavy builds don't actually need a large standing Grain surplus to stay safe, either: food and grain both trend toward abundance by the mid-game once your town-trading options are running, so a competent economy outgrows any real feeding bottleneck without dedicated infrastructure for it. Treat Field and Forest tiles as their own independent income lines, sized to what your building plan consumes, rather than as support pieces for an animal strategy that doesn't need the support.

Animals & Food

Solo framing: every animal decision below is measured against your own board and your own nine half-years, not against an opponent's herd.

The end-game animal-balance scoring trick

Animal scoring is tiered by count, and the tiers punish the obvious play. The type you have the fewest of scores 2 points per animal, your second-most-numerous type scores 1 point per animal, and whichever type you've stacked highest scores 0 points per animal. Pour everything into one big sheep herd and that entire herd — the thing you spent the game building — is worth nothing at the final count. Keeping sheep, cattle, and pigs roughly level in number is worth dramatically more than letting any one type run away, because it keeps every animal you own scoring at the top or middle tier instead of dumping your biggest investment into the zero-point bucket.

Nothing about breeding does this for you automatically — animal pairs reproduce on their own schedule regardless of what ratio you'd prefer, so an unmanaged herd drifts toward imbalance by default, usually toward whichever type you started earliest or fed most attention. Staying balanced takes active management: extra stall or stable capacity so you have somewhere to house three separate herds instead of one large one, and a release valve for bleeding off the type that's pulling ahead — slaughtering surplus animals or delivering them off your board through a trading action. Treat that capacity and that release valve as a real infrastructure cost you plan for, not a side effect of playing normally.

None of this means animal goods aren't worth pursuing along the way — manufacturing and delivering the byproducts (wool, milk, and the rest) stays good, easy value throughout the game regardless of how the endgame count shakes out. The balancing act is specifically about herd counts at the final tally; keep working the goods chains off whatever animals you have on the board.

Food is a soft tax, not a threat — but plan a real source early

Compared to the starvation-style pressure in other worker-placement designs, food here behaves more like a running tax than a real danger — it's there to keep the economy honest, not to threaten the game. That's not a reason to ignore it. Lock in one dependable food source early and let it run in the background for the rest of the game rather than letting it become a recurring decision point.

The bigger risk isn't starving — it's wasting actions patching food together turn by turn instead of committing to a single engine early and moving on to everything else the board offers.

Prioritize productive assets over one-off goals

The strongest complaint about high-scoring but stalled games is chasing one-off ability buildings — single-use effects, often the buildings that only ever pay out once — instead of routes that keep paying every remaining half-year. A single-use building competes for the same actions and resources as a generator that will fire nine, ten, eleven more times before the game ends, and it simply can't win that comparison on total value, even if its one-time effect looks strong in isolation.

Animal pairs, and the stalls/stables housing them, belong squarely in the "generative" category — every remaining half-year they're in play is another chance at offspring and another round of goods production. Whether balanced-scoring or volume-scoring ends up mattering more for your particular game, the underlying question of where to spend actions has a clear answer: favor the building or animal that produces repeatedly over the one that produces once, and get your generative pieces onto the board as early as you can so they have more half-years left to compound.

Uptrade the non-pregnant half of a breeding pair

Don't let a breeding pair sit in the stall doing nothing beyond reproducing. If one half of the pair isn't the one currently driving the pregnancy, that animal is available capacity — use it, convert it, or otherwise put it to work rather than parking it as dead weight next to its partner. The same logic that favors productive, repeatedly-paying assets over one-off ones applies inside the stall itself: an idle animal earns nothing extra just by existing, so any action that gets value out of it while the pair keeps reproducing is close to free.

Balance vs. specialize is still an open question. A balanced spread across scoring areas — not leaning hard into one or two routes — has outscored more specialized, heavily-optimized attempts in practice, even when the specialized games came from more experienced play. Whether animal husbandry specifically can be pushed hard enough to beat a balanced approach is untested territory: heavy livestock focus is a plausible high-scoring avenue that simply hasn't been proven out yet. Until it is, the safer default is the one this section leads with — balance your herds — while treating an aggressive animal-volume build as a real but unproven alternative worth experimenting with in a future game rather than assuming it's already the stronger line.

Vehicles & Trade

Vehicles don't just haul goods — they're the standing engine that turns raw resources into points across the whole midgame. Get one running early, keep it fed, and let it do quiet, repeatable work every half-year rather than treating it as a one-off purchase.

Vehicles are the actual engine, not a convenience

Sending goods to market and making deliveries through a vehicle is high-value action, and the faster raw goods and resources get bled off toward town, the healthier your overall food economy stays. That's the core reason a vehicle isn't a nice-to-have accessory — it's the thing that keeps your resource production from backing up and going stale.

By game end, aim for 2-3 vehicles. Their job is flipping raw goods into made goods, and most of those flips are worth a 100-125% increase in point value over the raw good. That's a huge efficiency jump for what is, mechanically, a very cheap and repeatable action — which is exactly why it deserves to be planned around rather than fit in opportunistically.

Build cheap, flip free — vehicle timing

Have a vehicle built by the end of your first winter. Don't pay for the upgraded cart up front — start with the cheaper 3-space cart (5 Wood plus a Horse), then flip it to the 4-space version the following summer. You get the same end state for less up-front cost, just staggered across two seasons instead of paid for all at once.

The logic scales: get the cheap version of your engine pieces running as early as possible, then upgrade them for free (or cheaply) once the timing lets you, rather than delaying your first action while you save up for the "real" version. An early, small vehicle earning value for several seasons beats a late, large vehicle that only starts contributing near the end.

Peat boats: the fungibility play

Owning one or two Peat Boats lets you use peat as a universal patch for your economy. Peat trades 1:1 for most resources, so instead of being locked into whatever peat specifically gets used for, it becomes flexible currency that fills whatever gap your economy happens to be short on that turn.

That flexibility is the entire value proposition — a Peat Boat isn't about wanting more peat for its own sake, it's about not being stuck when your plan calls for a resource you don't have on hand. Treat peat production as feeding this fungibility play rather than as a resource with a single fixed destination.

Manufacturing: specialize, don't spread

Pick either animal-based or plant-based goods chains as your primary manufacturing focus, and only do the other in a lesser capacity — just enough to cover deliveries and building costs. Trying to run both goods chains as equal priorities dilutes the actions and vehicle capacity that a focused chain would otherwise convert efficiently. Target roughly 25+ points banked in finished goods by game end as a rough benchmark for whether your manufacturing focus is pulling its weight.

This pairs with not spreading thin on buildings generally — pick a couple of buildings to invest in deeply rather than a little in many, and lean on green buildings where possible, since they function as rule-breaking free actions in a pinch.

Destinations: a food tool, not a scoring engine in themselves

The research available on Destinations specifically is thin, but the throughline from the vehicle-timing and manufacturing advice above still applies: a vehicle's value comes from the goods-upgrade and delivery loop it enables, not from any single visit or trip in isolation. Treat Destination trips as part of keeping that delivery engine running smoothly rather than as a separate scoring path to chase on its own.

Endgame

The last handful of half-years plays by different rules than the rest of the game — your engine is mostly built, your resource base is mostly fixed, and the puzzle shifts from growing your economy to spending down what you already have as efficiently as possible before the clock runs out.

The last few half-years are triage, not accumulation

By the final rounds, the number of buildings and plays still open to you almost always exceeds the actions and resources left to execute them. That's the core shape of the endgame puzzle: it isn't about finding "the" correct move, it's prioritizing under a hard action-scarcity constraint. Expect to look at your board and see several genuinely good build packages available at once — the skill is picking which ones to finish, not discovering a single best line, and accepting that some good options simply won't fit.

A useful sequencing principle for the last few rounds: bank what you've already accumulated first, secure whatever resource everything else depends on second, then spend down in bottleneck order. Concretely, that means leading with a building that "cashes out" goods already sitting on your track — since piling up more raw goods has no further use this late — before moving to a building that shores up your food supply so it can fund the rest of the sequence, and only then chaining the remaining builds in whatever order your tightest resource allows. This logic transfers to any board state: look at what's already stockpiled with nowhere else to go, lock in the resource that gates the rest of your plan, then work outward from there rather than building whatever looks individually strongest.

Patience is also a legitimate line at this stage. Rather than rushing a big combo in the first of your remaining rounds, spreading construction evenly across the last few turns can be just as sound as a single concentrated push — there's no universal answer to "rush now vs. spread out," only whichever sequencing lets you actually pay for and place everything you're committing to.

Brick and cart space are the real bottlenecks, not food or buildings

When you try to push an all-out building spree in the endgame, the wall you hit usually isn't the buildings' own resource costs or your food supply — it's brick and hauling capacity. Brick specifically needs a Clay Worker plus a Peat-fueled upgrade processed before winter ends to come online in time, which makes it easy to under-plan: if that upgrade chain isn't already running by the time you enter your last few rounds, brick becomes the ceiling on how many buildings you can actually complete, regardless of what else is available.

Separately — and this constraint is independent of brick or any other resource — loading space in your carts and vehicles caps how much you can haul in a given trip. Even holding all the resources a building needs, if you can't move them where they're needed in the actions remaining, the building doesn't get finished. This is arguably the single most binding restriction on late-game building volume: it doesn't matter how rich your stockpile is if you don't have the cart capacity to deliver it. When planning an endgame "build everything" push, reverse-engineer the sequence from brick supply and cart capacity first, then slot buildings in around those two limits — not the other way around. Food, by contrast, is rarely the actual constraint late; expect to sacrifice some food to afford a building spree and treat that as an acceptable trade, since food is comparatively easy to patch back later or simply live without for the few remaining rounds.

Small actions add up when the big ones are exhausted

Once the headline building plays are locked in, the remaining points come from small, easy-to-forget conversions rather than another big swing. Litter Storage (trading Flax for an extra Horse once you already own enough Horses to qualify) is a good example: it's a low-opportunity-cost action that slots neatly into an already-packed sequence, but it's exactly the kind of move that gets overlooked in the heat of planning a dense final round — worth deliberately checking for on every endgame pass rather than assuming you'd naturally remember it.

Animal breeding is another source of "free" late value: it triggers passively alongside your formal remaining actions, so it should be counted as part of your endgame resource plan rather than treated as background noise you don't need to track. And in the very last round specifically, sweep your hand for loose unplaced resources — anything still sitting uncommitted that could be dumped onto a vehicle or an open board space for points is value left on the table if you don't spend the action to place it before the game ends.

There's also a genuine tradeoff worth weighing late: if you're deciding between banking extra animals (say, an extra Cow to hit a goods-track threshold) versus keeping a different animal type (Horses) for their utility, recognize that some conversions are recoverable later via other buildings and some aren't — check whether your board gives you a way to rebuild the animal type you're trading away before you commit to the swap.

Depot vs. Stable: a genuine fork, not a stacking choice

A Depot doubles the value of points on your home board's Goods Track at endgame scoring — a strong payoff for a build that's been feeding that track all game, especially one paired with a Peat Boat that keeps converting Peat into goods on your home board. But converting a Stall into a Depot instead of a Stable is a fork, not an upgrade you can stack: that Stall's alternate path — a Stable, useful for animal-balance or volume-breeding plays — is mutually exclusive with the Depot conversion on the same tile. Decide which direction a given Stall is going before you commit its upgrade, because you don't get both from one tile.

This decision isn't automatically correct just because doubling sounds powerful, either. The alternative approach — spending goods efficiently as you produce them rather than stockpiling for a doubling bonus at the end — is an equally valid strategy, since a doubled score only helps if you actually have a large pile of goods sitting unused at game end; if your build tends to consume goods as it goes rather than hoard them, a Depot's bonus has little to double and a Stable (or simply not making the conversion at all) is the better use of that Stall. Decide which of the two your board is actually trending toward — accumulation or throughput — before locking in the fork.

Balance vs. specialize — still an open question, weight accordingly

Whether to balance across many scoring categories or specialize hard into one or two carries into the endgame specifically, and the honest answer is that it's still unsettled. There's real disagreement here worth holding as genuine tension rather than resolving into a false consensus: some experienced solo play favors big, efficient buildings and a small number of reinforcing focuses — running a steady animal economy (keeping sheep and cattle around for repeat food, wool, and continued breeding, rather than cashing them out) alongside chasing efficient point buildings, while treating actions spent chasing scattered one-off bonuses as generally poor value. Other successful endgames lean the opposite way, deliberately avoiding investment in any one big infrastructure category — for instance, treating heavy dike-building as a low priority late unless you're genuinely out of board space for buildings, since dike actions rarely generate points as directly as a well-timed building does.

What both approaches agree on: the endgame rewards actions that generate large points per action spent, not investment in incremental setup whose payoff won't arrive before the game ends. If you're specializing, specialize into a build that keeps scoring across several categories as a byproduct of one running engine (animals plus goods-track plus building value together, for instance) rather than into isolated bonuses that only pay once. If you're balancing, make sure each category you're touching in the endgame is still earning points per action at least as well as a more focused alternative would — balance for its own sake, at the cost of per-action efficiency in the final rounds, isn't obviously better just because it's diversified.

Tea & Trade Expansion

Fields of Arle, like most Uwe Rosenberg designs, is balanced so every action is roughly equal in isolation — real power comes from chaining actions into a coherent engine, not from any single strong play. Tea & Trade's new mechanics mostly exist to patch two base-game weak links (farming, and wool/leather), not to replace that underlying framework.

Ditches: what they actually unlock (and why they're still overrated)

Ditches double plow effectiveness, which finally makes a dedicated farming/plowing strategy viable — before this expansion, plowing more than once in a game rarely paid off. They're also meant to enable large-scale sheep breeding (and by extension a slaughterer/butcher/leather line) without the colonist-based turf wars that made that strategy awkward to pursue competitively in the base game. Both fixes work by creating a payoff for dehydrated (drained) land, which the base game never gave you enough of.

That said, getting the sheep/leather line to actually pay off is harder than it sounds. Simply breeding 2 sheep early to multiply fast, then slaughtering a couple once you reach 6, is a sound outline — but several players who tried exactly that found the strategy's actual payoff elusive: the breeding and slaughtering steps are easy to execute, but converting the surplus into points (leather, then further processing) is where the plan tends to stall. Treat "get sheep, slaughter for leather" as necessary but not sufficient — the sequencing and conversion end of the chain needs its own plan, not just an assumption that leather will fall out the other end.

Ditches also carry real rules subtlety worth knowing before you invest turns in them. The core rule: a land space counts as dehydrated once it's bordered by at least 2 ditches. But a large Moor tile spans 2 land spaces, and it's tempting to place ditches in a "shared corner" arrangement — one ditch on top of a moor, one running down along the border it shares with an adjacent moor tile — hoping to flip both tiles cheaply. That doesn't work: the moor tile only flips once at least one of its underlying land spaces independently has 2 ditches bordering it alone. A ditch arrangement that splits its coverage across 2 tiles without concentrating 2 ditches on one single space wastes those ditches — you may still flip the tile (revealing peat), but the land underneath won't count as dehydrated for sheep-holding or the plow bonus. An L-shaped placement of 2 ditches wrapped around one single large moor tile is legitimate and does dehydrate it; splitting that L across 2 separate moor tiles is not.

Boats are the expansion's real power spike

Trading with Finland is one of the expansion's most underrated boat actions — evaluate it case-by-case rather than dismissing it by its raw point math. Loading a trading boat with a winter-wear good (e.g. a wool coat) nets you timber directly — up to 3 timber per coat traded. On paper that looks like a net loss of roughly 1 point, since 3 timber is worth less at game-end than the coat you gave up. But the trade delivers that timber without spending a separate gather action and without occupying cargo space in another vehicle, which stays free for other use. Once you account for what timber actually lets you build and do — not just its printed end-game point value — the real cost is closer to trading a coat for wood and then converting that wood elsewhere, which nets out to a strong rate (roughly 2.5 VP for 3 wood) if you're short on forests or woodcutting capacity. The lesson generalizes: judging a trade purely by its scoresheet point delta undervalues resources whose worth is mostly in what they unlock, not what they're worth at the end.

Trading boats loaded with tea are also the engine behind the expansion's peat/tea combo lines (see the worked example below) — a boat trip that both turns over resources and feeds East Frisian Tea into your stockpile is consistently one of the strongest uses of an early action.

Grandma's double-turn combo

Grandmother's action grants 1 East Frisian Tea (EFT) plus 4 food per visit, and paying 1 EFT lets you take the action a second time in the same visit — a double turn, which is valuable because actions, not raw resources, are the genuinely scarce thing in this game. There's a specific timing rule worth knowing before you try to chain this: you cannot fund the second execution using tea the first execution just produced. EFT for a double action must be committed before the visit resolves — at the moment you place your worker, not after seeing the first execution's output. This same "pay upfront" timing applies anywhere else the double-action/EFT-spend interaction shows up, such as building the Tea Factory.

The correct way to run it: arrive already holding at least 2 EFT, commit both upfront to take Grandmother twice, then resolve both executions. Net result of a double visit funded this way: 8 food total, and you end the visit holding the same 2 EFT you started with, since each execution refunds 1 EFT as part of its own output. Practical takeaway: never spend your tea stockpile down to zero before visiting Grandmother — walking in holding tea is what makes the double-action loop self-sustaining rather than a one-time spend.

Round-count sensitivity: shorter games favor fewer conversion steps

The standard 3-player game runs 7 rounds, versus 9 for 2-player — and that shorter round count makes some strategies structurally harder to pull off. Multi-step conversion chains (breed wool/leather, then process it, then sell it) need enough rounds to pay off more than once to be worth the setup investment, and several players report simply not being able to make a wool/leather production-and-transformation strategy work within 7 rounds — the engine takes too long to spin up relative to how few rounds remain to cash it in. Forests suffer the same problem: their value is in compounding long-term wood generation, and a shorter game gives that compounding less time to matter.

If you're finding these deeper conversion strategies keep falling short in 3-player games, that's a real structural constraint, not just execution error — some players extend 3-player games to the full 9 rounds (matching the 2-player length) specifically to give wool/leather and forest strategies room to develop. Absent that house rule, the practical adjustment is to favor strategies with fewer conversion steps, or ones that start paying off immediately, over deep processing chains that need several rounds just to reach their first payout.

A worked example: moor-draining feeds peat feeds trade

One strong early-game line pairs heavy ditch (Schloote) construction with Frisian-tea-boosted peat cutting: build Schloote toward and across your moors early, turning them over to drain them, then spend East Frisian Tea to cut peat from the newly-drained land in bulk. This does double duty — draining moors frees fields for sheep, wheat, or flax, and simultaneously unlocks a peat surplus you can trade toward whatever a target building needs (a mill, a Waterkant house, and so on).

One version of the opening: spend round 1 on a Tools or Workbench action to boost the Schloote/peat-cutting tracks, then build several Schloote inward to turn over 1–2 moors; round 2 brings a trading boat loaded with tea (2 tea, or 1 tea plus a second pulled from Grandmother) to fund a big peat-cutting action off the newly-drained land; round 3 repeats the Schloote push to finish draining the remaining moors while investing stockpiled tea to cut peat in bulk. By round 3 this can have several drained fields available for sheep or wheat/flax, plus a peat surplus useful for trading toward whichever building you're chasing next — one player used exactly this peat surplus to afford a wool-clothing building, then converted that clothing via a peat-boat trade into the food needed to unlock a further building that itself yielded clay and turned over a final moor.

The named tradeoff: this line runs wood- and vehicle-poor early, since nearly every early action goes into ditches, peat, and tea rather than wood-gathering or transport — it needs a deliberate plan to backfill wood access later (a Carriage can substitute for the trading boat otherwise used to fetch tea, freeing the boat for other trade). Even an imperfectly-sequenced first attempt at this line has scored competitively (130+ points), which suggests the core idea — moor-draining feeds peat, peat feeds trade — is robust to execution mistakes rather than a narrow combo that only works when run exactly right.
