The Riftbreaker

Base layout, power chains, defence design and mech loadouts — building a colony on Galatea 37 that survives what the planet sends at it.

The Clock

The waves that kill you are not random and they are not purely a function of time. Most of what makes them stronger is something you did — an HQ upgrade, a finished mission, a map you chose to settle. Once you know which of your own actions feed the difficulty, you stop being a passenger on the escalation curve and start setting its pace.

Three separate dials, three separate causes

Wave pressure is not one number. It is at least three, and they respond to different inputs — which is why players comparing notes so often talk past each other.

Enemy level — how tough each individual creature is — tracks mostly the in-game day count, modified by your difficulty setting and slightly by map threat. It ticks whether or not you are doing anything useful. Notoriously, it keeps ticking during the story segment where attacks are switched off and you can roam freely: that "safe" stretch is still advancing your scaling clock, so parking there to farm research means the next hostile map greets you with creatures several tiers above what your defences were built for.

Wave size — how many creatures show up — tracks the threat rating of the map you are standing on, visible on the orbital panel. Threat is fixed when the map is generated and does not drift during play. This is the dial you control by choosing where to build.

Wave frequency — how often they arrive — tracks your difficulty setting, the biome, and enemy strength. Every biome has its own guaranteed minimum gap between waves, so two runs on the same difficulty in different biomes genuinely do feel differently paced.

Scaling has a ceiling

Enemy scaling caps out; it does not climb forever. Brutal simply sprints toward that cap instead of walking. Practically, this means a run that survives its own mid-game crisis tends to get easier, not harder — the economy keeps compounding after the threat stops.

The HQ upgrade is a deliberate aggro button

Upgrading the headquarters fires an extra, stronger wave roughly two minutes later, on top of the normal cadence. It is the single most controllable escalation event in the game, and treating it as a free "power up" is how early runs die.

The corollary is a tempo strategy that trivialises even extreme custom multipliers: waves scale hard when you advance, so bank everything you can while your HQ level stays low. Mines on every deposit, storage, power, research — all of it grows your side of the curve without touching the enemy's. Players running Brutal at 500 % wave multipliers describe the game as almost trivial once they internalise this, because the enemy is fighting the base you had two days ago while you are fighting with the base you have now.

When you do upgrade, stack the cost. Wait for a wave warning, then start the HQ upgrade just before that countdown expires so the upgrade wave merges into the wave you were already going to fight, rather than paying for two separate fights. Have the follow-up building queued before the upgrade lands — the research or communications hub that the new tier unlocks should go down the instant it is available, because the wave that arrives is the price you have already paid.

Custom survival: warmup dies with your first upgrade

A long warmup timer only holds while HQ stays at level zero. Upgrade during warmup and waves immediately begin arriving at your configured interval, with the remaining warmup discarded — set a two-hour warmup and a ten-minute interval, upgrade at minute fifteen, and you get attacked every ten minutes from then on. Reported as biome-dependent and acknowledged by the developers as probably a bug, but plan around it: if you bought warmup time to build a fortress, spend it all before you touch the HQ.

Progress checkpoints, not just clocks

In campaign, completing resource missions raises wave difficulty as surely as the calendar does, because each completed mission unlocks new materials, which unlock better towers, weapons and armoury tiers. The game scales to your unlocked capability. That is why rushing objectives on a first playthrough produces the classic death spiral — thousands of enemies arriving while you are still rebuilding from the last wave — and why the fix is counter-intuitive: slow down, bank resources, and get genuinely comfortable with your current defensive tech before you unlock the next tier.

The same logic explains the notorious mission-completion spike. On a mining outpost, finishing the objective (raising the rare-ore factory, the uranium centrifuges) summons a wave with tanky units mixed in, far nastier than that map's routine attacks. Build the objective when you are ready to fight, not when it becomes affordable. The cobalt outpost mission is the one commonly cited exception with no completion wave.

Attacks only happen where you are

Maps you are not standing on are never attacked, but they keep mining, keep producing and keep researching. This one rule reshapes the whole campaign.

It means an overwhelmed main base can be saved by leaving it. Rift out to another map, build there, let the waves follow you, and your HQ rebuilds and refills unmolested. It also removes the rebuild-under-fire treadmill: hop away while materials accumulate, hop back, and place everything in one uninterrupted building session instead of racing a countdown.

Taken to its logical end this becomes an exploit — park your economy on maps you rarely visit and they are simply never raided. Some resource-concentration maps go further: on several of them attacks do not spawn at all until you build the mission structure, so the entire map can be stripped wave-free if you never trigger it. Whether you use that is a taste question, but knowing it exists tells you why "just build more turrets everywhere" is the wrong instinct — your exposure is a function of where you stand, not what you own.

Two hard limits shape the plan. You may hold a maximum of eight remote outposts, and once you leave a mission map you cannot return unless you left an outpost there. So pick your permanent farm maps deliberately while you are still on them.

Everything targets the closest thing

Creatures path to the nearest structure or to your mech, and waves pick their entry point relative to what is closest to the map border. That is a targeting rule you can build around, and most good defensive layouts are really just applications of it: outlying bait clusters placed nearer the map edge than your real base will take the wave instead; scattered small outposts split a single big wave into several small ones; a lone wall segment far from anything peels off a slice of the swarm to chew on it.

Your mech is subject to the same rule in reverse — while you are out in the open a large part of the herd will follow you, which is exactly what you want when your towers need a clean line on the rest.

Nests are a second, separate spawn source

Creature nests generate their own attacks on their own timer, independent of the perimeter waves. Those attacks are noticeably weaker than a real wave, which is why clearing nests feels like it accomplishes nothing — it removes a minor local nuisance, not the main pressure.

Nests also respawn. Sweeping the map clean is wasted effort because it re-seeds, so the better play is to control where the pressure originates: leave one nest standing inside an existing kill zone and its recurring spawns become free kills and free drops instead of unpredictable trouble on an unwatched flank.

Nests are no longer passive targets, either. They now carry a health boost and one randomly rolled ability from the Omega set — direct-damage and buff abilities only, since walling you in would be self-defeating for something that wants its spawns to reach you. A nest can rain fireballs, throw lightning bursts, spawn drones, or heal neighbouring nests, and a careless approach genuinely kills mechs. The compensation is loot: cleared Omega nests can drop a fountain of resources, mods and unique items. Biome variants raise the stakes further — the metallic valley nest is a defended structure ringed with alien plasma and artillery towers, each of which pays resources when destroyed, and the fungal swamp nest spits acid on its own.

Read the announcement

In campaign, attacks are telegraphed with a direction marker before they land, and every defensive structure — including high-tier turrets — builds in under twenty seconds. That is enough time to raise a wall and a turret line into the announced lane, then sell it afterwards so it is not drawing power and AI cores between fights. Survival does not give you this: you learn the direction when the timer hits zero and get roughly a minute of small spawns before the real wave, which is enough to reinforce or to sell something exposed, not to build a fresh fort. Pre-built defence matters far more in survival for exactly this reason.

Recap

Enemy strength follows the calendar, wave size follows the map's threat rating, and wave frequency follows difficulty and biome. Your HQ level and your completed missions are the levers you actually control, so bank hard between advances and time each upgrade to collide with a wave you already expected. Maps you are away from are never raided, everything targets whatever is closest, and nests are a separate, respawning, now genuinely dangerous spawn source best left standing inside a kill zone.

Siting & Layout

Where you put things decides how much defending you have to do. Terrain that funnels attackers, a perimeter drawn before you need it, and buildings arranged so one splash hit does not take out four structures at once are all worth more than another row of turrets — and they are free.

Choosing the headquarters site

Early on, siting barely matters. The only thing driving the decision at that stage is proximity to carbonium and ironium, and that dependency ends the moment mining moves out to remote outposts. This is why relocating the HQ after a long scouting trip is usually a mistake: the starting location typically sits on carbonium nodes worth roughly fifty thousand each, where deposits elsewhere on the map commonly hold half that. You trade a large, guaranteed economy for a marginally prettier wall line.

The criteria that actually matter arrive in the mid game, and they are worth thinking about on day one even though you cannot exploit them yet. In rough order: access to mud and sludge, a roughly central position, and natural barriers that form chokepoints. Water is the resource that matters most over a full run — it feeds the largest energy and plasma production — so a site chosen for long-term liquid access beats one chosen for starting ore.

Centrality is not aesthetic. Long-range towers are paid for in engagement time, and every extra metre a wave has to cross from the map edge is another volley landed before contact. A base in the corner gives half its artillery nothing to shoot at.

When to reroll the map

Power is the stat everything else is downstream of: more power means more AI cores, which means more towers. Research labs in particular are voracious, with each tier roughly doubling the draw of the one before. A map with no mud or sludge within reach, or too few carbonium and ironium nodes, is a map that will strangle you in the mid game — restarting costs minutes, while playing it out costs hours. A commonly used floor is five carbonium and three ironium deposits inside the area you intend to hold. On Brutal specifically, rerolling until you find a cliff face or a big crevice near the start is worth the time: impassable terrain forces enemies spawning from several directions onto a handful of paths.

Draw the perimeter before you build inside it

At high wave multipliers the usual approach — expand outward to each new node, ring the production sprawl, then collapse the line back into something compact late — falls apart. Spawns arrive along an entire map edge, so a wave entering from the left hits your left defences and simultaneously wraps around to the top and bottom. There is no "the attack side" any more.

The answer is to decide the final defensive perimeter from scouting, before the HQ goes down, and to build inward from that decision. Aim to enclose at least three of each basic resource, with the remaining deposits close enough outside that you can sell those factories off before a wave reaches them and rebuild afterwards. Pause, look at what you have explored, and draw the border in your head — it is the single highest-leverage minute in the run.

The perimeter has to enclose more than you expect: a block of wind and solar with energy storage, a bank of AI hubs, sixteen to twenty solid material storages, tower ammo factories and ammo storage, communications hubs, and space near mud, sludge or geothermal for research labs. A perimeter that fits your day-two base will strangle your day-six one.

Use chokepoints against the monsters, not against yourself

The intuitive move — a short wall section plugging a narrow gap — often backfires, because that short section faces a wide open area on your side and everything that breaks through fans out into your base. Pull the wall back from the chokepoint instead, so attackers funnel down a narrow path and emerge into a long wall packed with turrets. The choke should shape their approach, not shorten your firing line.

Stop building one giant perimeter

The instinct carried over from other base-builders — one enormous wall with a safe interior — is the wrong shape for this game, and it fails for a specific reason. As the base grows, the wall grows with it, and the pathing will find one weak tile in an enormous run of it. You then either garrison turrets every few tiles at ruinous AI core cost, or spend every wave kiting to thin it out while stragglers chew on your storage.

Several smaller bases work better. Each is small enough to be covered entirely by repair towers, each carries a radar for early warning and to suppress nearby respawns, and each has a perimeter short enough to actually finish. Because attacks path to whatever is closest, the bases sitting nearer the map edge absorb the waves, and interior bases need little more than a simple wall that buys the seconds it takes you to teleport in.

The same logic applies to remote extractors. Do not run walls out to them; give each its own small walled pocket with a couple of turret types so it defends itself.

Firebases — bait as a design principle

The most efficient version of this is deliberate. Build defence clusters flung out well beyond the territory you are protecting, positioned so anything entering from the map edge is closer to the firebase than to your production. The wave flocks to the bait, and your economy is never touched. Placement toward the map edge does the defending — not wall count around the base.

A firebase is built tight, with no open interior and nothing power-generating inside it, so every tower's firing arc covers the whole approach. A workable build order runs: a power cable out from your production, terminating in a cluster of power storages so the towers keep firing if the line is ever cut; radar and a repair tower beside them; then a layer of artillery; then a layer of sentinels; then wall, gap, wall, gap, wall. In practice the cable is rarely cut because it runs toward the map interior, away from where enemies come from — the storage is insurance, not routine.

Those wall gaps matter more than they look. Many large enemies attack with area damage and would destroy several adjacent wall layers with a single hit; spacing the layers means one hit takes one layer.

Scale it as tech arrives: sentinels give way to railguns, artillery upgrades once uranium is available, and once titanium is online the innermost wall layer is replaced with minigun towers while a fresh outer wall layer is added to make up the loss. Four to eight such positions — the cardinal directions, or the corners and edge midpoints of the map — is a common shape. A dozen defence points, each occupying a three-by-three block of floor tiles, is what an extreme-multiplier campaign ends up with.

Concentric rings for the main base

Where you do want a single fortified core, build it as rings rather than a box: HQ and critical buildings in the centre, then successive wall rings with towers, with power generation and resource gathering built in the space between rings. When you expand outward, strip the turrets off the inner walls — they are no longer the contact line and their AI cores are needed further out.

Late in a run the move is often to shrink rather than grow. Downsize the main base, double the wall thickness, and line it one space back with repeating laser, railgun, missile and plasma towers plus repair and artillery, and the base holds on Brutal without your attention.

Repair coverage geometry

Repair towers are what let a small box survive at all, and their coverage is geometric rather than approximate — worth laying out properly rather than sprinkling.

In a line, a repair tower covers three tiles to either side of itself, so towers placed with a six-tile gap between them leave no holes and a line of them covers a twelve-tile-wide strip. Two such lines running parallel cover twenty-four tiles between them, which turns repair into a set of rails you build the base around.

For area coverage, an eleven-by-eleven grid with a tower at each cell centre gives complete overlap; a nine-by-nine grid is often preferred because it fits a five-by-five arrangement of two-tile buildings around each tower with a gap for readability, and each repair tower just reaches its neighbours so they repair each other. Placing them at the very edge of each other's range is arguably overkill against normal waves, but it is what carries a base through simultaneous raids, earthquakes and meteor strikes.

Keep at least one repair station inside the wall but within range of your forward bunker and minelayer positions, so front-line structures repair themselves between waves. And remember what repair towers do not do: they repair, they never rebuild. A flattened bunker still needs manual reconstruction, and repair drones are not a substitute for shields or walls.

Small placement habits that compound

  • Leave air gaps between buildings. Many creatures deal area damage, so packed structures take multiplied damage while spaced ones stay within what repair towers can keep up with.
  • Cluster only what is easy to protect. Wind and solar drag-build into neat blocks — put those blocks around a repair tower in hazard-prone areas.
  • Make power cables redundant or shield them. In high-risk areas a single severed connector takes the whole tower network offline; run a second path or tuck cables behind walls and other structures.
  • Floor in front of your walls speeds your own movement during an attack, which matters a lot when you are the mobile reserve — but flooring and minelaying conflict, so pick one per stretch.
  • Nestle against terrain. Rock cliffs and mud pools act as free wall segments, shorten the perimeter, and make attack paths predictable enough to concentrate defence on.
  • Reserve slack. Keeping spare power and around twenty free building slots, with AI control centres already standing, lets you drop a fresh turret line instantly on a direction you were not ready for.
  • Build outward in chunks. Taking one new area at a time and defending from there beats extending one enormous perimeter — and pairs naturally with using rock formations as kill corridors or as permanent blocks on a flank.

On pipeline missions the same principle applies in miniature: pick the route first, reinforce the flooring, extend walls along it, then lay pipe once the corridor is covered — rather than laying pipe and defending whatever line it happened to take.

Recap

Site the HQ for water and centrality rather than starting ore, and do not relocate off a rich starting node. Draw your final perimeter from scouting before you build, enclose enough space for the base you will have rather than the one you have, and pull walls back from chokepoints so attackers funnel into a long firing line. Prefer several small, repair-covered bases and outlying firebases over one enormous wall, since everything targets what is closest. Lay repair coverage on a deliberate grid, space buildings against area damage, and protect your cabling.

Power

Power is the stat every other system is downstream of. AI cores, and therefore towers, are bought with it; research labs escalate their draw with every tier; shields, heavy artillery and the rift charge all run on what your generators produce. A run that stalls is almost always a run that ran out of grid.

Power is local — resources are not

Ordinary resources pool globally: mine ironium anywhere and you can spend it anywhere. Power and liquids do not work that way. They exist only on the network they are physically connected to, and there is no teleporting them. Wireless transmission pylons help within a map and do nothing between maps.

The user interface tells you when you have made a mistake here: any network not joined to your main grid is labelled Local. Seeing that label in your main base means there is a break somewhere in the line — usually a connector an enemy chewed through.

Three exceptions are worth committing to memory, because they define what can be centralised and what cannot. Ammunition teleports, so factories can live anywhere. Animal biomass is global but must be picked up by hand, with no automated collection. And liquids can be moved between maps through compressors and decompressors, which is the loophole that makes the whole cross-map economy work — ship acid-biome gas out to run upgraded gas plants elsewhere, or ship water to a desert or cavern map so fusion becomes possible there.

Self-powered outposts beat long cables

Powerlines are fragile and a long run across contested ground is a liability you have to defend forever. Give each outpost its own small generation instead — wind, solar and storage at minimum — plus a portal so you can arrive instantly. The goal is a network of places that repair themselves and that you teleport between, not a web of infrastructure that needs walking maintenance. Where power genuinely cannot be generated locally, put cultivators there instead and export the plant biomass, which does travel.

Density, not output

The most common mid-game mistake is scaling wind and solar. They work, they cost nothing to run, and they are the correct opening — but scaling them to a real base means hundreds of buildings. The space consumed by enough wind and solar to make a thousand power is about the same as a single upgraded gas plant with a much smaller footprint, and the building count itself is a problem: mass structure spam strains the simulation and pushes the game into slow motion. If your late-game run is stuttering, the hundreds of turbines are a likely culprit.

They are also the only sources subject to the day-night cycle and weather. Solar operates for roughly thirteen hours of the day — around fifty-four percent uptime — and its advertised cost efficiency ignores the energy storage you must build to carry the night. Counting that storage, solar's real cost per delivered energy is roughly triple its headline figure, which drops it below wind for most purposes. Wind runs around the clock and suffers far fewer weather penalties; prefer it unless you are in a biome with a solar bonus or a wind malus.

Upgrading generators trades resource efficiency for space efficiency. Every source gets worse per resource spent as you upgrade it while getting better per tile occupied. That is the correct trade once your perimeter is fixed and your economy is running, and the wrong one while you are still cash-poor — which is why the answer to "should I upgrade my power" is nearly always "not yet, build another one" early and "yes, immediately" late.

Gas is the mid-game answer

Sludge into a filtering plant into a gas powerplant is the chain that carries most runs. Its virtue is that it scales cleanly: the whole system, support buildings included, roughly triples in space efficiency as you upgrade it, and each pumped sludge source goes vastly further — a level-one pump underwrites a few hundred energy, a level-three pump underwrites nearly three thousand.

Ratios matter more than raw counts here. At level one, two pumps feeding two filters feeding a single powerplant nets around five hundred energy for a modest resource outlay. At level two the same two pumps support three filters and three plants. At level three the ratio settles at roughly one pump to two filters to three plants — two pumps, four filters, six powerplants — for something close to six thousand net energy in a hundred and sixty-eight tiles. Pump capacity, not plant count, is the thing to design around.

Because of that, a sludge pool large enough for four pumps — or two pools close enough to share infrastructure — is a legitimate reason to move or expand your main base. Early on, treat sludge as power generation rather than as a water source: two pumps, two filters and a gas plant is around five hundred energy at a stage where that is transformative. The second tier of liquid handling improves it further, and notably pumps need no rare resources to reach level two, so more sludge converts directly into more power without gating.

Where a map has mud rather than sludge, the water filter into biocomposter route produces gas from plant matter instead. Composters need very little water each, so one filter can serve both a composter pair and a research lab. Two composters feed one gas plant for roughly five hundred energy, at the cost of a steady leaf supply — which is a good reason to swing your melee weapon at every plant you pass while exploring.

Fusion and what plasma is really for

Fusion is gated behind all three major rare metals and is the most expensive thing you will build, but you need it regardless of whether its energy alone justifies the cost, because plasma is a fusion by-product and plasma runs shield generators, feeds heavy artillery when supercharged, and is consumed by the rift charge at the end of the campaign. A run that never builds fusion is a run that discovers this at the worst possible moment.

It runs on water or on supercooling liquid, and the choice is an economic one: if your titanium income supports it — roughly four or more per second from a cultivator — supercoolant is the better feed, otherwise use water. One geothermal plant is enough to sustain a pair of fusion reactors, at which point energy stops being a constraint at all; an upgraded reactor produces enough to run an entire map.

Build cost is the barrier, and it is a soft one. Mech modules that reduce building cost stack multiplicatively enough that a full set takes construction down to around a third of list price, which makes a fusion buildout dramatically cheaper if you swap loadout before a building session. On a map with no water at all, an ionizer is the alternative route to shields.

Storage is a defensive building

Energy storage holds a large buffer, especially at the top tier, and one or two are enough to let a turret line run purely off stored charge and recharge slowly between waves. That decouples your peak defensive draw from your generation capacity, which is exactly the failure mode where towers fall silent halfway through a wave. Build storage before you build the next generator.

Matching power to the biome

Biome is the first input to the power decision, not an afterthought — a mismatched grid starves the defences no matter how good the towers are. Players who lose acid-biome runs to a specific wave often find the actual cause was a wind-heavy grid in a low-wind biome, and fix it by switching to solar and cultivated biomass.

  • Tropical and jungle — the generic case. Take geothermal where vents exist, sludge where it exists, and treat those as the only space-efficient consistent sources available.
  • Acidic — gas, unambiguously. The volume of sludge supports gas plants indefinitely, which is why keeping an acid map as a permanent power and production site is a standard campaign move.
  • Desert — solar is doubled here, but vents and sludge are scarce and water is scarce enough that nuclear and fusion belong elsewhere. Supplement with plant biomass, or import gas.
  • Volcanic — magma generators, with the fuel on site. Geothermal is the overlooked pick: doubled output here makes it a genuine powerhouse and it produces mud as a by-product.
  • Radioactive — nuclear to get running, then gas via decompressor, which is infinite and consumes nothing.
  • Metallic valley — morphium, which behaves like gas without the processing chain.
  • Caverns — wind is of limited use; plan on imported liquids.

Where the power actually goes

Two consumers deserve explicit budgeting. Alien research laboratories are enormous draws that escalate steeply with each tier, so reserve capacity for them deliberately — earmarking an entire gas plant for the lab rather than dumping new generation into AI hubs and towers is a sound habit. And rare-element miners draw far more than basic ones, which is why a remote rare-ore outpost usually wants a biomass generator rather than a couple of turbines.

The mirror-image mistake is cultivating a power-hungry resource too early. A cultivator's own draw can exceed what the operation returns if you start uranium farming before the economy supports it — though at the upgraded tiers the drain is modest enough that a uranium farm is comfortably net positive, and because cultivators produce plant biomass alongside their target resource, a farm burning its own biomass locally usually generates more power than it consumes.

Recap

Power and liquids are local; ammunition and resources are global; liquids alone cross maps, through compressors. Open with wind and storage, step up through geothermal to gas, and treat fusion as mandatory for plasma rather than optional for energy. Design gas around pump capacity, budget storage as part of solar's cost, and pick generation to match the biome rather than habit. Build energy storage as a defensive structure, keep cables redundant, and reserve headroom for research labs before they arrive.

Economy

Running out of ore is not a balance problem, it is a diagnosis. Mining is the bridge, not the engine — every long run eventually converts to cultivated and synthesised income, and the players who never make that transition are the ones who conclude the map is exhausted and the game is unwinnable.

Mining is temporary by design

Deposits are small relative to consumption, deliberately so. That makes renewable income matter far more here than in comparable base-builders, and it means the correct response to a depleting map is to tech toward infinite sources rather than to hunt richer patches.

Two of those sources exist. Plant cultivation, unlocked through alien research, converts water and power into any resource you have a matching scanned species for — and produces biomass on the side, which burns for power. Carbon and iron synthesizers convert power directly into the two basic materials, no deposit required. Between them, a mature run mines almost nothing: it is entirely possible to finish a campaign having barely touched the main map's ore.

Until then, upgrade miners before expanding to marginal patches. Higher tiers drain a deposit at the same rate but return more ore per drain, so the same node yields more from a better extractor — a top-tier miner pays its own upkeep in the resource it mines several times over. Note that extraction rates were reduced in a later patch, so older figures overstate what a mine returns; the ranking between tiers is unchanged but the absolute numbers are lower, which pushes the transition to cultivation earlier than it used to be.

Use the geoscanner constantly, not occasionally. It reveals additional deposits including rare ones, and one material has no deposits at all, so scanning is its only discovery route. Scan whenever the cooldown is up, even mid-crisis.

The turtle trap

The classic failure is staying on the main map, walling in tighter, and treating mining as the economy. It produces exactly the symptoms players describe as imbalance: depleted nodes, edge-of-map patches that cost more to hold than they yield, and defences that consume more than the mines return. The fix is to leave. Outpost maps exist to be the answer to an exhausted home map, and a run that never establishes them will stall regardless of how well the walls are built.

Reading a cultivation plant

Every cultivable plant is described by a total average yield and a percentage split across the resources it produces. Both numbers matter, and neither alone tells you which plant to farm — a high rare percentage on a low-yield plant frequently loses to a lower percentage on a high-yield one. Multiply the two and rank plants by absolute output of the resource you actually want.

Genus is a fast heuristic once you know it. Crystalline plants never produce biomass and split their output between the two basic metals and a rare, sitting in the middle of the range. Fungus plants carry the highest rare shares, including the handful of outliers approaching half their output. The bulbous family is biomass-heavy and, importantly, contains most of the plants that carry two rares at once. The bushy family is ironium filler with a token rare percentage — those entries sit in the single digits and are effectively basic-resource farms wearing a rare's colour.

Naming follows resource colour, which is useful but only signals a plant's headline rare. Watch for name collisions across genera: the same species name can appear as a high-value fungus and a near-worthless bush, distinguished only by the small genus label. Several families are exact statistical clones of each other, differing only in which biomes they grow in or which secondary rare they carry — pick within a family on availability, not output.

Two structural facts shape planning. First, biome locks: cobalt plants are jungle-only, palladium is overwhelmingly an acidic crop, titanium is a volcanic one, uranium grows only in desert and acidic maps, while a few materials spread widely. Plan plantations per biome rather than per resource. Second, the rare is always the minority of any plant's output — every plantation is also a basic-resource farm, which is why cultivation solves your carbonium and ironium problem at the same time as your rare-metal one.

Dual-rare plants deserve deliberate use. Several species carry a second rare at around a tenth to a quarter of their output, which turns one plantation into two supply lines — worth taking even at a small loss on the primary when the secondary is something you would otherwise need a whole outpost for.

Farm layout that does not need optimising

Cultivators need less room than people expect, and their planting areas overlap, so packing them tight eventually costs output. A little overlap is fine and not worth avoiding. Harvester placement barely matters at all: close to the cultivator, spread out, straddling two cultivators, or eating into planting space all wash out in practice. Plan on three to four harvesters per cultivator and stop thinking about it.

The reason not to optimise is that one extra cultivator more than compensates for any layout inefficiency, and once you are hitting your production target being slightly over-built is irrelevant. Spend the attention on water instead: a dedicated farm map wants pumps on every available water source, pipes running between rows, and cultivators lined along both sides of each pipe with enough gap for planting. Two dozen cultivators spread across the resources you care about is income you never think about again.

Water availability is therefore the real siting criterion for a farm. Jungle is the standard pick — open space, accessible mud, and pumps that serve cultivation alone rather than competing with gas production. Acidic maps have abundant water and sludge but the pump sites are contested between cultivators and gas plants, so choose them when you want the gas. Volcanic maps look waterless but geothermal vents there produce doubled power and mud, and a couple of vents comfortably run eight cultivators — the real constraint is fitting the layout around magma pools and cooling stations. Because maps are procedurally generated, check the actual roll before committing to a biome's reputation.

Keep some cultivation at the headquarters even after a dedicated farm exists. When you are suddenly short of one specific material, changing a plant type at home is far quicker than a trip.

Storage is a strategic building

Research now consumes resources per item, which changed the economy's shape: the constraint late in a campaign is not income but the ability to hold enough at once to pay for what you want. Endgame research and construction can demand six figures of a basic material, so storage targets escalate steeply through a run — a few thousand early to survive mid-battle rebuilds, tens of thousands before each headquarters tier, and hundreds of thousands before the largest projects.

Solid material storage needs no power, which makes it the one structure you can carpet across an empty safe map at no infrastructure cost — an open desert map with floors researched becomes a storage park. Keeping a healthy buffer also has a tactical function: mid-battle repairs and rebuilds are only possible if the materials are already banked, and selling structures returns full value, so anything about to be overrun should be sold rather than defended.

Free money you are probably leaving on the table

Creatures drop resources, and the volume scales with wave size — at high multipliers the drops alone fund a research queue, and once all resource types are unlocked through missions the drop rate climbs sharply. Mods that increase drops turn wave defence into farming. One quirk worth knowing: the marker for a map's sole deposit of a given material can vanish from the minimap once you already hold enough of that material, so over-collecting from drops before you have located the node can hide it.

Survival economics are different

Cultivation is effectively unavailable in survival. Species familiarity accrues but the encyclopedia lists everything as unknown, so no species can be selected in a cultivator — which removes the entire renewable rare-resource engine that campaign runs on.

What remains is detector sweeps to surface deposits, enemy drops, occasional hive yields, and synthesizers powered hard from the opening — spending the early minutes on power production so synthesizers can run is the closest survival gets to an infinite economy. Accept that you will not build everything: scrape together enough rare material for a few superior weapons, prioritise which upgrades matter, and settle for whatever type you actually find. With enough carbonium you can also sustain defences by repairing them indefinitely rather than out-teching the wave.

Recovery when the economy breaks

If carbonium and ironium have bottomed out and the main map cannot fund a recovery, the move is to relocate the problem. Go to a low-threat map — an early desert or recon area — and carpet it with solar panels and synthesizers. It is cheap, it is safe, and it rebuilds a basic-materials income without fighting anything, which is precisely what a broke run needs.

Balance your defensive mix while you do it. An all-ammunition turret line is the fastest way to drain a fragile economy; energy towers cost you a one-off build and then run on the grid. And use the closest-target rule deliberately: let part of an outpost stick out as bait, then run power lines to miners placed further from the map edge, so the economy sits behind the aggro magnet rather than inside the blast radius.

The endgame project

The mega structures are less a build than a logistics campaign, and they are worth preparing for long before you can research them. Their research alone can cost six figures of a basic material with construction on top, which is the real reason to be running two hundred thousand or more in storage rather than the tens of thousands that carried you through the mid game.

The chain starts with a laboratory structure that unlocks research on the rest, then three structures that must each reach their top tier: one that grants free AI cores on every map, one that accelerates plant growth on its own map to the point where harvesters can work wild plants without cultivators at all, and one that reveals hidden underground caches. That last one is the weakest of the three by a distance — its upkeep demands a bank of refineries consuming rare metal every second — so research and build it last and switch it on only when an objective actually needs it. A cryogenic base exporting coolant makes it far more affordable. A final workshop structure gates the last build and requires everything else maxed.

The single most important planning decision: give the final structure its own map. Activating it summons the strongest raid in the game, and no map is exempt — including the raid-free ones. If your other mega structures are sitting on that map, they are all in the blast radius. Build it somewhere with mud or sludge for local power, bring its enormous liquid upkeep in through decompressors rather than producing it locally, and have defences finished before you activate. If it runs short of any input you start the process over.

Recap

Treat mining as the opening bridge and convert to cultivation and synthesizers as fast as alien research allows. Rank plants by yield multiplied by rare percentage, plan plantations around biome locks, and take dual-rare species where they fit. Do not optimise farm layout — add a cultivator instead. Scale storage aggressively because research and endgame construction are paid in banked resources, sell anything about to be lost, and keep an energy-based share of your turret line so defence does not eat the economy. In survival, cultivation is off the table, so power and synthesizers carry the run.

Outposts

Outposts are the campaign's real strategic layer. They are where your economy lives, where your waves get split, and — because maps you are not standing on are never attacked — where risk stops applying. Deciding which maps to keep is a bigger decision than any tower choice.

The rules that constrain everything

You may hold eight remote outposts at once. Leaving a mission map without establishing one means you cannot go back — with the exception that a map still carrying an incomplete mission remains reachable. Since several maps are only worth keeping for one specific resource, and since some are worth keeping forever, the eight slots are a real budget to plan rather than a limit you bump into by accident.

The other structural fact is that no base is attacked while you are absent, and production, research and mining all continue regardless. Outposts therefore carry no real risk once established, only the invisible cost that their mines quietly run dry — depletion is not shown on the teleport screen, so a farm you left running may have been idle for an hour before you notice.

The developers did not anticipate outposts being used this safely and have discussed making them riskier, so treat the more extreme applications as strategies that may not survive a patch. And be aware that different players draw the line differently: some use undefended off-map mining freely as the intended reward for expanding, others consider anything beyond a map's specific rare resource to be cheating themselves out of the game. Both positions are common and the game does not adjudicate.

Choosing maps by threat

Threat rating, visible on the orbital panel, is the single most useful number when picking an outpost site because it sets wave size. The difference between a full-threat map and a three-quarter-threat one is enormous; a full-threat map produces waves comparable to attacks on your main base, which on Brutal means top-tier walls and turrets before the site is viable at all. Most half-threat maps carry no real danger, and even near-zero threat still produces constant small attacks rather than silence.

At the bottom of the scale sit the reconnaissance and low-threat exploratory maps, several of which never generate waves at all. These are the best sites in the game for anything that does not need to be near your defences: mass gas generation, synthesizer banks, storage parks, cultivator farms, research buildings. One acidic recon map converted into a power-and-production hub will carry an entire campaign.

Resource-concentration maps are the other standout. On several of them attacks come from a single direction in small groups, so a farm behind one chokepoint needs almost no defence and the rest of the map is yours. Be aware that procedural maps sometimes misreport their threat between generation and the orbital panel, so verify before committing a large build.

Wave composition follows your outposts

An outpost's waves draw units from its own biome, but your main base's waves draw from every biome you hold an outpost in. Settling a hostile biome therefore makes your headquarters attacks more varied and harder to counter, not just that outpost. Outpost waves also grow over time toward a maximum that can exceed your main base's — so a farm left running for hours is not permanently safe, it is safe until you visit it again.

What each biome is for

  • Volcanic — free energy. Geothermal output is boosted here, roughly three cultivators fit per vent area, and the power surplus makes it the natural home for the highest-draw buildings: synthesizers and global ammunition production. Costs are cramped layouts around magma pools and mandatory cooling.
  • Desert — space. Once floor technology is researched, empty desert becomes a storage park, since solid storage needs no power at all. Also the uranium biome for cultivation, and a solar site.
  • Acidic — sludge, and therefore gas, in effectively unlimited quantity. The best home for mass power, synthesizers and palladium cultivation, and the earliest large economic boost once the second tier of liquid handling is available.
  • Jungle — water and open ground, which makes it the default farm biome, plus the cobalt crops.
  • Marsh — a decent farm biome, usually made redundant by a volcanic farm.
  • Cobalt zone — low threat, so it doubles as cultivation space; cobalt is consumed heavily by upgrades and repair infrastructure, so it is worth mining actively rather than trickling.
  • Metallic valley and expansion biomes — morphium, morphium towers and their liquid, but leave them until last.

How many, and mine or cultivate

Strictly, zero outposts are required, and one is enough to produce every ore type via cultivators. In practice most runs settle around four dedicated to the endgame resources, established as research reveals each area, plus two or three more for equipment materials. A farm-everything approach with three well-chosen farm maps also works and never mines outside the starting area.

The mine-versus-cultivate decision comes down to consumption volume. Mining is faster, so materials you burn in bulk — the ones feeding power plant upgrades and mass construction — are worth extracting in their home biome. Materials used only for high-tier weapons and reforging are better handled by a few cultivators trickling into storage on a safe map, because you simply do not need much unless you reroll equipment constantly.

A common progression takes titanium first, since it upgrades geothermal, then palladium, which upgrades gas — the two power lines that decide your mid game. Uranium's main use is nuclear power, and by the time you reach it you usually have what you need for fusion, so it can be deferred or skipped. An equally defensible ordering chases whatever upgrade you need soonest: cobalt early because repair facilities and railguns consume it in bulk, then titanium, then palladium. Leave expansion biomes until last, and note that completing the final per-element outpost missions is not required to finish the campaign — once the handling technology is unlocked, everything is obtainable through cultivation.

What lives where

The division is dictated by what travels between maps. Ammunition and ordinary resources are global; power, AI cores and locally consumed fluids are not; liquids move only through compressors.

So move to a safe outpost: communications hubs, ammunition factories, ammunition and material storage, cultivators, research buildings and the armoury. Keep at the headquarters: power generation, AI cores, plasma and other fluids consumed on site, and rift infrastructure. The payoff is not just safety — it is space. The headquarters map takes the worst waves in the game, so every sitting-duck structure removed from it is another layer of defensive depth that fits around it.

Cross-map liquid logistics is what makes the whole arrangement work. Compress gas or magma at the outposts that have them and decompress at the headquarters to run upgraded power plants for map-locked buildings like AI cores and rare-ore miners. Compress water at a wet map and ship it to a desert or cavern outpost so cultivators and fusion can run there. Both directions are used constantly in mature runs, and liquid teleporters remove most of the pipe-laying that used to be involved.

Defending an outpost — or not

The minimum viable outpost is enough power to run the mine, a portal, and simple walls. If it is lost, rebuild it; the cost is trivial compared to the effort of making it genuinely safe. For a mine just outside the main base, run an unprotected power line and accept that you will occasionally replace it.

Early on that means wind turbines, the mine, a portal and a radar, with you personally handling anything that shows up. Later it means a wall or double wall with repair coverage — and crucially, holding off on turrets until an attack actually looks likely, since idle turrets cost power and AI cores permanently for occasional use. Because attacks target the closest base to the spawn rather than your headquarters, outposts absorb aggro whether or not you meant them to, which is an argument for treating them as expendable rather than fortifying them all.

Contested permanent mining outposts are a legitimate choice, but choose them for the challenge rather than the efficiency: they split your attention and your resources away from the headquarters, and a low-threat site produces the same materials for free.

Two habits pay off while you are away. Watch your basic materials and teleport home the moment a headquarters upgrade becomes affordable, since banked resources do nothing. And upgrade power before adding or upgrading research labs, because lab draw roughly doubles with each tier and a lab that browns out your grid stops the research it was built for.

Recap

Eight outpost slots, no return without one, and no attacks while you are away — those three rules define the layer. Pick sites by threat rating, favour recon and low-threat maps for production, and let each biome do the job it is good at: volcanic for power, acidic for gas, desert for storage, jungle for farms. Move everything that travels between maps off your headquarters and keep only what must be local. Defend outposts minimally, treat them as expendable aggro sponges, and remember that the biomes you settle feed back into what attacks your main base.

Defence

There is no best tower, and looking for one is the mistake. Resistances vary by biome and by species, ammunition is a harder limit than damage, and the difference between a line that holds and one that collapses is usually damage-type coverage and supply — not tier.

How to choose towers at all

Every tower is described by three things: its damage type, its range, and whether it concentrates damage on one target or spreads it. Its tooltip states the damage type and whether it burns ammunition or energy, and researching a species fills in that creature's vulnerabilities in the database. Match the two and you have your answer for that map's threat mix.

The shortcut, when you cannot be bothered, is simply to keep a varied spread of damage types on every line. That is enough to be covered against almost anything, and it is why a mixed wall consistently outperforms a wall of whatever the current best tower is. Between a long-ranged fire option, a long-ranged energy option and massed artillery you will always have something that works, or at minimum immediate feedback about what not to use here.

Ammunition is the real ceiling

A tower ammo factory produces two and a half tower ammunition per second. A minigun tower consumes twenty per second while firing. That is eight factories to sustain one minigun at full rate — which is why you cannot line a wall with them, and why over-placing any ammunition-based tower converts it into expensive scenery. Energy towers sidestep the problem entirely, which is most of their appeal. Tower ammo factories serve only towers; armouries serve only your mech; nothing else produces ammunition at all, and there is no prioritisation between the two pools.

Ammunition factories stop consuming resources once every magazine is full, so the long drain you see after building lots of ammo storage is the storage filling, not a permanent tax. Off-map factories still display a negative rate on the overview, but actual income matches the undrained figure — treat that readout as a display artefact. The one genuine sink to watch for is production of ammunition types you have not yet researched: those magazines never fill, so the drain never stops. Synthesizers or cultivators feeding the same resources make the whole question moot.

The pattern that works is a solid uniform row of a workhorse tower — miniguns in the kinetic row, plasma or laser in the energy row — with a few of these substituted in at intervals for damage-type coverage.

Those substitutions double as visual section markers, which is more useful than it sounds when you are placing a third tower type along a long wall: replace one or two towers in each marked section and the whole line stays evenly mixed without counting tiles.

Note also that a mine layer can be built purely to seed a minefield and then sold once the mines are down, though bound-skill multi-mine drops from the mech do the same job faster and cheaper. Mines and flooring conflict, so a stretch of wall gets one or the other.

Interleaving them into an existing row — replacing every fourth structure in a shield row, for example — buys continuous repair without adding depth.

The critical limitation: repair towers repair, they never rebuild. A destroyed structure stays destroyed until you place it again, so a wiped bunker still needs manual reconstruction. Nor should repair drones be treated as a damage sponge; they are maintenance, not tanking.

The respawn suppression is a strategic tool in its own right: blanketing a cleared map in radar coverage stops it re-seeding, which is how players turn a farm map into permanently quiet territory. Pair radar with every heavy artillery group, and put at least one in every base however small.

Shield generators stack. One provides a given shield pool to the walls and turrets in its radius; two provide double. That fact alone reshapes late defensive layouts, because it means depth of shielding is a real axis rather than a binary.

Their radius is large enough that three rows of generators all still project forward over the front turret line, so a three-deep shield block means your leading turrets sit inside overlapping coverage rather than being the sacrificial row. Let the generator's range set the layer count rather than picking a number.

They are not free: each one draws standing energy and supercharged plasma every second, so a deep shield line demands a live surplus rather than a stockpile — roughly double your consumption in production is a comfortable margin. On maps without water, and therefore without fusion, an ionizer is the route to shields instead. One orientation gotcha: a shield tower's plasma intake side must not face a morphium pipeline.

Floating towers drop into any pool of liquid and need no power run at all, which makes them immune to the classic failure where one zerged power connector silences an entire line. The incinerator variant behaves like a flamer and gives fire coverage in places you could not otherwise build; the piercer does solid work on heavy ranged units.

Morphium towers deal good line damage, build on both ground and liquid, and carry built-in shield generators — which saves your plasma for heavy artillery instead. They need neither power nor AI cores, only a morphium pipeline, and morphium comes from the metallic valley, so using them at the headquarters requires liquid compression technology to ship it home.

The combined-arms argument is straightforward: keep converting plasma for heavy artillery and shields while warping in morphium to run morphium towers for their own shielding and piercing, paying only a modest power draw for the decompressors.

Building a wall that holds

The consistent finding across layouts is that depth beats thickness. Several thin rows with space between them outperform one dense slab, because area attacks that would destroy four adjacent wall pieces only destroy one, and because spaced rows let more towers engage.

A standard depth ordering runs short-range and cheap filler at the front, medium range behind, and artillery with radar at the back. In practice that becomes a three-row wall: the outer row your highest-grade cheap sentries, the middle row acid, flame, rocket and minigun with plasma and shock added late, and the inner row artillery and railgun. Turrets shoot over and past each other, so stacking depth costs you nothing in firing arcs.

An endgame version of the same idea builds each row as a solid uniform backbone with a second type sprinkled through it. The outer row is a solid line of miniguns with a few lightning towers spread through it and one or two rockets per section between them — that row alone handles the overwhelming majority of what arrives. The second row is a solid line of plasma or laser with morphium towers spaced through it. The third holds shield generators with a repair tower beside each, and artillery filling the gaps between them. Four rows of wall in front, with energy walls as the outermost layer.

Alternatively, stagger rather than align. Three diagonal, offset rows of railguns fire slightly out of sync with each other, which spreads damage over time instead of volleying it all at once — fewer wasted overkill shots on an already-dying target, and more continuous output. Put the anti-ranged element at the very back where enemy fire cannot reach it.

Wall progression itself is unglamorous: one layer, then two, then three, then upgrade the tier. Scatter isolated wall pieces outside the real line for creatures to stop and chew on, and keep the sections irregular so waves stall in front of your guns rather than arriving as a clean front.

The 40×15 doctrine

Even on Brutal, do not ring the entire main base. Build rectangular defence outposts roughly forty by fifteen on each of the four approaches, fill them completely with towers, and wrap each in the best wall you have. You get the concentrated firepower of a fortress on every lane an attack can actually use, without paying for perimeter that will never be tested. Radial wall spokes running outward from the base achieve something similar: the leading edge of a horde attacks the spoke's outer face and clogs the approach while everything behind queues up, at which point artillery does the heavy lifting and direct-fire towers mop up.

Logistics that decide whether the wall fires

Ammunition — for both your mech and your towers — is transported across maps automatically, so you can tear down the armoury on the headquarters map and rebuild it somewhere quieter without losing supply. Only power and AI cores must be native to the map a tower stands on. That single asymmetry justifies the whole outpost economy: put ammunition factories and storage on a safe map fed by synthesizers, and keep the headquarters map — which eats the nastiest waves — free of sitting-duck structures so more defensive depth fits around it.

Liquid teleporters remove the need to weave pipe across an entire base, which is what makes scattered artillery batteries and gas-fed towers practical at all. Gas is teleportable; plan around that rather than around pipe routes.

For a fast, disposable line — a mission outpost, or a lane you suddenly need covered — remember that flamer, minigun, rocket, artillery and acid spitter all run at one energy per second. A single biomass power plant will run a radar, an AI hub and a mix of those, which is about thirty seconds of building for a functional defence.

Finally, keep rift portals at every defence point. You are the strongest weapon on the map, and an outpost only has to survive long enough for you to arrive.

Are tower mods worth it?

Mostly no, with specific exceptions. The structural argument against is hard to beat: buffing a tower's fire rate is worse than building another tower, and by the time you have accumulated enough high-quality mods to matter, energy, ammunition and AI cores have stopped being limiting factors, so you can simply place more towers. Mods are also lost when you replace a tower unless you remember to unequip them first, and range mods cannot go in artillery at all.

The exceptions are where a mod changes what a tower can do rather than how fast it does it. Damage-type mods matter when you know which creatures dominate an area — an acid mod on a sentinel meaningfully improves it against acid-vulnerable heavies. Ammunition-saving mods pay off when you are leaning hard on one ammunition type, and combined with rate of fire they make shotgun towers self-sustaining. Big towers gain more from the same mod than small ones, because mod value scales with base output, so heavy artillery and gatling plasma are where your best mods belong. Cryo mods make flamers useful against a few specific creatures. And piercing mods want bullet towers, not rockets or tall plasma turrets.

Recap

Mix damage types rather than hunting for a best tower, and size your ammunition industry before you place ammunition-hungry towers. Build depth — several spaced rows, each a solid backbone with a second type seasoned through it — rather than one thick wall, and put long-range and anti-ranged elements at the back. Cover everything with repair towers and radar, use shields as a stackable depth layer once plasma allows, and keep firebases small, tight and closer to the map edge than anything you care about. Solve logistics first: ammunition travels between maps, power and AI cores do not.

Research

Research is not a clock you wait out — it is a resource you produce, in two separate pipelines, at a rate you decide by how much you build. Getting that wrong is why players find themselves fifteen hours in with a tech tree that has barely moved.

Two pipelines, two buildings

Communications hubs feed the first two research tabs; the laboratory feeds the alien research tab. They are entirely separate pipelines. Stacking and upgrading hubs does nothing whatsoever for alien technology, and a run that stalls on alien tech while the base bristles with hubs is the classic version of this mistake. Match the building to the tab you are actually researching.

Both scale on count and level, and here the arithmetic matters. Each level roughly doubles the energy draw for one additional research point — the first upgrade is nearly free value, the second is reasonable, and beyond that you are paying double for a small increment. A top-tier laboratory alone can consume as much energy as a small base. The efficient shape is therefore wide rather than deep: raise the headquarters to unlock more building slots, then run several structures at a middle tier rather than a couple at maximum.

Switch off the pipeline you are not using

At any given moment you are researching from one tree or the other, never both. The idle building type is drawing full power for nothing, so turning it off while you work the other tree frees a large slice of grid — often enough to run a defensive line you could not otherwise afford. Just be aware that a laboratory you have already upgraded is an expensive thing to have switched off, which is another argument against over-upgrading them.

Research costs resources now

In campaign, every research item carries a one-time resource price paid when you queue it, and those prices climb the deeper into the tree you go. This is the single largest change to how research plays: the old approach of queueing the entire tier and waiting is gone, and ordering is now a genuine decision between what you need this hour and what might be useful later.

The knock-on effect is that your economy has to stay healthy for the whole campaign rather than just the opening, and rare materials that used to have a single niche now have a permanent sink. Survival is unchanged — costs there work as they always did.

Several interface features exist to support this and are worth using. The research screen shows your stored resources so affordability is visible at a glance, hovering an item reveals its cost, unaffordable items are flagged with an icon of the missing resource directly on the tree, and the search function matches both research names and the buildings or weapons they grant — so searching for a pipe finds the technology that unlocks pipes. The search persists as you move between branches.

What to research, and in what order

There is no canonical order and looking for one is a trap. What exists is a set of priorities most experienced players converge on.

Economy before weapons. Power, mining and logistics gate everything downstream; a small set of turrets carries your defence for a surprisingly long time. Early is power and basic production, mid is weapons and towers, late is fusion and alien technology.

Rare-resource handling goes straight to the top of the queue, always. You cannot meaningfully collect or farm a resource before its handling technology is researched — and that includes cultivation, so a plant that lists a resource yields none of it until the handling tech is in. This is the most common way to waste a farm.

Check whether an upgrade is usable before you queue it. Several obvious-looking researches are dead weight for hours because their payoff is gated behind a material you cannot yet obtain. Higher artillery tiers need uranium, which arrives late and only doubles damage without adding range. Upper-tier gas plants need palladium, and upper-tier geothermal needs titanium — though that same geothermal technology also covers the solar and wind upgrades, which need only basic materials, so it pays for itself anyway. Top-tier energy storage costs an order of magnitude more cobalt than the tier below it.

Mech technology comes last, with exceptions. Grab the cheap ones opportunistically, and take exploration-flavoured upgrades — health, scan cooldown, building speed — early, because they compound over the whole run.

Do not over-plan. Time spent reading every node is time not spent playing while golems dismantle your base. Queue broadly, promote a specific item when you hit something that needs it, and accept that you will never finish the tree in one playthrough. Which technologies matter is learned by playing, and a wave you barely survive is the clearest possible signal about what to research next. One interface quirk: newly queued research lands at the back of the queue and must be moved up manually.

Two shapes of opening are worth understanding rather than copying. A defensive opening raises the headquarters tier early to unlock everything else, takes the two highest-value tower types before any breadth of weapons, then immediately funds them with resource production and ammunition handling, then upgrades the towers already in use rather than adding new types. A combat-led opening instead takes mech weapons first and expects you to meet waves personally in the field, which lets you defer walls and turrets but demands you actually go out and fight. Pick one deliberately: the failure case is taking half of each and having neither a working wall nor a weapon that clears a wave.

On Brutal specifically, explosives deserve to be first or second after you survive the opening wave, because basic turrets simply do not handle the large rock creatures and you will be resource-starved for long enough that a personal answer matters more than a built one.

Why nodes stay locked

Locked entries come from four different gates, which is why "just research it" is not one path. Some need a prerequisite research. Three unlock only by completing the titanium, palladium and uranium mining outpost missions. Many unlock through species familiarity — scanning a specific creature or plant, often several times. And some require owning and progressing an expansion, with a few requiring you to finish it, which means a base-game tree permanently displays entries you can never unlock.

Rather than guessing, hover the locked node. The tooltip states whether it unlocks from a mission or from scanning, and if scanning, names exactly what to scan. That single habit resolves nearly every "why can't I research this" question.

The scan-gated group is worth knowing about in advance because it changes where you point your bioscanner: alien camouflage, combat drones, time warp, gravity grenades and the hazenite line all come from scanning specific species in specific biomes. Scan everything you walk past — many species look nearly identical, the rarer groups sit at map edges, and the scanner now remembers accumulated progress so you can break off and resume. Scanning turrets contribute too, and beyond unlocking research, familiarity raises the yield you get from plants and grants damage bonuses against creatures you have studied.

Keeping research moving

Never let research idle — continuous uptime is one of the habits that separates comfortable Brutal runs from desperate ones. That means keeping the queue full, keeping the pipeline buildings powered, and treating a browned-out laboratory as an emergency.

It also means understanding where research time actually comes from. Exploring and travelling between maps eats enormous amounts of real time, during which everything keeps researching, so a run that pushes outward finishes far more of the tree than one that turtles. Fusion in particular is gated less by its research than by the field trips needed to gather what its farms and cultivation demand — which is worsened, not helped, by insisting on mining out each map before moving on.

Recap

Build communications hubs for the first two trees and laboratories for the third, go wide at middle tiers rather than deep at top tier, and switch off whichever pipeline is idle. Take economy and logistics before weapons, promote rare-resource handling the moment it appears, and check what a technology is gated behind before paying for it. Queue broadly and reprioritise on demand rather than planning the tree, use the hover tooltip to diagnose locked nodes, and scan everything you pass.

Mr. Riggs

You are the strongest weapon on the map. Towers hold ground; the mech decides fights. Everything about loadout, mods and movement skills follows from that — an outpost only has to survive long enough for you to arrive, and a wave you meet in the field is a wave that never touches your walls.

The two rules of loadout

Everything else is preference, but these two hold across every difficulty and every biome.

Two damage types, two ammunition pools. Carrying two weapons that share an ammunition pool halves your sustain for no coverage gain, and carrying two weapons of the same damage type means one resistant species shuts you down completely. Bullets plus explosives is the classic pairing precisely because nothing resists both.

One hand for rate of fire, one for damage. Mixed waves contain a mass of small creatures and a few large ones, and a single weapon rarely answers both. A fast-firing hand keeps the swarm off you while a heavy hand deletes priority targets. It is easier to name the pairs that fail than the ones that work: rocket with nuke, flamer with cryogenic, and anything paired with the blaster all leave you with one job covered twice.

The mechanical details matter here. Equipping a melee arm disables the other arm entirely, so melee is a commitment; two ranged weapons fire simultaneously. Matching melee pairs — two swords, two spears, two hammers — swing together for double damage and a wider arc, while matching power fists alternate instead. Mixed melee pairs cannot swing together at all, though some post-expansion pairings deliberately combine a physical and an energy variant to get two damage types out of one melee commitment. Bind slow-charging weapons to the right button and fast-firing ones to the left, and remember a third slot on a different ammunition type as the fallback when your mains run dry.

Creatures that repel melee are mixed into groups specifically to break your rhythm: your swing connects with one, the life-per-hit stream stops, the stagger pauses you, and you are surrounded. Players who drop melee for ranged loadouts routinely survive fights they previously lost. Melee still earns a slot for plant harvesting and as an ammunition-free fallback.

Mods are most of the weapon

Rarity determines only the size of the numeric roll, so redder is better all else equal. Grey weapons take no mods at all; blue and above take them but also consume more ammunition, which is why ammunition-conservation mods so often earn their slot — they are offsetting a cost the upgrade itself introduced.

The mods that change what a weapon does, rather than how much it does, are the ones worth hunting:

  • A damage type different from the weapon's own, applied as a damage-over-time rider. This is the trick that lets one weapon hurt things immune to its primary type, and stacking it across both hands means dealing three or four damage types at once. Note the arithmetic: a bonus damage mod only applies a target's vulnerability multiplier to the added portion, not the weapon's whole output.
  • Splash, which is quietly enormous on any fast-firing weapon and is the reason the in-menu damage readout misleads — splash appears not to be counted, so explosives read worse than they play.
  • Homing, which lets you fire while strafing and around corners, and corrects onto new targets. Splash is the alternative if you would rather aim.
  • Piercing, valuable because your effective sight range is short and packs are dense — but wasted on rockets and similar.
  • Cluster, which produces secondary explosions from every hit, including hits on terrain, so misses still break into fragments that find targets.
  • Stun, which enables genuine lockdown. Note that the plasma rifle accepts it and the minigun does not.
  • Cryo, taken on almost anything purely for the slow. Very little resists cold, which makes it the safest universal rider.
  • Projectiles per shot, which frequently doubles output outright and largely obsoletes the burst-projectile version.

Skip spread mods — they usually make spread worse — and treat health-on-hit as a nice rider rather than a survival plan, since the heal is too small to tank on. On a nuke launcher, reduced ammunition cost is the only mod really worth chasing; homing is unreliable because the projectile is so slow. And do not chase perfect rolls: settling a few percent below optimum costs a fraction of the attempts.

Armour modules and the two loadouts

Four armour slots, all stackable — four copies of the same upgrade is legal and often correct. Each upgrade carries two or three guaranteed mods plus additional rolled ones by tier, drawn from a pool specific to that upgrade rather than from everything. The interface only shows three stats on hover, so click through to the full list; a great deal of the system is invisible if you never do.

Three effects are worth more than the rest. Reduced building cost scales with the entire game — a full set takes construction to roughly a third of list price, which is transformative when you are building fusion plants, top-tier laboratories and the rift itself. Movement speed is the best single combat and non-combat stat in the game and saves enormous amounts of real time. Reduced skill cooldown turns your quickbar into spam: grenades, healing, mines, area scans and your movement skill all come back faster, which is the default pick when nothing else is obvious. Health and health regeneration are a tier below but you will roll them constantly anyway, and unlike shields they benefit from resistances.

Which leads to the things to avoid. Shield stats are a trap: shields do not benefit from damage reductions, the recharge delay is long enough not to matter in a real fight, and the regeneration mod is close to inert. Reduced building time is redundant since defences already build in seconds. Bonus resource drops and damage reflection are not worth a slot. Several stats have been reported as simply non-functional, notably blanket resist-all, acid resistance and radar range — treat any of them appearing on a roll as filler rather than value.

Among the upgrades themselves, the titanium machinery line is the standout: three guaranteed mods including the two best ones, and it can roll cost reduction on top. Maintenance tools are the cheap early source of guaranteed cost reduction, and the fusion core line does the same job strictly worse — skip it. Attack gear is the mod-farming pick thanks to guaranteed drop rate plus cooldown. Defence gear is the honest combat option; offensive equipment wastes a guaranteed slot on reflection, so take defence gear instead. Repair tools have the highest theoretical ceiling and are effectively impossible to roll well. Before committing to a line, check which rare materials it consumes — some lines are gated behind exactly the materials you have been deferring.

Keep two loadouts and swap

Before a building session, swap to cost-reduction modules; before heading out, swap back to combat. The saving on a fusion buildout or a research-building upgrade dwarfs the inconvenience. Two cautions: the same percentage also reduces what you receive when selling, so building cheap and selling dear is not a real exploit; and since crafted modules were weakened, the best ones now come from world drops — explore, fight and open caches rather than grinding the crafting bench.

Quickbar items are free damage

Quickbar items recharge on their own, so holding them in reserve is pure waste — use them constantly. Restocking most of them does consume resources, which is worth knowing before a long exploration trip, though the emergency detonation and radar pulse appear to be free. More armouries, at higher tiers, recharge them dramatically faster.

Grenades never become obsolete — they out-damage a grenade launcher per hit for most of the game and cost you nothing. Mines are effectively unlimited and are the best trap tool you have, with nuclear variants for genuinely dangerous packs; plant the whole set spaced out rather than stacking them, and remember both types have a brief arming delay, so a mine dropped on a creature's face gets eaten. The portable turret costs no ammunition or energy and is best used as an aggro sponge, since creatures rush it. Emergency detonation is your get-out-of-jail card and doubles as bait — draw melee creatures in, then detonate. Radar pulse reveals a wide area briefly and is worth spamming while exploring.

Alien-tech additions are worth chasing. Cryo mines do little damage but chain a slow along an enemy's path, giving turrets free damage time. Gravity mines and grenades pull small creatures into a lingering damage sphere, and combining a gravity pull with a nuke is a compounding kill. Several of these schematics come only from crystal pillars rather than the research tree, and pillar-unlocked schematics carry across future campaign and survival runs — one of the few forms of permanent progress in the game.

Movement skills

Dash was the only movement skill for years and remains the reflexive pick, but the others have been reworked into genuine alternatives, and the choice now changes how you fight rather than just how you travel.

Dash closes distance and disengages, and you can fire while dashing. Its real value late is the elemental trail: circle back so pursuers cross your own trail and it becomes a temporary wall that kills weak chasers outright. Cryo is the preferred element — very little resists it and the slow does the work — while fire is easiest to unlock but useless in volcanic regions. Trails are weak in the metallic biome, where most creatures fly over them.

Dodge roll is no longer a worse dash. During the roll you are invulnerable and pass through units, so it escapes through a pack rather than around it, it can be cancelled at any point, and it ends in an explosion that clears your immediate surroundings.

Power jump now marks its landing spot clearly, flies over obstacles below a certain height, destroys destructible ones on the way, stops short with a visible marker when something indestructible blocks it, and extends itself slightly when you are just barely short of clearing terrain. It is also the specific escape from being walled in.

Teleport is the strongest strategic option once unlocked — point and click past terrain, with the shortest cooldown of the set. It now travels as a bolt of lightning with the camera following, lands with an energy burst that stuns nearby creatures, reaches slightly beyond your vision, and extends the jump when you target an invalid spot, so you can point past an obstacle without seeing where you will land.

Chain your movement skill constantly for map traversal rather than saving it for emergencies — it is the difference between crossing a map in twenty seconds and two minutes.

Fighting habits

Keep moving. Creatures fire at where you are standing, so strafing between shots dodges most incoming fire outright, and the herd that follows you is a herd your towers are not fighting. When you are hurt, warping to the headquarters heals you for free — repair charges are not your only option.

Read the battlefield. A creature's hit glow tells you its health, from white through yellow to red, and enemies that only aggro when provoked show an alert glow as you pass; both proper health bars and damage numbers can be enabled in the options if you prefer explicit information. Enable camera zoom through the console and long-range weapons become dramatically stronger, since the default view is too tight to use their range at all.

Use the environment. Meteor and eruption impact markers damage creatures too, so lure packs into them. The large rock creatures have a dead zone at roughly spear length where they cannot hit you — stand there to recover while other ranged creatures shoot into their backs. And be careful around neutral creatures: they blame you for any damage they take nearby, including from another creature's attack or a falling meteor.

Recap

Carry two damage types on two ammunition pools, one hand fast and one heavy, and let mods add the coverage your weapons lack. Prefer ammunition-sustainable weapons at high difficulty and be wary of melee once elite waves arrive. Build armour around cost reduction, movement speed and cooldown, ignore shield stats, and keep a separate building loadout. Spend quickbar items freely, chain your movement skill everywhere, and remember the mech is the mobile reserve the whole defensive plan is built around.

Enemies

Damage type decides fights here more than damage number does. A weapon that is nullified against one species doubles against another, and the tower line that shredded last wave can be irrelevant to this one — which is why the answer to almost every defensive question is coverage.

Reading resistances

Six damage types are tracked — physical, area, energy, cryo, fire and acid — and every creature has a resistance profile and a vulnerability profile against them. Resistance reduces incoming damage of that type, with full resistance meaning immunity; vulnerability multiplies it, so a doubled vulnerability means twice damage.

Most species run a ladder of variants with rising health but the same resistances, which is convenient: a weapon that works on the base creature keeps working on its stronger versions. The exceptions are worth memorising because they invert your answer mid-fight — one common species changes which type it nullifies between its variants, and two creatures have boss forms with different profiles from their trash versions, one of which gains fire immunity and one of which loses most of its fire vulnerability.

What the table means for your build

Several patterns fall out of it, and they explain most defensive advice you will read.

Fire is the widest vulnerability and the most frequently nullified. It answers the entire small-organic bracket and two of the biggest health pools in the game at six times damage — but it is negated outright by the lava-blooded species and heavily resisted by several others. Fire is a specialist you should always have some of and never rely on alone.

Acid answers the rocky, armoured bracket — precisely the heavies that tear walls apart — and is nullified by the organic bracket that fire handles. That complementarity is exactly why acid towers and flame towers appear together in every good wall.

Cryo is the safest broad type. It answers a long list of creatures, only one species resists it at all, and it slows everything it touches, which is a defensive benefit no other type provides.

Energy is the weakest general-purpose type by this data — one species is genuinely vulnerable to it while five resist it — which is worth remembering before committing an entire wall to lasers and railguns. Energy earns its place on penetration and on zero ammunition cost, not on damage type.

The practical shortcut: a complementary two-element mix covers the roster, but only if the two are chosen properly. Cryo and physical work together because nothing in the base roster resists both. Fire and acid do not, because one creature resists both and the rest split neatly into two camps that each nullify one of them.

One more habit worth building: acid-natured creatures take extra damage from fire and fire-natured creatures take extra from acid, so as a first guess in an unfamiliar biome, bring the opposite element to whatever the environment is made of.

Omega creatures

Omegas are stronger strains carrying extra abilities, shown as icons above the boss health bar. Every ability has four levels, and the level is tied directly to your chosen difficulty rather than to the boss or your progress — so a Brutal run faces top-level versions from the first Omega it meets.

The combination rules are strict and worth knowing, because they bound how bad any single Omega can be. No Omega carries two copies of the same ability, an Omega can carry at most one direct-attack ability, and at most one elemental damage aura. Wall-spawning is incompatible with every aura and with almost every direct attack. The two support auras — healing and resistance — cannot appear together. What is not bounded is the number of Omegas: two can show up.

Behaviour you can exploit

  • Everything attacks the closest structure. This is the single most exploitable fact in the game and underpins bait clusters, decoy walls and outlying firebases.
  • Creatures do not flank. The rear of a fortress needs a token defence, not a mirror of the front.
  • Some creatures hit through walls, and spitters prioritise turrets. Depth of wall does not protect a turret from everything.
  • Enemy artillery cannot hit roofed buildings from certain angles, which makes a packed building block a partial shield in itself.
  • Power-leech creatures attach to cables and buildings, which is a good reason to run redundant lines to remote sites — and they make convenient stationary scan targets.
  • Areas covered by radar or power lines suppress respawns. Clear an area first, then run coverage through it, and it stays clear.
  • Most creatures cannot cross water or mud, so those areas are effectively walls; defend only their edges, and the few crossers die to a couple of basic turrets.
  • Diggers path toward your base rather than toward you, so getting behind them and firing is safe where meeting them head-on is not.
  • Nest eggs contain rare resources — destroy them before they hatch and the nest pays you.

Recap

Learn resistance profiles rather than damage numbers: fire for the organic bracket, acid for the armoured one, cryo as the broad safe answer, physical for the energy-immune, and energy chosen for penetration rather than for its damage type. Pick complementary pairs — cryo with physical rather than fire with acid. Against Omegas, identify the ability first: kill resistance and healing auras immediately, kite area denial away from your walls, and remember that gas answers drones, splash answers swarms, and power jump answers being walled in.

Biomes

Each biome changes three things at once: which power source works, which damage types are worth carrying, and what the weather does to your base. Treating a new map like the last one is the fastest way to lose a well-built run.

The biomes

Tropical & Jungle The default. Water, cobalt, easiest waves

The story campaign starts here deliberately — it is the most Earth-like environment and the safest place to learn. Expect frequent large carbonium and ironium deposits, a moderate number of geothermal vents, and relatively few sludge lakes but plenty of mud.

That mud-to-sludge ratio matters more than it sounds. Mud converts to water at twice the efficiency of sludge, so the correct division of labour is mud for water and sludge for gas. Geothermal is the standout power source where vents exist, since it produces both energy and mud.

Jungle also carries the cobalt crops and the most open space for farms, and it produces the mildest waves of any biome. If you are choosing a home for a large cultivator operation, this is the default answer. Trees can be cleared with nuclear weapons to free up building squares.

Desert & Radioactive Solar, storage, uranium — and quicksand

Solar output is doubled here, which is the biome's headline advantage, but vents and sludge are scarce and water is scarce enough that water-fed power belongs elsewhere. Nuclear works to bootstrap a radioactive map, after which imported gas is infinite and consumes nothing.

Quicksand damages buildings until stable floors are researched, which is the gate on using the biome at all. Once through it, the endless flat space becomes the game's best storage park — solid storage needs no power, so you can carpet floors and storage with zero supporting infrastructure and never visit again.

Energy weapons underperform here. The uranium cultivation crops are desert-locked, and cold damage is the recommended element for local fauna. Several desert maps carry very low threat, which is what makes them the standard recovery site for a broken economy: solar panels plus synthesizers, no defence required.

Acidic Plains Infinite sludge, no raids, and acid that hits like a nuke

The most valuable biome in the campaign, for one reason: the sheer volume of sludge makes gas power effectively unlimited, which in turn makes synthesizers, laboratories and ammunition production affordable at any scale. Several acidic recon maps generate no attack waves at all, which turns them into free industrial estates — cover them in radar so creatures stop respawning, and build everything that travels between maps there.

Wind is poor here. A wind-heavy grid on an acid map is a common hidden cause of collapsing defences; solar plus cultivated biomass is the correction if you have not yet reached gas.

Acid damage from the environment is severe, and shields are useless against it because the damage-over-time strips them continuously — health regeneration and health boosts are the survivable answer, along with anti-corrosive armour. Acid rain is fully negated once the acid-shielding upgrade is researched. Bring movement speed: local mushrooms react badly to metal, and some maps hide mines that the geoscanner doubles as a detector for. Defusing one mine can set off another, so give minefields room and clear them at range.

The yeast root colony mission is the biome's hardest content and inverts normal play. Find the root immediately, because the growth spreads slowly then explodes, and every delay costs resources to clear. Burn everything around it, lay acid-resistant tiles wide enough to hold — a narrow strip regrows through — use sludge pools and rock as parts of your perimeter, and place the research building only once your defences satisfy you, because placing it starts the waves. Flamethrowers clear plant fields fastest; melee keeps the fast acid creatures off you; sniping weapons handle the smaller roots. Leaving a map freezes its state, so retreating to re-equip is entirely viable.

Volcanic Doubled geothermal, magma power, no fire weapons

Fire is worse than useless here — the local fauna is highly fire-resistant, which is a cruel joke given that the region supplies fire-weapon materials. Bring cryo and acid instead; acid also dissolves the floating rock formations that clutter the terrain, and a nuclear launcher carrying acid damage is the fastest way to clear them.

Power is abundant in two forms. Magma plants run on an infinite local fuel with no processing, though each needs a cooling tower nearby to function and magma is far too consumption-heavy to export. Less obviously, geothermal output is doubled here, which makes vents a genuine powerhouse and provides the mud that cultivators need. A couple of vents will run a substantial farm.

The costs are space and hazards: magma pools make layouts cramped, eruptions demand repair coverage on top of the cooling infrastructure, and titanium crops are the biome's cultivation speciality. Volcanic maps produce the hardest waves of the common biomes.

Fungal Swamp Everything burns — except your acid towers' targets

Nearly everything here is fire-weak, which makes overhead fire and flame towers exceptional and makes this the one biome where you genuinely want a lot of them. Acid towers are correspondingly poor, since the local fauna grew up in it.

The nest variant here spits acid from the structure itself, so approaching one is a real fight rather than a formality. Floating towers dropped into the biome's pools need no power connection at all, which removes the failure where one severed cable silences a line.

Metallic Valley Morphium, fire immunity, and towers that need no power

Morphium is the draw: it powers plants at roughly gas output with no processing chain, and it runs morphium towers, which build on ground or liquid, carry built-in shield generators, and need neither power nor AI cores. Using either elsewhere requires liquid compression to ship the morphium home.

The local creatures are fire-resistant, which specifically blunts overhead fire towers — and once you finish this content those creatures join your headquarters raids, so a fire-heavy home defence quietly degrades. Most of them also fly over dash trails, removing that tool.

The nest here is a defended structure ringed with alien plasma and artillery towers. Dismantling those towers pays resources, which makes it one of the better fights in the game rather than a chore.

Caverns Where most of the usual advice inverts

Do not come here until you have a full loadout and access to essentially every resource type — the creatures are substantially stronger than anything before them. Most resist cryo and many resist fire as well, so the spear and the flamethrower both go back in the locker; acid is the element that works, including on your dash.

Artillery is not available on these maps, so portal bomb towers take over the long-range role. Orbital abilities do not function underground either; the anti-swarm tool becomes the antimatter ball, and generator overcharge replaces the emergency detonation while also restoring shields.

Power is scarce and the answer is import: geothermal converting mud to water bootstraps you but cannot keep up, so a decompressor line from an acidic base is the real solution. Environmental hazards are frequent enough that repair towers are mandatory, falling ceiling crystals behave like meteor showers, and destructible terrain must never be treated as a wall because creatures simply dig through it. Clear the ground beyond your walls so towers get firing time, and never fight the large diggers head-on — they path toward your base, so get behind them.

Wind is of limited use here, and caves are dense with exploding plants, so free-swinging a melee weapon through vegetation is genuinely lethal.

Cryo Coolant exports, and a sword that is not enough

The cold biome's fauna is dangerous in melee — a handful of ordinary creatures in contact is a death sentence, so kiting and ranged weapons carry you rather than a sword.

Its strategic value is supercooling liquid. A cryo base exporting coolant makes several late structures dramatically cheaper to run, including fusion reactors fed on coolant rather than water and the endgame structure whose refinery upkeep is otherwise punitive.

Weather and events

Some hazards can be baited and some cannot, and knowing which is which saves bases.

Telegraphed impact events — meteor falls, volcanic strikes — spawn centred on your mech and give around thirty seconds of warning. Walk out of your base before they begin and the entire event lands harmlessly in empty terrain; you can even leave once it has started and the impacts follow you rather than hitting your buildings. Earthquakes, gas-quakes and tornadoes are the opposite: fully random across the map, with no way to lure them away. Repair coverage designed into the base from the start handles almost all of it, with the notable exception that tornadoes still destroy power connectors and pipelines, which are too flimsy to survive.

Not all events are hostile. Strong winds boost turbine output, comets deliver valuable resources, and ion storms dump large amounts of energy and fully recharge storage — worth banking against if your map gets them often. Hail storms destroy buildings while leaving alien flora and fauna untouched, which is an argument for siting cultivators remotely with nothing beside them but harvesters and a repair tower.

If randomness of this kind is not what you want from a run, the custom settings let you disable negative events entirely while keeping the positive ones.

Choosing where to settle

Beyond resources, biome affects wave pressure directly. Every biome has its own guaranteed minimum gap between waves, so pacing genuinely differs between two runs on the same difficulty. On overall wave difficulty the common ordering runs jungle as the mildest, then desert, then plains, with volcanic the hardest — and acidic plains is the reference case difficulty is tuned against.

Remember also that settling a biome imports its creatures into your headquarters waves. A tour of every biome produces a home defence problem with no clean answer, while a focused set of outposts keeps your wall's damage-type requirements narrow. That is a real argument for taking fewer, better outposts than the eight you are allowed.

Expansion content and how it arrives

Expansion regions are not separate campaigns; they hook into the existing one through added maps and a mission chain, and they unlock as you progress rather than being available from the menu. The metallic content in particular begins with a meteor event: complete a scan mission or a mining outpost, return to your main base, and a marked impact fires the chain. On a save where the campaign is already finished, it arrives within minutes of loading.

Timing reports vary widely — some players trigger it a couple of minutes in, others after hours — and the new creature types can start appearing in your home waves before the mission chain itself begins. Do not walk past the fragment when it lands; picking it up and following through immediately is the difference between starting the chain and wondering for hours why nothing happened. Failures to trigger at all are reported often enough to be treated as bugs rather than unmet prerequisites, and neither reloading nor cycling maps reliably fixes a stuck spawn.

Leave expansion biomes until last in your outpost ordering. Their creatures are the strongest in the game, their resistances invalidate tools you have relied on all run, and their content assumes you arrive with a mature economy.

Recap

Match power to biome first — gas on acidic, magma and doubled geothermal on volcanic, solar in desert, imported liquids in caverns — because a mismatched grid starves everything downstream. Carry the element the local fauna does not share: fire on acid maps, acid on volcanic ones, cold in the desert, and acid again underground. Bait the telegraphed hazards by walking away and design repair coverage against the ones you cannot bait. Settle deliberately, because every biome you hold sends its creatures to your front door.

Modes

Campaign and survival are not the same game at two difficulties. They reward opposite skills, punish opposite mistakes, and the strategies that carry one will get you killed in the other. Knowing which game you are playing is the first strategic decision.

Story campaign — an untimed tempo puzzle

Campaign is open-ended. With no clock you can build several complete bases, finish most of both tech trees, and choose your own rate of advance — and because difficulty scales off your progress rather than the wall clock, that choice is the core of the mode. Campaign is the strategic game: the wave curve is something you author.

The flip side is that campaign at default settings is genuinely gentle. Many players find the attacking creatures close to meaningless, which makes combat and defence decisions feel weightless. If that is your experience, the fix is not to grind harder but to raise the wave multiplier or start a custom run — the mode has all the levers.

Campaign's real appeal is the world: hopping between biomes, standing up outposts across them, and running a logistics network no other mode offers. Play it first, ideally more than once, and treat survival as the endgame challenge.

Survival — a timed sprint you cannot out-plan

Survival compresses everything. Attacks arrive quickly and often, wave tiers ramp roughly an order of magnitude faster than the story mode, and the goal is to hold ninety minutes until the portal. You can continue past that point, but only to see how far the curve goes.

Everything else follows from the clock. You are on one map, in one biome chosen at the start, with every resource you will ever get inside it — no expedition layer, no biome-hopping, no outpost network to absorb waves. You will not finish the tech tree; realistically you end the run on tier two or three weapons and towers, so research choices are about which two or three things you actually need, not about eventual completeness. The loop is defend and rebuild rather than build and expand, and the mech's respawn works as normal provided the HQ is standing.

The mode's pacing lever is the timer setting: extending the run is the only way to reach higher-tier tech. Survival is also framed in the fiction as a training simulation, which is the in-world reason the bioscanner does not function there — with knock-on consequences for the whole rare-resource economy covered under Economy.

Economy first, fortress second

The survival opening is a scaling race, not a defensive one. The timer punishes a slow ramp far harder than thin walls punish you, so mine greedily, spread wide, accept exposure, and let the mech cover the gaps. Selling refunds full value, so anything about to be overrun is refundable rather than defensible — sell it and rebuild after. The crunch is entirely in the pre-economy window; once income is flowing the difficulty drops a full tier.

Open campaign — campaign without the rails

Open campaign keeps campaign's structure and removes its guidance. You choose any biome to start in, including hostile ones, and each start is a genuinely different opening because you begin with only basic technology and must research everything else in whatever order that biome's problems demand. A radioactive desert start, for example, hands you effectively unlimited power from boosted solar performance but makes early construction miserable thanks to omnipresent quicksand and radiation.

Research is unlocked by species familiarity rather than by story missions, and every requirement is stated on the research screen, so the whole route is plannable in advance instead of gated behind objectives you have not seen. There is exactly one objective — build the prime rift station — and mission generation opens up for all biomes as soon as the orbital scanner is up. Both extremes are supported: colonise the entire planet, or beeline the portal and go home.

It is not an easier mode. Wildlife is exactly as hostile, difficulty selection works as normal, and the portal-opening event still buries you in enemies from every direction, so the endgame still has to be prepared for. All biomes are accessible without owning any DLC, which for base-game players is the first time metallic valley, crystal caverns and fungal swamp resources are usable in a campaign at all.

The endgame fight

The final portal defence is a different problem from routine wave defence, and it fails in ways that have nothing to do with your tower count.

The first is a supply failure. The rift charge consumes plasma and supercharged plasma, and if the supply dries up the charge simply reports that it was interrupted, with no explanation, even with your base intact and no enemy near it. Secure plasma throughput before you start the charge — a stalled charge at sixty percent with a healthy base is the classic version of this loss.

The second is concentration. Because waves target whatever sits closest to the map border, several small heavily defended outposts scattered around the map divide the final wave between them instead of letting the whole thing land on your HQ. Each only needs a small walled footprint, enough repair towers to cover it entirely, and a tower mix built around plasma and artillery. Layer the walls in separate lines with gaps between them rather than one thick slab.

The third is your own role. Movement speed is the most valuable stat in that fight. Keep a teleporter at every outpost, hop to whichever is under pressure, and use the mech on the enemy's back line — ranged and artillery units — while towers handle everything in melee. Keep moving: a large slice of the herd will chase you, which frees your turrets, and you naturally outrun the heavy melee units that would otherwise be the ones killing you. Drop gravity mines and nukes along your retreat path so the pursuers eat them, and bind grenades to spare mouse buttons; multiple skills can share a button, so one press can throw two grenades or drop several mines. Small two-or-three-layer boxes with a couple of turrets, built purely to be chewed on, buy the seconds you need to work the herd over at range.

Anticlimax warning

The final rift-opening wave has been reported not to scale with difficulty or wave multiplier, so a 500 % run can end on a wave that feels smaller than the routine attacks that preceded it. Do not read a comfortable final fight as proof your defences were adequate. Note too that after the gate sequence there are no further waves at all — if you want to keep fighting on that save, stop before triggering it.

Recap

Campaign is untimed and scales off your progress, so it rewards deliberate pacing and a wide outpost network. Survival is timed and scales off the clock, so it rewards a fast economy, a thin defence and a mech that carries fights personally. Open campaign trades story gating for familiarity gating and free biome choice without lowering the threat. The final portal defence is won by securing plasma supply, splitting the wave across scattered outposts, and playing the mech as a mobile response element rather than a turret.

Brutal & Custom

Brutal is a knowledge check before it is a build check, and the custom sliders are the most under-used feature in the game. Between them they let you dial in a run that is hard in the way you want it to be hard — and avoid one that is merely tedious.

What Brutal actually changes

The headline change is unit quality rather than unit count. The ordinary trash creature you shrug off on lower difficulties is replaced by an elite variant with more than twenty times the health. Almost everything else is comparable, but that one substitution means roughly five times the tower count to hold the same wave — a multiple to plan your defence budget around, not something you patch with incremental additions.

Because tower count rises that steeply, ammunition throughput becomes the real ceiling. Ten or more ammo factories is not excessive on Brutal; towers that cannot fire are decorations, and starving a newly unlocked artillery line is the most common way to waste a tech investment.

Note also that the difficulty tiers were raised across the board in a later patch: current Hard sits roughly where Brutal used to, so older video guides show weaker waves than you will face — tier two spawns where you now get tier three. The principles in them still hold; the numbers do not. The Hard-to-Brutal jump specifically is very steep, and there is no shame in using custom settings to land between them.

The knowledge checklist

Experienced Brutal players reduce the mode to five habits: know each enemy's weaknesses and abilities and exploit them; site the base to use terrain rather than open ground; kite competently so monsters chase you past your towers without landing hits on them; handle several fronts at once; and never let research idle, with a priority order decided in advance. None of those is a building.

Brutal-specific tactics

Walls everywhere, not just at the base. Isolated wall segments scattered across attack paths and at map edges split waves — part of the swarm peels off to attack a lone wall far from anything, breaking a single overwhelming push into manageable pieces. This is at its strongest in the early game, when you have the least real defence.

Throwaway wall mazes in front of the real line do the same job at close range: enemies stop to chew, and artillery does its best work against a stalled clump. Depth beats thickness — walls in separate lines with gaps, not one enormous slab.

The mech's death explosion is a legitimate early weapon. Diving into a pack and letting the machine blow has decent radius and force, and it kills stone monsters before you have any tower or ammo economy capable of the job.

Omega creatures are the defining Brutal problem, and the answer is presence: be personally on whichever side one is attacking. Late waves will still damage defence points depending on where the heavy ranged units spawn, and the response is to teleport between points, soften the ranged units with grenades, and repair.

Melee weapons become a liability as the run advances. Players who drop them for ranged loadouts consistently report surviving fights they previously lost, because standing still in contact with an elite wave is not survivable at Brutal enemy damage.

The tempo exploit and its limits

Campaign at extreme multipliers is beatable primarily because you control wave scaling through your HQ level. The playbook is uncomfortable but effective: scout widely before building anything, site the HQ centrally near several carbonium and ironium deposits, then spend days building nothing but economy — mines on every nearby node, batteries, then solar, then wind as you expand, then storage — while holding the HQ at level one because a level-one attack wave is trivially defended.

From there you farm every available research before each HQ upgrade, build a ring of a dozen defence points around the map at the corners and edge midpoints, and only advance when the next tier is fully paid for. It is slow and it feels like grinding, and that is the point: the difficulty is a tempo problem rather than a skill problem, and grinding is the solution to a tempo problem.

Two caveats keep this honest. It does not transfer to survival, where there is no scaling lever at all — Brutal survival at even doubled multipliers is far harder than Brutal campaign at quintupled ones. And the wave multiplier is the forgiving knob; the enemy damage multiplier is the genuinely dangerous one, since even a mature endgame defence sits close to its limit at normal damage.

Reading the custom sliders

The custom panel is not a difficulty slider with extra steps. It exposes combat pressure, economy generosity and wave pacing as separate axes, which is what makes it useful: you can run lethal combat with a generous economy, or a stingy economy with mild combat, and get two completely different games out of the same map.

Presets worth stealing

Three developer configurations are instructive because each isolates a different skill. An iron man setup tests mech combat by keeping you under-powered — doubled research time, doubled building cost and build duration, brutal waves at triple multiplier — while a long warmup and a comfortable eight-minute interval keep it beatable. A no mercy setup inverts that: technology and buildings almost free, infinite deposits, but enemy damage at five hundred percent, waves at five times multiplier on a four-minute interval, and six items dropped per death — you cannot take a hit, so base defence has to do everything. A co-op intensity setup uses constant overlapping waves with cheap everything and reduced enemy damage, so nobody is ever idle.

For campaign specifically, the two useful directions are opposites. Raising research cost and research time steers a run toward economy, megabase and outpost play. Lowering building costs while raising attacks per wave steers it toward action. Both are more interesting than simply raising the difficulty label.

The community favourites are worth knowing too: maxing wave count while minimising research time, build time and build cost with resources at extreme density produces a run where thousands of enemies arrive frequently and genuinely enormous defences are both necessary and affordable. At the other end, unlimited deposits plus a long headstart removes scarcity management entirely and turns the game into a base-design and research exercise — a legitimate choice, not a lesser one.

Emulating the mode you actually want

The most common complaint is structural: campaign has the world and survival has the tension. Survival's custom panel gets you partway to a hybrid — an infinite time limit turns it into a long-term build rather than a sprint, the largest map size restores some sense of expansion, spacing the waves out preserves lethality while giving breathing room, and pairing increased resource yield with reduced deposit frequency forces you to spread across the map instead of turtling on a dense start. What it cannot give you is the multi-biome outpost network, which remains campaign's alone.

Balance mods fill some of the remaining gaps. Multiplier-based tweak suites let you retune resource yields, power output, research speed and mech performance independently, along with shared build cost, build time, building health and unit caps — with sensible exceptions kept out of scope, such as the headquarters, rift station and elevator caps, and buildings that would open duplication loops. Note that changing deposit sizes interacts oddly with survival's automatic replenishment. Elsewhere, a destructible-rock mod is popular for clean base layouts, at the cost of losing the free chokepoints rocks provide, and range-extended power connectors reduce the busywork of cabling remote sites. Quality-of-life picks around scan speed and build convenience change the tedium level without touching the challenge.

Recap

Brutal replaces trash units with elites, which multiplies your required tower count roughly fivefold and makes ammunition throughput the true bottleneck. Win it with knowledge — enemy weaknesses, terrain siting, kiting, multi-front management and uninterrupted research — plus scattered walls to split waves and personal presence against Omegas. In campaign, control the tempo by holding your HQ level down while your economy compounds, and remember that the trick does not exist in survival. When you tune a custom run, treat combat, economy and pacing as three independent axes, raise enemy damage last, and leave the sandbox panel enabled.
