Risk of Rain 2

Survivors, items, proc chains and the race against the difficulty clock — how to build runs that survive the loop.

The Clock

Every other decision in Risk of Rain 2 is downstream of one question: is your power growing faster than the difficulty coefficient? The coefficient never pauses, never reverses, and compounds. Understanding exactly what feeds it is what separates players who "get good items sometimes" from players who reliably reach the loop.

What actually drives difficulty

The game tracks a single hidden number — the difficulty coefficient — recalculated every second. Three things feed it: how many players are in the run, how many minutes have elapsed, and how many stages you have completed. The first is fixed at the start. The other two are yours to manage, and they are not equivalent.

Time adds to the coefficient linearly. Completing a stage multiplies it by 1.15. That difference is the single most important fact in the game. Ten minutes of standing on stage 1 nudges the number; five stage transitions multiply it by roughly two. Because stage completions compound, the cost of each additional stage grows — the jump from stage 12 to 13 is far larger in absolute terms than the jump from 2 to 3, even though both are ×1.15.

The practical reading: looting is cheap, travelling is expensive. Sitting still on a map you have already cleared is pure waste, but sitting on a map you are still extracting value from costs you far less than the transition itself will. Players who blitz stages to "outrun the clock" have it backwards — they are accelerating the term that compounds while skipping the items that would have paid for it.

Where the coefficient shows up

It is not an abstraction. Enemy level, chest prices, and enemy gold rewards all read directly off the coefficient. Chest cost scales with it raised to the 1.25 power, so prices inflate faster than difficulty does — one reason late-stage looting on a thin wallet is miserable. Enemy gold scales linearly with it, which is why teleporter events on stage 6 fund an entire map when the same event on stage 1 barely buys two chests.

The spawn director's budget

Enemies are not spawned at random density. A director accumulates credits proportional to the coefficient and periodically spends them on a group of up to four enemies drawn from the stage's pool. Cheap enemies cost little; bosses cost a lot; elites cost 6× the base monster (Blazing, Overloading, Glacial) or 36× (Malachite, Celestine).

Two consequences matter for play. First, the director refuses to spawn groups that are far too cheap for its budget, which is why Lemurians thin out and then vanish entirely as a run progresses — the early-game trash literally prices itself out of the spawn table. Second, elites appear because the director can finally afford them, not because of an arbitrary timer. If you are seeing Malachites, your coefficient has crossed a threshold and everything else on the map got worse at the same time.

This is also why the enemy composition on a late stage feels qualitatively different rather than merely tougher. You are not fighting stronger Lemurians; you are fighting a completely different, more expensive roster that the director could not previously afford.

Rush or loot? The honest answer

The community has argued this for years and both camps are describing real experiences. The resolution is that the correct pace is not a constant — it is a function of how far ahead of the curve you currently are.

Think of time as currency spent on lottery tickets. Each chest is a ticket; each minute spent earning the gold for it accrues interest in the form of difficulty. If the prizes you draw are worth more than the interest, you are ahead. If they aren't, you are in debt, and debt in this game is not repaid gradually — it is repaid all at once when a stage rolls a combination you cannot handle.

So: when you are behind, take risks; when you are ahead, bank the lead. A run with a thin item stock should be pushing Void Fields, slamming Mountain Shrines and clearing every chest, because the alternative is dying to the curve anyway. A run that is already comfortable should stop full-clearing — take a handful of items per stage and move, because the marginal item is worth less than the marginal minute once your build is functional.

Where exactly that line sits depends on how much difficulty you can absorb through play skill. On base Monsoon and up through the low Eclipse levels, aggressive risk-taking pays off, because the game remains survivable while you are behind and the upside is large. From Eclipse 4 upward the calculus inverts: you are permanently fighting uphill, good runs are scarce, and protecting the one you have is worth more than any item you might draw.

Know when to fold

The most common way to lose a strong run is greed on a map you had already won. Once your build works, every extra circuit of the stage is a coin flip where the downside is the whole run and the upside is one more white item. Take a few pickups and leave.

Stage 1 is the cheapest risk you will ever take

Time on stage 1 is the least valuable time in the run — the coefficient is at its floor, so a minute costs almost nothing, and the emotional cost of losing a 4-minute run is nil. That makes stage 1 the correct place to be greedy.

Open by scouting rather than by fighting. Spawns are slow early and you have to explore anyway, so map the chest locations first and then commit to the densest cluster instead of sprinting across the map for a single container. Hit Combat and Blood Shrines as soon as you find them — they convert your time directly into gold without waiting for the spawn director to feed you kills. Check for Newt altars while you roam.

Set a floor rather than a clock: never leave stage 1 with fewer than 4 items, even if that means starting the teleporter late. Beyond that floor, roughly 3:00–4:00 is a healthy teleporter start. Arriving on stage 2 fast enough matters for one concrete reason — Rallypoint Delta has a timed chest containing a Preon Accumulator, and it locks out if you dawdle.

Do not chase distant single items. The map will always contain one more chest tucked behind terrain, and the walk there is pure coefficient with no compensation. There are plenty of items next stage.

Pacing after stage 1

The rule of thumb is a floor of about 3 items per stage, never zero, with the pace set by your current lead. If stage 1 went long but paid well, claw the time back by rushing stage 2 — a big lead purchased with a lot of minutes should be converted into a fast transition. If stage 1 was thin, keep farming until the map is genuinely exhausted.

From stage 3 onward the economics shift in favour of full clears. Enemies are worth meaningfully more gold, the maps are larger, and the item density is higher. The efficient method is to pick a point and run one large circle rather than criss-crossing: a circuit spreads out enemy spawns behind you instead of concentrating a pursuing mob in one place, which is a survival benefit as much as a speed one.

Bank gold before starting the teleporter and clear any non-teleporter boss that has already spawned. The event itself will pay for the remaining chests, so leaving purchases until after the event is strictly more efficient than buying beforehand. Chests near the teleporter in particular should be deliberately left for the post-event flush — the small EXP loss is irrelevant since your level falls behind the ambient level eventually regardless.

By the time you reach the loop the economy has fully inverted. A single teleporter event pays for essentially every interactable on the map, so the optimal line on arrival is to go straight to the teleporter, complete it, and then buy everything worth having with the proceeds.

Buying more tickets

The bottleneck early is almost never chest availability — it is gold. Most of the minutes lost on stages 1 and 2 are spent grinding enemies to afford containers that are already sitting there.

The clean fix is Brittle Crown, which converts hits taken into gold. It compresses the grind dramatically, and the money-loss-on-hit downside barely registers if you are continuously spending rather than hoarding. It is awkward on stages where you must spend health for items, so buy early in a stage or wait until after the teleporter event. With enough stacks and enough dodging, it makes the stage 4 legendary chest affordable without grinding time for it — a genuinely different way to route a run.

Brittle Crown also has a training effect worth naming: if your goal is climbing Eclipse, it punishes getting hit with a resource you actually feel, which teaches damage avoidance faster than raw HP loss does. Defiant Gouge does something similar but is strictly worse — it depends on shrines spawning and forces additional fights on you.

Teleporter rushing as a deliberate strategy is built on this. With two or more Brittle Crown stacks and the skill to not get hit, you finish the event immediately on arrival, then use the crown-inflated event money to buy the whole map at once. It folds all your grinding into the event and eliminates fighting-for-money entirely. One-shot protection widens the error margin considerably — Safer Spaces is ideal, Oddly-Shaped Opal helps, Tougher Times is too random to rely on but does no harm. Since gold lost per hit scales with damage taken, flat mitigation like Repulsion Armor Plate or Jade Elephant also protects your wallet, not just your health bar.

Focused Convergence deserves a mention as a pure time purchase: shrinking the teleporter radius shortens the event meaningfully. On Eclipse 2 and above the smaller bubble becomes a liability, but below that it is a clean trade.

Free time and free stages

A few places give you progress without charging the clock, and they are always worth taking.

The Gilded Coast does not advance the coefficient at all — no time pressure, no stage penalty on the way in. It is a free four chests plus a Titanic Knurl, gated only on your ability to beat Aurelionite. Take it whenever you can afford the altar without grinding for it, or when the Lunar Seer offers it.

On Siren's Call, if killing the teleporter boss quickly is a problem, start the teleporter and fight the Alloy Worship Unit at the same time — the two events overlap fine, and you can abandon the AWU if it turns against you.

Multiplayer and the coefficient

Every additional player raises the coefficient's starting value and steepens its growth, and disconnected players still count. The starting bump is barely visible going from one to two players and severe in a large lobby — a full group begins the run at a difficulty a solo player does not reach for several minutes.

The compounding also hits harder: each stage transition in a big lobby is a much larger absolute jump. Large groups therefore cannot afford to skip loot, and coordination on who takes which item stops being optional.

Note that hosting a lobby by yourself with nobody joining does not raise scaling — a private multiplayer session is mathematically identical to singleplayer.

Not all farming costs the same

Killing enemies for items — as with Artifact of Sacrifice — does not spike difficulty the way clearing stages does. That is what makes long farming sessions on an early stage viable: you are buying items with the cheap resource (time) instead of the expensive one (stage transitions).

The difficulty ladder

Drizzle halves the rate at which time feeds scaling, hands you 70 armor, and boosts starting health regen. Rainstorm is the unmodified baseline. Monsoon runs time at 150% and cuts starting regen to 60%, and is the only initial difficulty where Deicide, The Calm and the survivor mastery skins are obtainable — if you want the alternate skins, you have no choice about the difficulty.

The visible bar passes through named tiers from Easy up to HAHAHAHA, and each tier contains two intermediate notches. Every notch and every tier boundary levels the enemy team: +30% health and +20% damage per level, the same rates players get. HAHAHAHA caps the displayed ambient level at 99, but the coefficient itself keeps climbing behind the cap — enemy stats stop scaling while spawn budgets and prices do not.

A useful self-check on Monsoon for players who intend to clear every chest: finishing stage 1 on Easy, stage 2 on Hard, stage 3 on "I SEE YOU" and stage 4 on HAHAHAHA is roughly the pace a full-clear run should hold. If you cannot dodge well enough to hold that, fall back to leaving each stage after a handful of items.

Recap

Difficulty is a single compounding number fed mainly by stage completions, with time as a slower linear contributor. Loot generously while the coefficient is low, tighten up once your build works, and treat every stage transition as the expensive purchase it is. Set item floors rather than clock targets, solve the early gold bottleneck with Brittle Crown if you can dodge, and take the free stages that don't charge you anything. Above all: when the run is winning, stop gambling with it.

Items & Stacking

Two copies of an item are almost never twice as good as one, and occasionally they're worse. Which of those applies depends on the item's stacking type — a hidden property the tooltip does not tell you, and the single biggest source of wasted picks in the game.

The four stacking curves

Everything except equipment and Beads of Fealty stacks, but the shape of the curve varies. Knowing which shape an item follows tells you whether the eleventh copy is worth picking up.

Linear is the default and the majority. Each copy adds the same absolute amount. The trap is that constant absolute growth means collapsing relative growth: going from one Soldier's Syringe to two doubles your bonus; going from fifty to fifty-one adds almost nothing to what you already have. Linear items are excellent early and functionally dead once deeply stacked.

Hyperbolic covers chance-to-block and chance-to-trigger effects that would break the game at 100%. The formula asymptotes — it approaches certainty without ever arriving. Tougher Times is the canonical case: one copy blocks 13.04%, not the advertised 15%, and ten copies block 60%, not 150%. Picture a box holding 100 red tickets, with each item adding 15 green ones; more greens improve your odds but a red draw remains possible forever. These items diminish brutally — fifty to fifty-one Tougher Times moves your block chance by two tenths of a percent.

Reciprocal is the same math without the complement, so the curve is mirrored: strongest at one stack, weaker with every addition, never reaching zero. Item text describing "stacking reduces the effect by 50%" is only accurate for the second copy — the third leaves you at 33% strength, not the 25% the description implies. A variant divides by stacks-plus-one so the first copy still does something when the item affects a value unrelated to its own effect.

Exponential is the rare and dangerous one, where stacks multiply each other. Shaped Glass doubles damage and halves health per copy, so two copies are ×4 and ÷4, not ×3 and ÷3. Crucially, exponential items have no diminishing returns at all — going from five to six Shaped Glass doubles your entire accumulated multiplier. That is why a handful of exponential items outclasses a mountain of linear ones late in a run.

Items can also mix curves. Fuel Cell is linear in charges (+1 each) and exponential in cooldown (×0.85 each) simultaneously, which is why it feels like it scales forever even though half of it is a flat addition.

The tooltip lies

Listed values frequently do not describe the real effect, especially for non-linear stackers. Never reason by multiplying the displayed number by your stack count. And note that which variable scales differs per item even among items that look identical: AtG Missile and Tri-Tip Dagger both trigger on hit, but stacking AtG raises the damage while stacking Tri-Tip raises the chance.

Knowing when a stack is finished

Everything hard-caps at the 32-bit integer limit, but many items reach an effective ceiling far earlier, past which additional copies are pure waste. Recognising these ceilings is what lets you redirect picks toward something that still scales.

Linear chance-to-trigger items dead-end at 100%. Ten Lens-Maker's Glasses is exactly enough for guaranteed crits — and only nine if you also carry Harvester's Scythe or Predatory Instincts, each of which contributes a flat 5% that does not itself scale. The one exception is Railgunner, whose passive converts surplus crit chance into crit damage, so glasses never stop paying for her.

Percentage-healing items cap where they reach 100% max HP restored — Medkit at 20, Monster Tooth at 50, Bustling Fungus at 44. Rejuvenation Rack lowers those caps exponentially, while Aegis raises them: exactly one Aegis triples the ceiling, two roughly doubles it, with diminishing effect beyond.

Multi-target items cap against the map's entity limit of 45 simultaneous enemies — around 22 Ukuleles or 21 Razorwires exhausts the target count, though their radius keeps growing indefinitely and can still be worth something.

A few items have genuinely hard limits. Focused Convergence stops at 3 copies with no further effect. Fuel Cell caps at 255 charges. Shaped Glass tops out at 127 — picking up a 128th kills you instantly.

War Horn's cap is conditional rather than fixed: it is finished the moment its buff duration exceeds your equipment cooldown, giving permanent uptime. With a 60-second equipment that's about 14 copies; with a 15-second equipment it's three. The same conditional logic applies to Alien Head, whose useful count depends on your longest-cooldown skill — roughly nine to fifteen depending on survivor, and only Artificer, Mercenary and Captain gain anything from pairing it with Hardlight Afterburner, since only for them is the utility skill the longest cooldown.

Proc coefficient changes your caps

On-hit items don't fire once per attack — they fire once per attack scaled by that skill's proc coefficient. A coefficient below 1 means both a lower trigger chance and a weaker effect, so the number of items needed to reach a given chance rises accordingly.

The practical effect is that item caps are survivor-specific and even skill-specific. Ten Tri-Tip Daggers guarantee bleed on a coefficient-1 attack, but MUL-T's Nailgun at 0.6 needs seventeen, and Blast Canister at 0.3 needs thirty-four. Wide-hitting or slow skills swing the other way — Arrow Rain and Caustic Leap need vastly more items to cap because each individual tick contributes so little, while high-coefficient item procs like Preon Accumulator carry enormous effective weight per hit.

Note the interaction with hyperbolic items: because they asymptote, a sub-1 coefficient means the maximum reachable chance sits permanently below the theoretical ceiling. Only 57 Leaf Clover can push past it.

Luck: Clover and Purity

57 Leaf Clover rerolls every failed random check once per copy, keeping the favourable result. The chance becomes one minus the failure probability raised to the copies-plus-one — a 10% AtG becomes 19% with one Clover, 27% with two. The rerolls diminish and never reach certainty, but they climb fast when the base chance is already respectable.

The crucial corollary is that Clover does nothing at 100%. If you have ten Tri-Tip Daggers, twenty Sticky Bombs or twenty-five Ghor's Tomes, the Clover contributes zero to those items. Clover is at its best on fixed-chance effects that stacking cannot improve — AtG, Ukulele, Molten and Charged Perforator, Happiest Mask, Brittle Crown — because those are the ones where rerolls are the only lever available.

Clover also covers crit chance, Artificer's Flamethrower ignite, Monster Log drops and elite equipment drops. It explicitly does not affect Tougher Times, and it does not touch chest spawns, shrine outcomes, Artifact Key drops or Sacrifice drop rolls — it improves your items, not your world.

One underappreciated case: Clover rerolls toward the favourable outcome even when the roll is a downside. Spinel Tonic's 20% chance of a permanent stat-decrease affliction collapses to 4% with one Clover and under 1% with two, turning a risky item into a nearly free one.

Purity is the mirror, and the asymmetry is savage. Clover rerolls failures, so your chance becomes p + (1−p)p. Purity rerolls successes, so your chance becomes p². That same 10% AtG goes to 19% with a Clover but to 1% with a Purity. It is not "you proc less often" — it is "you effectively do not proc at all."

The rule that follows: the lower your base chances, the more catastrophic Purity is; the higher they are, the cheaper it gets. Ninety percent becomes eighty-one, which is nothing. At exactly 100%, Purity costs literally zero. So a build carrying ten Tri-Tip Daggers loses nothing from Purity while a build carrying AtG, Ukulele and Meat Hook is gutted by a single copy.

Purity's own benefit is −2s off all skill cooldowns (+1s per stack), floored at 0.5 seconds so you cannot spam infinitely. Judge it on both the items you hold and the items you still expect to find. Late in a run with no meaningful proc items — or with them already at 100% — it's close to free. Grabbing it from a stage-2 Bazaar is how you spend the rest of the run regretting the AtGs you later picked up.

Some builds want it from the start. Survivors who hit hard but rarely gain little from on-hit items and a lot from cooldown reduction: Loader is the clearest case — Runald's and Kjaro's Bands serve her far better than Ukuleles ever would, so take Purity early and build movement, healing and Bands instead. Mercenary, Artificer, and sometimes Acrid follow the same logic. Equal counts of Clover and Purity cancel exactly, but if you hold good proc items you should simply run the Clover rather than trading it for cooldowns.

Item priority

Early, the ordering that holds regardless of survivor is damage first, then utility, then healing, then mobility. This inverts later: mobility items become far more valuable in the back half, when positioning is the difference between surviving a spawn wave and being deleted by it. The exception is a fragile survivor on a hard difficulty, where enough movement speed to escape a bad situation is worth buying before damage — at a triple shop, buy movement until you're fast enough to run away, then switch to damage.

What actually decides runs is reaching a critical mass of items, not finding any particular one. This is why item trades are dangerous: walking away from a trade is the correct default because trades thin the stack that keeps you alive. One-for-one exchanges are defensible; net-negative trades belong only at the very end of a run when you are finalising a build. The exceptions worth breaking the rule for are the items that reshape a run outright — Unstable Tesla Coil, Shattering Justice, Ukulele.

Some general verdicts hold across most survivors. Lens-Maker's Glasses, Tougher Times, Armor-Piercing Rounds, Hopoo Feather, Old Guillotine, the Bands, Dio's Best Friend, Genesis Loop and Titanic Knurl are close to universally good. Rusted Key is a hard no. Bustling Fungus is excellent on Engineer and near-worthless on anyone who has to keep moving. Death Mark is workable only if you can assemble enough consistent debuff sources, which usually you can't.

Several items are conditionally good in ways worth checking before you grab them. Clover is weak without proc items to reroll. Predatory Instincts and Harvester's Scythe both want an existing crit rate — the Scythe additionally suffers on slow-firing survivors. Transcendence is strong but makes every healing item in your inventory dead, so audit your stack before taking it. Shaped Glass gives enormous damage but eventually you cannot dodge everything, and on a long run it will kill you.

Synergies that multiply

Individual items add; combinations multiply. The combos worth deliberately steering toward share a structure — one item generates events, another converts those events into damage or resources.

Kill-chains. Gasoline explodes and burns on kill; Ignition Tank triples burn damage. Stacking Gasoline widens the radius and extends the burn until kills chain into more kills and entire stages evaporate. Will-o'-the-Wisp works the same way, and pairing it with Ukulele is more than the sum: each Wisp explosion hits several enemies at once, and every one of those hits independently rolls the Ukulele. Add Sentient Meat Hook to drag more bodies into each blast.

Equipment engines. Forgive Me Please triggers your on-kill effects; Soulbound Catalyst reduces equipment cooldown on kill. Together they form a closed loop — trigger on-kills, get kills, cut the cooldown, trigger again — and the payload is whatever on-kill items you own. Spinel Tonic plus Gesture of the Drowned is the same idea aimed at stats: Gesture force-fires the tonic continuously, giving near-permanent uptime on a roughly doubled statline while the affliction downside barely materialises. Fuel Cells serve either loop.

Crit conversion. Shatterspleen turns crits into bleed, and bleeding enemies explode for damage scaled to their health — devastating against bosses specifically. Feeding it crit chance turns an occasional effect into a constant one. The broader crit package (Lens-Maker's, Predatory Instincts, Harvester's Scythe) is why so many builds converge on nine or ten glasses.

Common-item damage stacking. Delicate Watch, crit items, Armor-Piercing Rounds, Crowbar and Focus Crystal each amplify a different slice of your damage — flat, on-crit, versus bosses, versus full-health targets, versus nearby targets. None is spectacular alone; assembled they produce a large multiplier out of nothing but white items, which is why a stage-1 white haul can carry a run.

Drone scaling. Spare Drone Parts spawns an attack drone and upgrades your existing ones with chainguns and missiles; Empathy Cores spawn probes that grow stronger with each ally you have. Each item improves the other's value, and a fully assembled drone stack can carry a run by itself.

Single-target conversion. Poly Loot — corrupted Ukulele — hits the same enemy repeatedly instead of chaining outward, converting an AoE item into single-target burst. Plasma Shrimp fires missiles on every hit while shielded and ignores proc coefficient for that trigger, so it multiplies off Poly Loot's many hits; ICBM triples each missile.

Movement as damage. H3AD-5T v2 explodes on landing with damage scaling off fall height, and Wax Quail launches you skyward off any slope. Together they make maximum-height landings repeatable rather than accidental.

Interactable-driven. Squid Pup spawns a turret every time you open an interactable, and those turrets scale their damage with enemy level so they never fall off. Remote Caffeinator both spawns and heals them, which fixes their one weakness.

Build archetypes

Most successful builds are one of a handful of shapes, and recognising which shape your item drops are pushing you toward is more useful than chasing a specific list.

Proc-density builds want attack speed and low-value, high-frequency hits: Soldier's Syringe plus AtG Missile, backed by the crit package, produces a permanent cloud of missiles. These are the builds that Clover multiplies and Purity destroys. They fit Commando and MUL-T naturally, though MUL-T's lower coefficients mean crit items pay off more slowly for him.

Debuff-stacking builds use fast attacks to apply many simultaneous status effects. MUL-T's attack speed hits Death Mark's threshold almost instantly, and the supporting cast — Tri-Tip Dagger, Chronobauble, Molten Perforator, Ifrit's Distinction, with Mired Urn, Frost Relic or Shatterspleen as substitutes — is largely interchangeable so long as the debuffs are distinct.

Burst builds skip on-hit density entirely and lean on the Bands, Crowbar and Focus Crystal, with movement items to control engagement. Loader, Railgunner and Mercenary's quick-kill line all live here, and they are the builds where Purity is a genuine upgrade.

Sustain-through-offense builds convert damage dealt into survival. REX with capped crit plus Harvester's Scythe erases his HP-cost drawback entirely — every attack becomes healing, provided you land it. Loader's Razorwire loop does the same trick with self-damage: Helfire Tincture damages you, Razorwire retaliates against everything nearby, Planula heals back what Helfire cost, and Fuel Cells keep the cycle running.

Cooldown builds discard chance entirely. Artificer with Alien Head, Purity, Backup Magazines, Hardlight Afterburner, Brainstalks and Fuel Cells uses abilities effectively continuously; Primordial Cube clumps enemies for the payoff. Bandit does something structurally similar with stacked Purity, where a short enough Smoke Bomb cooldown produces permanent invisibility and a form of flight.

Turret-mediated builds are Engineer's alone, since his turrets inherit his items and act as independent carriers. Bustling Fungus plus N'kuhana's Opinion turns his stationary turrets' constant healing into a damage source. The walking turrets, being faster-attacking and mobile, are the better vehicle for on-hit builds like a Shatterspleen bleed engine.

Recap

Identify each item's stacking curve before deciding whether to keep taking it — linear items fade, hyperbolic ones asymptote, exponential ones never stop. Learn where your key items cap, and remember that your proc coefficients move those caps. Treat Clover and Purity as opposite ends of the same lever and take neither blindly. Aim for critical mass over any single item, protect the stack by refusing trades, and steer toward combinations where one item feeds another rather than collecting good items in isolation.

Proc Chains

The reason two players with identical inventories can have wildly different damage output is that items don't belong to you — they belong to the attack that triggers them. Every skill carries a hidden multiplier that decides how good it is at setting items off, and once you understand that number you understand why some survivors want AtG Missiles and others should give them away.

What the coefficient actually multiplies

The proc coefficient attaches to the attack, not the item, and it scales the effect, the trigger chance, the duration, or any combination of the three depending on the item. It is not a simple on/off gate.

Tri-Tip Dagger is the clearest illustration because it scales in two dimensions at once. Nominally it's a 10% chance of a three-second bleed. Fired through MUL-T's Auto-Nailgun at 0.6, each stack is worth 6% and the bleed runs shorter. Fired through Railgunner's Supercharged Railgun at 3.0, each stack is worth 30% and the bleed lasts nine seconds. Same item, same stack count, wildly different value.

This is deliberate balancing. Attacks that fire many times per second get low coefficients so they cannot trivially reach guaranteed procs; attacks that land rarely but hit enormously get high coefficients to compensate for how seldom they connect. The result is that nominal item chance and effective item chance are different numbers, and you frequently need well over 100% nominal chance to guarantee a trigger — seventeen Tri-Tip Daggers through the Nailgun where four suffice through the Railgun.

The rule is not universal, though, and the exceptions matter for build planning. Commando's Double Tap fires quickly and keeps a full 1.0. Artificer's Flamethrower ticks twenty-two-plus times per cast at 1.0 each. Both are enormous proc engines that look like they should have been penalised and weren't.

Crit is exempt

Critical strike chance ignores proc coefficients entirely. At 100% crit from ten Lens-Maker's Glasses, every attack crits regardless of what fires it — which is part of why crit-conversion items like Shatterspleen work on survivors whose on-hit items otherwise underperform.

How chains form and where they die

A proc chain is on-hit effects triggering further on-hit effects. Items have their own coefficients for this purpose, and they are mostly small, which keeps chains from running away — but not from being devastating.

The canonical loop is Ukulele plus AtG Missile. Ukulele's lightning carries 0.2, so each bolt has a small chance to launch a missile; the missile carries a full 1.0, so it has the complete 25% chance to trigger lightning back. A single connecting hit can cascade outward and clear a screen. Ceremonial Dagger firing at 1.0 three times over is similarly generous, which is why it perpetuates kill-chains so reliably.

The single most important structural fact about chains is where they terminate: damage-over-time effects have a proc coefficient of 0.0. Bleed, burn, ignite — none of them can trigger anything. This is why a bleed-heavy build produces enormous raw damage but no cascade, and why stacking Tri-Tip Daggers into a chain build gives you two separate damage sources rather than one multiplied one.

Several items are silent dead ends in the same way, either at 0.0 outright or because they never raise the on-hit event other items listen for: Gasoline, Sticky Bomb, both Bands, Brilliant Behemoth, Helfire Tincture and Ifrit's Distinction all deal damage without propagating. The Back-up's drones are a special case — they hit at full coefficient, but they carry their own inventory, so they proc none of your items.

At the other end, some effects propagate unusually well. Molten Perforator's magma balls fire at 0.7 three times over. Preon Accumulator's main explosion is a full 1.0 and hits everything nearby, making it one of the strongest single proc events in the game. Molotov fire pools tick at 0.5 once per second per enemy standing in them, so their effective proc rate rises with crowd size.

57 Leaf Clover is disproportionately valuable in a chain build precisely because it multiplies at every link — more procs means more links means more opportunities for further procs, and the effect compounds through the chain rather than adding to it once.

Reading your own survivor

The practical exercise is to look at what your primary and most-used secondary actually carry, then let that decide your item priorities.

Low-coefficient, high-frequency, multi-target attacks are better at exploiting linearly-stacking trigger-chance items, because they get many rolls per second even at reduced odds — but they demand a much larger item investment to reach their ceiling. Huntress's Arrow Rain at 0.2 across nineteen ticks and Acrid's Caustic Leap pool at 0.1 both need enormous dagger counts to cap, yet they blanket whole groups when they get there.

High-coefficient, single-target attacks are the opposite: they reach guaranteed procs on very few items but only ever apply them to one enemy. Railgunner's Supercharged Railgun at 3.0 caps a dagger stack almost immediately.

Survivors sitting at a flat 1.0 across their kit — Bandit's Lights Out and Desperado, Loader's gauntlets, Mercenary's entire skillset, Captain's tazer and probes — are the reliable middle ground where standard on-hit items simply work as advertised.

Watch for skills that split into components with different coefficients, since the choice between them changes your item math. MUL-T's Blast Canister is 1.0 for the canister and 0.3 across five bomblets. Artificer's Charged Nano-Bomb is 1.0 for the explosion and 0.3 for the sparks. Acrid's Caustic Leap is 1.0 for the impact and 0.1 for the pool. In each case the burst component is your proc vehicle and the lingering component is close to inert.

Some skills carry a hard zero and should never be considered proc tools at all: REX's Disperse, Captain's Supply Beacon, Railgunner's Concussion and Polar Field devices, False Son's Meridian's Will.

A corollary for multiplayer: on-hit items handed to a teammate are not wasted, because the missiles still fire — just from someone else. That is what makes Purity a genuinely cooperative pick on Artificer, who converts cooldown reduction into value while her teammates convert the discarded proc items into value.

Debuff stacking and Death Mark

Death Mark applies a 50% damage vulnerability once an enemy carries four or more distinct debuffs. On its own it is a weak item, because assembling four simultaneous distinct debuffs is genuinely hard. Understanding which effects count as distinct is what converts it from a dead pick into a build axis.

The key insight is that similar-looking effects often count separately. Ignite and Burn are mechanically distinct and both tick, so a fire build carrying both has two debuffs, not one. There are two Root types — REX's, which slows and heals him, and Hooks of Heresy's, which fully immobilises even Mithrix — and they count separately. Slows are the richest vein of all: Tar from Mired Urn, the laser slow from Arrow Rain and Engineer's mobile turrets, Chronobauble's slow and Runald's ice slow are four separate debuffs that a single build can carry at once.

Poison and Blight are Acrid's alternatives and count separately, though solo you only ever hold one since it's a skill choice. Bleed from Tri-Tip and Shatterspleen is among the strongest options generally. Bandit's Hemorrhage is a distinct, stronger bleed triggered by crits with his secondary. Shattering Justice's Buildup and Pulverize both count but only one is ever active. Helfire Tincture counts, which is not widely known. Ruin from Essence of Heresy counts even before it detonates, and applies from every hit of any attack.

Cripple is the lunar debuff — heavy slow plus armor reduction — and comes from Effigy of Grief, Perfected elites and most of Mithrix's attacks. It's excellent for ranged survivors and actively dangerous for melee ones, since deploying Effigy means standing inside your own crippling field.

Weaken is REX's passive, applied by his primary, which makes him the game's most natural Death Mark carrier — the debuff is simply always up. Expose is Mercenary's, but it's consumed by his very next attack, so it's a poor foundation and a fine finisher: use it as the fourth debuff that completes a nearly-assembled mark rather than the first.

Artificer's freeze is not a debuff. It's a temporary execute threshold and contributes nothing here.

A survivor with no innate debuff needs four items to do the job, which is why the most reliable generic package is Shattering Justice, Runald's Band, Tri-Tip Dagger and Chronobauble — with Shatterspleen substituting for the dagger in a capped-crit build. Runald's ice slow is the best general-purpose choice because it's consistent for anyone with a hard-hitting move to trigger it.

Survivors with one or two innate debuffs need correspondingly fewer items, which is the whole reason the archetype is viable at all. REX and the Heretic both carry two permanently and can afford almost any pair of supplements. Engineer running the mobile Carbonizer turrets gets the laser slow for free at high proc rates without spending an equipment slot, and his turrets retain elite aspects too.

The counter-considerations are positional. Effigy of Grief is bad on anyone who fights inside its radius. Mired Urn is bad on anyone who keeps distance. Helfire Tincture is excellent on Mercenary, whose i-frames let him ignore the self-damage entirely, and lethal on low-health survivors like Bandit or the health-draining Heretic. Runald's Band needs something to actually trigger it — Huntress running Arrow Rain has nothing suitable, while Bandit's special procs it on every shot.

Recap

Proc coefficients live on attacks, scale chance and duration together, and make item caps survivor-specific. Fast multi-hit skills need far more items to reach the same effective chance but roll more often; slow heavy skills cap instantly and hit one target. Chains cascade through items with non-zero coefficients and die instantly at any damage-over-time effect. Build toward whichever half of that structure your survivor actually supports, and treat Death Mark as a build to commit to deliberately — counting distinct debuff types — rather than an item to hope for.

Survivors

Survivor choice decides which items are good, not the other way round. A pick that changes your primary's proc coefficient changes your entire shopping list. Tap any survivor for their kit logic, skill choices and item priorities.

On tier lists

Rankings are real but soft — most of the roster clears Monsoon in competent hands, and the differences are mostly about how much the survivor forgives. The genuine outliers are at the ends: Heretic and Void Fiend are absurd when assembled, Artificer and Mercenary punish mechanical mistakes hardest, and Commando is deliberately average because he's the teaching character. Play what you enjoy; the item logic below matters far more than the ordering.

Choosing skills at all

Alternate skills are not upgrades — several are sidegrades that reshape which items you want, and a few are outright worse for what you're trying to do. The useful question is never "which is stronger" but "which changes my proc profile, my mobility budget, or my cooldown economy in the direction my build is already going".

A useful metric when comparing two damage skills is proc value: damage per second multiplied by proc coefficient. It captures the thing raw DPS comparisons miss — that a skill firing at 0.2 coefficient contributes far less to your item damage even when its listed numbers look better. On Huntress this is exactly why Ballista beats Arrow Rain so decisively, and why Flurry only edges out Strafe once crit is nearly capped.

Universal early priorities

Regardless of survivor, a handful of picks are worth taking on sight early: one of each movement item to make disengaging possible, a shield or barrier source since neither competes with your healing, and enough attack speed to make your primary feel like a weapon rather than a suggestion. Old Guillotine matters as soon as elites become routine, and Hopoo Feather solves an astonishing number of positional problems for one green.

The other constant is that fragile survivors should buy mobility before damage. There's no damage number that saves you from being cornered, and every survivor in the bottom half of the health chart dies to positioning failures long before they die to insufficient DPS.

Recap

Pick a survivor, then read their proc coefficients and let those pick your items. Skills that fire fast and wide want stacked chance items and lots of them; skills that hit once and hard want burst multipliers and cooldown reduction. Everything else — health pool, mobility, whether the kit forgives a mistake — determines how much survivability you must buy before you're allowed to start scaling damage.

Commando is the default survivor precisely because every skill in his kit teaches a mechanic you'll need on everyone else. He is genuinely middling in raw power and genuinely excellent as an item platform — the two facts are the same fact.

Why he's the best on-hit carrier

His primary fires two bullets per press and keeps a full 1.0 proc coefficient despite the rate of fire, which is a combination almost nobody else gets. His special adds six more full-coefficient hits on a short cooldown. The result is more proc rolls per second at full value than any other survivor, so every on-hit item in the game is at its best in his hands: Tri-Tip, Sticky Bomb, AtG, the Bands, Ukulele, Meat Hook. Take them as your primary damage source and never skip one.

That also means attack speed is not a stat boost for him, it's a damage multiplier — every extra shot is another roll on your entire on-hit stack.

Skill mechanics worth knowing

His primary loses damage over distance and its spread widens the longer you hold fire, so he wants to be close and to fire in bursts at range — release as soon as the crosshair starts flaring and the spread resets.

His secondary is not a cooldown to save. On a roughly three-second timer it should be part of your continuous rotation: it's the best waveclear in the kit, does respectable single-target damage, and resets the primary's accumulated spread. Angle so it lines up as many bodies as possible. It fires at the same rate as the primary, which means Backup Magazines effectively raise its rate of fire.

The special is the highest-damage skill and better on one large target than a crowd — the secondary already owns crowds. Its stun is the real value: it stops Golems firing their laser and makes Imps tractable. Attack speed shortens the lock-in duration without reducing shot count.

The utility's cooldown is short enough that with enemy pattern knowledge you can go through fights untouched. Movement speed increases its distance. Critically, you can roll while the special is firing but you cannot start the special mid-roll — rolling during a suppressive burst turns a stand-still commitment into a safe one, and is the single most useful piece of Commando tech.

One ordering wrinkle: the special resets your primary's spread but applies its own, so going straight from special to primary inherits the bad spread. Slot the secondary between them to clear it, timed so it doesn't cut the special short.

Items

Because the kit is so rounded, the entire item pool works and there is no meaningful tier list — take survivability early, attack speed constantly, and on-hit items whenever offered. Cooldown reduction is near-overkill given how short his timers already are, but adds consistency. Backup Magazine is the standout utility pick since it lets you fire the secondary about as often as the primary.

His genuine weakness is AoE. He has no native area damage beyond the secondary's line, and on Monsoon area damage is close to mandatory — Brilliant Behemoth, Ukulele, Gasoline and Will-o'-the-Wisp are the fixes. A reasonable self-check is to judge the run after the first teleporter boss dies: without at least one real AoE source, the run is unlikely to survive the mid-game.

Solo, the answer to his low base damage is proc density plus pacing — he cannot brute-force a stage, so he wants more items and more time to accumulate them than a burst survivor does.

Recap: a full-coefficient, high-frequency shooter that turns every on-hit item into damage. Play close, burst at range, roll while suppressing, and make sure you solve AoE before the mid-game does it for you.

Huntress has three distinct phases and confusing her current phase for a different one is how most Huntress runs end. Early she is hunted — wet paper with low damage and unreliable cooldowns. Mid she has itemised over her flaws and plays identically but survives. Late her secondary scales absurdly and she is one of the hardest carries in the game.

The auto-aim is the kit

Both primaries and her secondary lock on and never miss, and both primaries fire while sprinting. That combination — attacking at full effect while never stopping moving — is her entire survivability model, and it's why movement speed is worth more to her than to almost anyone.

The technique is counterintuitive: don't put the crosshair on the target. Hold forward plus a strafe key and keep the crosshair slightly off the enemy; the lock-on cone catches it and you retain full sprint. The cone is small and it takes practice.

The cost of guaranteed hits is zero control. You cannot aim these skills, they won't fire without a target in range, and if the target dies mid-flight the attack is wasted into the corpse. Her special exists partly to solve that — it targets a specific enemy when the lock-on won't cooperate.

Skill choices

Strafe versus Flurry is a crit bet. Flurry fires three arrows, and on a crit fires six that all crit — which quadruples its output. But its coefficient is lower, so on proc value Flurry only overtakes Strafe once crit chance is around three-quarters, and even at full crit the improvement is under ten percent. On raw DPS the crossover comes far earlier. So: Flurry if you are chasing pure damage and will find crit items, Strafe if you want consistency and proc reliability. Flurry also wastes more arrows into corpses.

Blink versus Phase Blink is a genuine trade. Blink is long-range with full vertical control, which climbs walls, skips paths and reaches Newt altars you otherwise can't. Phase Blink gives three shorter charges recycling faster, and is usable as an in-combat dodge. Dodging otherwise-undodgeable attacks generally beats saving item-collection time, so Phase Blink is the safer default — but if you're routing for items or speed, Blink's mobility is real.

Arrow Rain versus Ballista is not close on paper. Arrow Rain's many ticks fire at a very low coefficient, so its proc value is a fraction of Ballista's despite the total damage looking respectable; it would need to hold nine enemies inside it for the full duration to compete, and its slow is too mild to keep anyone there. Ballista dumps damage instantly at full coefficient and procs Bands cleanly. The one argument for Arrow Rain is that its crowd control feels close to mandatory on high Eclipse — and it supplies a laser slow debuff for Death Mark builds.

Secondary mechanics

The Laser Glaive bounces to the nearest target to its current position, which is sometimes behind you, and it passes through terrain to reach that target. Later bounces do more damage, so throwing into a crowd rewards predicting the path so the final hits land on the tankiest body. It can hit the same target repeatedly, which is why it's good against a few tanky enemies as well as a swarm.

It doubles as a scout — the targeting finds enemies you didn't know were there, and watching where it flies tells you what's around the corner. It cancels sprint, so re-press sprint after throwing. Attack speed increases its throw rate. Its bounces carry a healthy coefficient, making it her one genuinely good proc vehicle.

Items and tech

Early, buy survivability without apology — she dies fast regardless of skill and getting to the mid-game is the whole problem. Out-of-combat healing works unusually well for her because her disengage is so strong. Chronobauble and Rose Buckler both keep enemies off her while she strafes.

The items that actually decide the run are the ones that raise her secondary's uptime and damage: Backup Magazine, Bandolier, Alien Head, Brainstalks, and Crowbar to make each glaive burst rather than chip. Kill-chain items pair well because when things die around the glaive it spends bounces seeking fresh targets instead of re-hitting one.

Attack-speed items are her weakest category — her primary is not worth buffing when the secondary outclasses it, so pass syringes along in a group. Fixed-chance on-hit items are similarly poor value given her primaries' coefficients, though the glaive redeems them somewhat.

Two pieces of tech: holding yourself airborne with the special buys time for utility charges to refill, and the glaive slows your descent — enough to cancel fall damage or extend airtime, which pairs with Blink's vertical mobility.

Recap: survive the early game by buying defence, keep sprinting while the auto-aim does the work, and funnel everything into glaive uptime. Ballista over Arrow Rain unless you specifically want the crowd control, and mobility items are never wasted.

Bandit is high damage on a fragile frame, and his two defining mechanics both reward the same behaviour: getting behind things and killing them quickly.

Backstab and the reset loop

His passive makes every attack from behind an automatic critical. That's an enormous amount of free damage and, more importantly, it removes the need to build crit chance at all — the crit items other survivors must buy are optional for him, freeing those picks for movement and damage.

The consequence is that movement speed is his damage stat. Hopoo Feather and Wax Quail aren't survivability picks on Bandit, they're what lets you circle an enemy fast enough to be behind it. Side-strafing with Wax Quail is particularly effective.

His special is a slayer shot on a short cooldown, and killing with it resets all his cooldowns. Chained together this is a loop that carries stages 1 and 2 almost regardless of items. The alternative special trades the resets for stacking damage tokens per kill, which scales harder over a long run but loses the loop.

The special also carries a very high proc coefficient, making it an excellent trigger for Bands and other high-value single-hit items.

Kit notes

His secondaries apply Hemorrhage on crit — a stronger bleed that stacks — which makes him excellent against the enormously inflated health pools of late loop bosses. Given the backstab passive, that's a lot of Hemorrhage. The tradeoff is that both secondaries are individually weak attacks, so the debuff is worth more than the hit.

His utility stuns, damages on entry and exit, and grants invisibility in between. Reading it purely as an escape wastes it — it's also an offensive repositioning tool that puts you behind whatever you were just in front of.

Attack speed does speed his reload, but it isn't a priority; most of your time is spent moving, not firing.

Items

Movement first, then damage multipliers that benefit from guaranteed crits. Focus Crystal is strong because his shotgun primary wants to be close anyway. Crowbar suits the burst pattern. Tougher Times in quantity is the practical answer to his low health, since he cannot afford to trade hits.

Purity is worth considering only if you genuinely have no proc items — it's a real option on a Bands-and-burst build and a disaster on anything else. Helfire Tincture is actively lethal on him; his health pool is among the lowest in the game.

For equipment, anything that clears trash during a teleporter event is ideal, because it lets you spend all your own damage on the boss. Targeted equipment is also strong given his single-target focus.

Laser Scope deserves special mention — on a survivor with a guaranteed-crit passive it turns a mediocre run into a dominant one.

Recap: build movement, get behind everything, and use the special's reset loop to snowball early stages. Hemorrhage carries him through late-game health inflation, and he skips crit items entirely thanks to the passive.

MUL-T carries two primaries simultaneously and swaps between them, which makes him the most loadout-flexible survivor in the game and the one whose item priorities shift most based on that choice. He has a large health pool and poor mobility, which pushes him toward a stand-and-deliver playstyle.

The coefficient problem

His machine-gun primary carries a 0.6 coefficient, and his canister bomblets sit at 0.3. This is the defining constraint on his builds: every chance-based on-hit item needs substantially more stacks on MUL-T than on anyone else to reach the same effective rate. Seventeen Tri-Tip Daggers for guaranteed bleed instead of ten; crit items pay off more slowly than they look like they should.

You can lean into it or around it. Leaning in means accepting the item tax and building enormous stacks, which his attack speed and shot volume genuinely support — his rate of fire applies on-hit debuffs faster than anyone despite the penalty, which is why he's the natural Death Mark carrier. Leaning around it means running his piercing primary, which retains a full coefficient, and building burst instead.

Loadout shapes

Two primaries of the same type is a legitimate and often optimal choice. Double machine-gun maximises projectile volume and pairs with his damage-boosting special. Double piercing rifle maximises single-target precision, and holding the equipment-swap special lets you fire the rifle considerably faster than normal.

The saw loadout is a melee-range option built around a specific tech: his sprint attack ordering combined with cancelling out of the canister via the mode-swap special. Backup Magazines refund canister charges, which makes the cancel loop repeatable.

His utility is a charging transport mode that doubles as his only real mobility — use it to compress early stages where his slow speed costs the most time.

The equipment-swap special deserves attention on its own terms: it lets MUL-T carry and alternate two equipment, which changes how you value equipment pickups entirely. Equipment you'd normally skip becomes a second slot rather than a replacement.

Items

Attack speed and on-hit effects fit best, accepting that you need more of them than the tooltips suggest. Because his coefficient penalty applies to chance but his volume is enormous, items that always trigger — flat damage boosts, on-kill effects — are proportionally better on him than on high-coefficient survivors.

His health pool means defensive items compound well rather than being wasted on a survivor who would have died anyway. Healing and armour convert directly into staying power.

Recap: pick a primary pair, then build to that choice — volume plus deep on-hit stacks, or precision plus burst. Budget extra items for the coefficient penalty, exploit the double-equipment special, and use his bulk as an asset rather than treating him as a squishy shooter.

Engineer is widely called the easiest survivor and it's an accurate description of the outcome rather than the process. His turrets copy his entire item inventory on placement, which effectively triples the value of everything he owns — and everything else about him is built to sit still long enough for that to matter.

Turrets are separate entities

Understanding what that means mechanically is what separates competent Engineer play from great Engineer play. Turrets inherit items but share nothing back: Leeching Seed heals the turret, Infusion buffs the turret's health and that health is lost when the turret despawns. They retain elite aspects if you have them. They can be killed by swarms, which is why survivability items matter to a survivor who is theoretically standing behind a shield.

The signature interaction is Bustling Fungus. Turrets never move, so their healing field is permanently active — they heal each other and you, and stacking both turrets in one spot makes it work properly. Pick up every Fungus you find; it's near-worthless on anyone who has to keep moving and it's the item that makes Engineer functional. The risk is that standing in the pile makes you a target for area damage, so watch your surroundings and be willing to abandon the position.

A quieter use of turret placement is farming. Enemies spawn in a circle around players, so a turret left alone with no player nearby has nothing to shoot. Pick a large area, place one turret at each edge of your spawn circle, and shuttle between them — both turrets stay fed and generate income while you do something else.

Always keep a turret charge in reserve. Turrets are your only real anti-air, they generate aggro and absorb hits meant for you, and with Fungus they're an on-demand healing station. Having both on cooldown when something goes wrong is how Engineer runs end.

The rest of the kit

His primary feels awful without attack speed and becomes one of the most brutal attacks in the game with it — a large instant hit with a high chance of triggering multiple on-hit effects. Hold to charge rather than clicking repeatedly; charging fires roughly twice as many grenades. Finish the volley before using another skill, since any skill cancels the charge and there's no benefit to interrupting. The projectiles arc downward, so aim above distant targets, and swinging the camera after launch spreads the volley over a wider area.

His mines persist until triggered and place faster with attack speed. Always be regenerating at least one charge — a mine dropped somewhere pointless still beats an unused charge. The three practical patterns are carpeting a line as enemies charge you (easiest done by backing up and dropping at your feet), blanketing an area you care about like the teleporter or a cluster of chests you can't yet afford, and sticking them to an ally who is about to be in the middle of a fight.

The bubble shield is not a safe box — enemies walk in and attack you inside it. What it does is block incoming ranged fire, including Titan lasers, and it blocks outgoing fire too, including your own turrets'. From inside you can shoot out. Grenades bounce off it, which is exploitable: bouncing them off the dome is an awkward but effective way to hit air units.

The mobile turret variant trades the stationary healing platform for a slow-applying, high-proc-rate walking damage source, which is what makes it the Death Mark and bleed vehicle.

Items

Anything with a percentage chance or flat damage scales magnificently because grenades and turrets both apply it repeatedly. Attack speed is the multiplier that makes the grenades exploit those items. Mobility items matter more than they look like they should, since he has none — Milky Chrysalis alone fixes his traversal, and Wax Quail is the more reliable if less dramatic version.

N'kuhana's Opinion is unusually good on him because he generates so much healing, and it procs from turrets as well. Cooldown reduction items enable everything he does given how long his timers are.

Backup Magazine is a trap — his mine charges are already generous and an eleventh does nothing. Crowbar has almost nothing to attach to. Both are better in someone else's hands.

Recap: place turrets thoughtfully, hoard Bustling Fungus, keep a charge in reserve, and buy attack speed to make the primary carry its own weight. Everything you pick up counts three times, so the usual item-priority logic bends heavily in his favour.

Artificer has more raw damage than anyone and no safety net whatsoever. Every other survivor holds your hand somewhere — Mercenary through mobility, Engineer through item multiplication, Huntress through auto-aim. She does not. She is as good as her player and no better.

Cooldown management is the skill

Most survivors have a cushion: a strong basic attack that's always available means poor cooldown decisions rarely get punished. Artificer's charges are her damage, so dumping everything on a trivial target and having nothing left for what arrives next is a real and frequent way to die.

Stagger deliberately. Use the longer-cooldown skill first and the shorter one a few seconds later, so something is always coming back up. Equally important is the discipline of withholding — declining to spend a skill on a target you could kill more slowly, so it's available for the thing that actually threatens you.

Flying is her defence

Her passive hover doesn't stop a fall instantly, it caps your descent speed — so start hovering early to break a fall rather than reacting at the ground. You can toggle sprint mid-hover. Directional control while hovering is far better than in free-fall, though changes take time to register.

Chained with jump pads, high ground, her special, Hopoo Feathers, H3AD-5T, Milky Chrysalis or Eccentric Vase, she can stay airborne for extremely long stretches and cross maps without touching ground. Constant airtime dodges most damage outright, and dropping out of hover for a moment is how you duck under an incoming ranged attack.

Because flight is her defence, flying enemies are her greatest threat — Lesser Wisps in particular, since they contest the space she lives in.

Skills

Her primary is best understood as a spammable secondary rather than a primary: its damage is comparable to other survivors' cooldown abilities. It travels slowly enough that you must lead moving targets. Its job is finishing or softening what your bigger skills didn't kill.

Never hoard charges. They regenerate fast, so sitting at maximum is wasted damage — always have at least one recharging. The real cost is dumping all four at once, which leaves a long dead window; weaving single bolts between your other skills is the correct rhythm. Burst all four only against bosses or a high-priority target, and preferably when you have a defensive skill ready to follow.

Fire versus plasma is a genuine fork. The fire bolt ignites, which guarantees kills on small flyers and delivers higher single-target damage — meaningful when flyers are your nemesis. The plasma bolt's small explosion clears clusters more efficiently and can proc items multiple times per attack, which scales slightly better late.

Her secondary options split the same way. The bomb covers a much larger area and is the better defensive tool because its area stun stops more enemies than the spear's longer freeze does; its arc is awkward to aim. The spear flies straight, is much simpler to land, and nukes single tanky targets. Both regenerate primary charges while charging, making them the right follow-up after burning your bolts.

Two subtleties: you can start a charge and then sprint while holding it, dodging while building damage. And deliberately missing is sometimes correct — hitting the ground between several squishy enemies beats a direct hit on one.

Her utility freezes and creates a damaging barrier, with three distinct uses. As an execute, dropped under a target near the freeze threshold to skip a long damage phase. Against a boss, placed underfoot so many pillars connect at once. As a panic button, freezing a crowd so you can follow up into a stationary pack. The cooldown is long enough that spending it on the wrong one hurts.

Her special is a choice between damage and the mobility she otherwise lacks: the flamethrower is enormous sustained damage in a dangerous position, and the surge is vertical escape that also enables near-permanent flight. Note that sprinting instantly cancels the flamethrower — the same input that instantly casts her ice wall.

Items

She wants cooldown reduction above nearly everything, plus attack speed to accelerate charge rates. Backup Magazine is a genuine priority. On-hit chance items serve her poorly and are better given away, which is precisely why Purity is one of the few survivors it genuinely improves — a build of Alien Head, Purity, Backup Magazines, Hardlight Afterburner, Brainstalks and Fuel Cells uses abilities essentially continuously, with Primordial Cube clumping targets for the payoff.

The other archetype is an on-kill build. Her ignite chains into kills, kills feed on-kill effects, and Forgive Me Please plus cooldown reduction turns that into a self-sustaining loop. Ignition Tank makes her fire primary hit substantially harder.

Recap: stay airborne, stagger cooldowns, weave primary charges instead of dumping them, and buy cooldown reduction over on-hit chance. She rewards mechanical precision more than any other survivor and punishes carelessness immediately.

Mercenary is a melee survivor who effectively flies, and whose defence is offence in the most literal sense — his attacks grant invulnerability frames, so attacking is safer than retreating. He's fast, high-ceiling, and unusually indifferent to enemy attacks once you can chain properly.

Expose is the engine

Every third primary hit marks the target. Hitting an exposed enemy consumes the mark for a large damage bonus and reduces every other cooldown by a second. That coupling is why attack speed is his best stat by a distance — more exposed procs means more damage, more cooldowns, more airtime, more combo.

The follow-up choice depends on the target. Soft enemies can just be finished with the primary; tanky ones should have the mark spent on your biggest hit. If your secondary is not on cooldown you are playing him wrong.

String discipline and cancels

Any other ability cancels an in-progress primary string, losing the exposed proc. That makes his lowest-damage tool a genuine liability: only start a string you can finish. Conversely, the animations are longer than the actual frames — once you hear the swing land the attack is done, so chaining straight into another ability off the third hit is a large free damage dump.

His primary is Agile, meaning you can sprint while attacking. That enables string preparation: begin the string during the approach so your first hit on the enemy is already the third. Wax Quail extends the range of this considerably. Note that high attack speed tightens the timing and shortens the effective reach, so string prep is an early-game tool that fades.

Skill choices

The default secondary is two horizontal slices with a forward push, which can whiff the second hit on a single close target — use it grounded as a controlled gap-closer or against a horde. Airborne it slices vertically, lands both hits on one target and keeps you up. Its notable weakness is real end lag: a vulnerable window where you cannot act, which terminates the combo. On slanted terrain it launches you vertically without spending a jump.

The alternate secondary is an uppercut that carries both you and the target skyward. It's simpler, hits harder, has much better recovery, and — critically — it can trigger Bands where the default cannot. It also becomes your best post-exposed hit, which reshapes your combos entirely, and gives excellent vertical reach for chasing flyers. Hitting an already-airborne enemy launches it absurdly high, which buys time against wisp swarms.

The alternate utility avoids the constant reorientation of the default and delivers higher single-target damage, at the cost of the default's repositioning.

His passive double jump does more than it looks like: it reaches air units when the utility is down, prevents fall damage when the secondary is down, keeps him on tankier targets long enough to combo twice before landing, and makes him the only survivor who can use Wax Quail twice per jump.

Items

Attack speed first, always — his special scales with it and Expose multiplies off it. Focus Crystal fits perfectly since he only leaves melee range to wait out cooldowns. Backup Magazine is close to mandatory; leave one for him if a Huntress is competing for it.

With enough Purity he chains his special into effective permanent invincibility, which is the most degenerate thing in his kit and entirely legitimate. Transcendence's fall protection matters less than usual given his vertical control, but it still nullifies healing items — combined with Purity that leaves a lot of printer fodder, so hunt a Focus Crystal or Tri-Tip printer and stack hard.

Helfire Tincture is unusually strong on him: his i-frames mean he takes no return damage while it burns, especially during his special when he's inside the enemy.

Because cheesing the final boss is awkward for him, keep a conventional equipment for that fight rather than a movement item.

Recap: attack to survive, finish every string you start, spend Expose on the biggest available hit, and buy attack speed above everything. Save your last dash as an escape, and use the i-frames as your health bar rather than stacking healing.

REX pays health to use abilities and heals it straight back from other abilities. The fluctuating health bar is the design, not a warning sign — panicking at it and playing conservatively is the most common way to play REX badly.

The loop

His primary applies Weaken — reduced enemy armour, movement and damage — and heals him for a share of damage dealt. His mortar-style secondary costs a percentage of current health and deals heavy damage on a sub-second cooldown. Spam both: the primary funds the secondary. Because the cost is a percentage of current health rather than max, it never quite kills you on its own.

That self-damage also triggers on-damage items, which cuts both ways — Medkit is excellent, Brittle Crown is catastrophic. Razorwire is a natural pairing given the frequency of self-harm.

Kit details

His primary fires three shots in a horizontal fan; only the last applies Weaken and the heal. Each shot carries half proc coefficient, partly offset by there being three of them.

His secondary drops from the sky and ignores ceilings, which matters because otherwise it would be useless indoors. Its range is enormous, making a back-line sniping posture viable.

His utility knocks enemies away and knocks him backwards, so it doubles as mobility — with practice it reaches places nothing else does. It's most valuable not as an escape but as a shepherding tool: shoving enemies into a cluster, into a trap, toward an ally, or off a cliff. It doesn't proc on-hit effects, and the alternate version trades the Weaken application for more damage and a second self-damage source.

His special roots a group, deals heavy damage over its duration, and heals per target hit — including continuing to leech from enemies that have already died, which offsets its steep cost. Its uptime is nearly total, but it should be spent on four or more enemies rather than convenience, unless you've built heavy cooldown reduction. It makes early Mountain Shrines much safer, since snaring several bosses at once is very strong. Its ticks carry zero proc coefficient.

His alternate secondary rains seeds over an area for repeated small hits, which massively raises Backup Magazine's priority and pairs with items that scale off many hits — Leeching Seed, Harvester's Scythe, Shatterspleen, Tri-Tip. The default secondary instead wants Bands and conventional on-hit items, since it's one big hit.

Items and counters

Capped crit plus Harvester's Scythe erases his one drawback completely — every attack becomes a heal, and provided you keep landing hits you sit near full health permanently. That interaction is strong enough to define his build.

Because his kit self-heals, he can afford to skip healing items entirely and buy attack speed instead. Weeping Fungus compounds the sustain nicely.

Two hard counters matter. Malachite elites block healing, which shuts the entire loop off — long runs get genuinely dangerous. And shield-converting items are a disaster on him: they pin his health at a value his abilities cost a percentage of, which is why Transcendence is a hard sell despite being the default answer to most late-game problems.

Recap: spend health freely and heal it back through the primary, keep Weaken permanently applied, use the utility to gather enemies rather than to flee, and avoid anything that converts health to shields or blocks healing.

Loader's damage scales with how fast she's moving, and her grapple gives her more control over her own velocity than any other survivor has. Those two facts make speed her damage stat and make her, functionally, the game's mobility king.

Momentum as damage

Her charged punch does dramatically more damage the faster you're travelling, and you can both sprint and grapple while charging it. Grapple into punch is the core combo and it near-instakills almost anything for most of a run. Aiming into rounded terrain launches you higher, so the same skill doubles as traversal.

Because she takes no fall damage at all, height is free. Dropping off anything costs nothing, which removes an entire category of risk from her movement and makes Eclipse's fall-damage modifier a nonevent for her.

Her passive also grants barrier whenever her gauntlets connect with an enemy. Since that's her normal attack pattern, barrier uptime is effectively constant while fighting, which is how a melee survivor with mediocre health survives being in the middle of everything.

Kit notes

Her basic swing hits multiple enemies, briefly stuns small ones, and — usefully — stalls her slightly in the air when used airborne. The alternate grapple deals damage and stuns, pulling heavy targets toward you and light targets to you instead; it trivialises small flyers, which are otherwise her blind spot, and is a straight upgrade once unlocked.

Her pylon covers the same weakness passively, zapping nearby enemies and proccing on-hit items. Thrown behind you before you leave to explore, it generates free money while you're elsewhere. The alternate special trades that utility for raw burst, which is the better pick once you're comfortable enough with grappling that you don't need the pylon as an anchor point.

While grappling, aim the camera where you want to end up rather than at the surface — the grapple carries you along your view.

Items

Movement speed first, because it is literally damage. Crowbar and both Bands are what turn her punch into a one-shot; crit items are excellent, particularly against bosses. Focus Crystal fits since she's permanently at close range. Armor-Piercing Rounds for boss damage.

There are two schools on attack speed. The burst school ignores it entirely and maximises single-punch damage. The attack-speed school builds around the barrier passive instead — since barrier generation is tied to landing hits and is independent of damage, faster swings mean more barrier. Both work; the attack-speed version is the more forgiving.

Purity is a genuine upgrade for her — she hits hard and rarely, so on-hit chance items underperform while cooldown reduction directly increases how often she can throw her big skills. Pair it with Alien Head or Hardlight Afterburner.

Be aware that shields don't count toward max health and aren't healed, so shield-stacking doesn't protect her from being one-shot the way it appears to. Malachite elites are worth actively avoiding.

Recap: build speed, grapple into every punch, use free fall damage immunity aggressively, and let the barrier passive tank for you. Bands and Crowbars turn her into an assassin; attack speed turns her into a brawler.

Acrid is a damage-over-time and area specialist with no natural defences, which forces a hit-and-run pattern: leap into a group, apply everything, leave before the group responds.

Poison versus Blight

This is the fork that defines his build. Poison deals a percentage of the enemy's maximum health per second, which means it kills anything given enough time — enormously valuable when looping, where enemy health pools become absurd and flat damage stops keeping up. It cannot land the killing blow itself, so a group poisoned and left alone will grind down but need a final push.

Blight stacks and does kill, scaling off your damage rather than the enemy's health. It's the aggressive option: better against tankable low-threat crowds, worse against the high-health, hard-to-dodge targets that actually threaten you.

The argument for Poison is that it shreds exactly the enemies you're worst against — heavily armoured, slow, high-health targets — and counters mechanics like shield regeneration that flat damage struggles with. The argument for Blight is speed and self-sufficiency. Neither is wrong; pick based on whether your runs die to bosses or to time.

The rotation

The core loop is leap into a group, spread the disease with your special, land a bite or two, and get out. His special spreads to a large number of targets and is where he actually shines, particularly against many weak enemies where a fast clear matters more than a big number.

The exception is when you can afford to stay: with barrier-on-kill items, more kills mean more barrier, so the longer a melee fight runs the more robust you become. That flips his entire risk profile and is worth building toward deliberately.

His third primary hit heals, so weaving into small groups sustains him. The damage-focused alternate secondary is his best answer to air; the slayer-tagged alternate is a finisher that triggers on-kill items reliably.

The leap choice is between cooldown-on-hit — which turns a large group into near-continuous mobility and area damage — and the lingering pool version, which offers more movement flexibility but a nearly inert proc coefficient. The former has Band support, which is otherwise almost absent from his kit.

Items

Group clear is the priority: Gasoline and Will-o'-the-Wisp are close to essential, and Ignition Tank multiplies the former substantially. Barrier-on-kill items address the total absence of defence. Movement speed lets the hit-and-run pattern actually work.

Because he spreads debuffs so aggressively, Tri-Tip Daggers scale unusually well on him — each spread carries the bleed along with it. Capped crit plus Harvester's Scythe gives him a real heal, and Focus Crystal is always live given his melee range.

Extra special charges are exceptional on him, since more casts means the disease reaches far more enemies.

Transcendence is a high priority for a non-obvious reason: he can launch himself very high with his leap, and against mostly grounded enemies that's a near-unconditional window of safety in which shields fully regenerate. That converts his one mobility skill into a repeatable full heal.

Recap: poison a crowd and disengage, choose Poison for looping and Blight for speed, and buy area damage plus barrier before anything else. His disease spreads your on-hit debuffs for free, so bleed and status items scale far past their normal value.

Captain trades mobility and defence for damage and utility, and has more strategic levers than anyone else in the roster — two beacons per stage that change how the stage works, plus a passive that quietly rewrites the value of drones.

Microbots and drones

His passive grants an item that blocks incoming ranged attacks at a fixed rate, faster with attack speed. It does not block laser-type attacks, which is why Lesser Wisps, Stone Golems and Titans remain genuine threats to him.

The important part is that it applies to any drones and turrets you purchase, but not to allied monsters from other sources. That single interaction makes buying drones far more valuable on Captain than on anyone else — with enough of them, high-damage projectile enemies simply stop mattering, and the drones also pull aggro from things he cannot dodge. Drone-summoning equipment is correspondingly excellent.

Since it's technically an item, it can be scrapped or multiplied by a Shrine of Order — but it returns at the start of every stage, so losing it is a temporary inconvenience rather than a disaster.

Beacon economy

Two charges per stage, chosen from four types, and you can bring duplicates. This is a resource-allocation puzzle rather than a combat decision.

The hacking beacon reduces nearby purchasables to free over time and is the most consistently valuable. It's best early in a stage when you're broke, and best of all on a Shrine of Chance or the hidden legendary chests on the deeper stages. It cannot affect anything that isn't priced in gold. Later in a run you can afford everything anyway, which is when it loses value.

The healing beacon heals nearby allies continuously. Its sharpest use is enabling Shrine of Blood exploitation — buy stacks with health, then heal it back — and otherwise it's a boss-fight tool. It loses relevance if you've taken Transcendence.

The shocking beacon periodically immobilises nearby enemies and is worth taking when you lack area crowd control, mainly for the boss fight. The resupply beacon recharges equipment and is the weakest of the four solo.

Each beacon's landing deals substantial damage in its radius — more than his own utility skill — but spending a beacon as a bomb wastes its actual purpose and the delivery takes a long time. A queued beacon can be cancelled by sprinting or by attempting to place it somewhere invalid.

Combat kit

His shotgun fires eight pellets and charging tightens the spread, with charge rate scaling off attack speed — enough attack speed turns it into a rifle. Because each pellet is a separate hit, landing all eight on one target makes on-hit items exceptional and triggers armour-shredding effects almost instantly. Charged shots knock back, which is most useful against flyers since they take extra damage from being flung into geometry.

His secondary looks negligible — one weak stunning bullet on a long-ish cooldown — and its real job is setup. It locks down a large target so his orbital utility actually lands, because the delay between firing and impact means most enemies simply walk out otherwise.

That utility is enormous burst, best spent on a full-health tanky target with Crowbars stacked, since Crowbar only pays above the health threshold. Stun into orbital strike is his one-shot. It falls off in a proc-based build later in the run but remains a strong early chunk.

His alternate utility is far more extreme: unusable in general combat, capable of deleting a boss outright, and able to skip the final stage's pillar phase by aiming it at yourself — a trick with enough conditionals attached that it's a genuine choice rather than a free win.

Items

Crowbars are the centrepiece of the burst build and other players rarely want them. Runald's Band is a more consistent one-shot than Crowbar because it has no health threshold. Attack speed serves both his charge rate and his microbot recharge.

He struggles badly against multiple simultaneous threats, so Gasoline and Will-o'-the-Wisp are a heightened priority, and flat mitigation helps specifically against the wisp swarms his microbots can't stop. Bleed builds work extremely well given his pellet count.

Recap: buy drones, spend beacons on economy rather than damage, stun before you strike, and stack Crowbars for the one-shot. His weaknesses — mobility, defence, crowd handling — all have item answers, and his passive makes purchased allies far better than they look.

Railgunner is the single strongest single-target damage dealer in the game and among the worst at everything else. Her entire design is built around one passive interaction and one weakness.

Crit becomes crit damage

Her passive converts crit chance beyond 100% into crit damage, which is the only case in the game where crit glasses never stop scaling. Every weak-point hit while scoped is automatically critical. The result is that the standard crit package — normally capped at ten glasses — becomes an unlimited damage sink for her.

Her scoped shot carries a proc coefficient of 3.0, three times the normal rate. That's the highest in the game and it means her item caps are dramatically lower than anyone else's: four Tri-Tip Daggers guarantee bleed where others need ten, and the bleed lasts three times as long. A small handful of on-hit items goes further on her than a large stack does elsewhere.

The mobility problem

She has no meaningful movement in her base kit and needs distance from her target to aim comfortably. That combination is what holds her back, and solving it is most of playing her well.

The item answer is stacked attack speed. At sufficient stacks the recoil from firing downward becomes genuine flight — around seventeen syringes is the threshold, and the same stacking widens her perfect-reload window and reduces her special's silence penalty as pleasant side effects.

Her utility mine is the other lever: only one can exist at a time, so dropping a second detonates the first, launching you instantly. That's an escape, a vertical boost, and the core of her movement tech.

Vertical mobility items are worth more to her than horizontal ones, because vertical velocity throws enemy aim off universally and works just as well while scoped — sprint speed and lateral dodging items lose most of their value the moment you're aiming.

Skill notes

Her cryo special freezes, which gives her the same execute-threshold trick that Artificer gets — extremely valuable against the final boss. At high damage it also cleanly triggers Bands, which is a simple route to skipping a boss phase outright.

Falling damage is partly self-solving: firing downward while falling kills momentum.

Items

Lean into damage, but deliberately buy some area coverage — her weakness against clusters of small enemies is what actually kills her, not a lack of single-target output. Movement speed, then crit, then anything that multiplies a single enormous hit: Crowbar, Laser Scope, Bands.

She fights and dodges poorly at the same time, so on high Eclipse where every hit costs permanent health she has a genuinely rough time. Buy defensive layers earlier than her damage suggests you need to.

Recap: her passive makes crit unlimited and her scoped coefficient makes on-hit items triple value, so a few items go a very long way. Solve mobility with attack-speed recoil flight and mine detonation, and buy area damage to cover the swarms she can't handle.

Void Fiend has two complete skill sets governed by a corruption meter, and the meter is the build. Everything about item selection follows from deciding whether you want to control the transformation or live permanently in one form.

How the meter moves

Corruption rises from taking damage, from landing critical hits, and passively over time. It falls when you heal. His special either burns a chunk of the meter and heals heavily, or you let it fill and transform.

Controlled form is defensive and grounded: his primary is an infinite-range slowing beam with aim assist and his dash carries him upward. Corrupted form flips everything to offence and pulls him to effectively melee range.

The consequence that catches people out is that healing fights the build. If you want corrupted uptime, most healing items are actively working against you.

Two builds

The corruption build stacks crit and attack speed so that landing hits continuously feeds the meter — with enough of both, corrupted form becomes permanent as long as there's something to hit. Transcendence is the top priority here, because shield regeneration is not healing and therefore doesn't drain the meter, which makes it both his tankiness and his only viable recovery in corrupted form. Barrier from other sources behaves similarly.

The healing build does the opposite: forsake Transcendence, stack void items until healing uptime is effectively continuous, and stay comfortably at high health while dipping into corruption deliberately. It's more forgiving and less dependent on finding one specific lunar.

The corruption build wants Focus Crystal in quantity — corrupted form is nearly melee — plus extra secondary charges, which also enable his movement tech. Fire damage items and Ignition Tanks give him the area coverage he otherwise lacks.

Items

Void items are unusually good on him beyond thematic fit: Plasma Shrimp in particular is close to mandatory for the corruption build, and Polylute, Needletick, Voidsent Flame and Tentabauble are all worth grabbing on sight. Purity is workable but deletes the void proc items, so it's an either-or.

Because you'll be passing on a stream of healing items you can't use, a Recycler is far more valuable in his hands than in most.

Avoid the Heresy lunars entirely — as of recent patches they replace both the controlled and corrupted variants of the skills they overwrite, which costs you half your kit.

Shaped Glass is reasonable if you trust your survivability, and movement speed generally deserves priority over yet more damage, since his damage is already excessive.

For weakness-patching, lean attack speed and on-hit if teleporter bosses are where you struggle, and movement speed if pacing is the problem — his controlled primary's cross-map range lets you kill from a distance while shopping efficiently.

Recap: decide early whether you're controlling the meter or living in corruption, and buy accordingly. Crit and attack speed for permanent corruption with Transcendence as your health bar, or heavy void-item healing for the safer version. Either way, audit healing pickups before taking them.

The Heretic cannot be selected at the start of a run. She is reached by assembling all four Heresy lunar items mid-run, each of which replaces one skill; the fourth completes the transformation. Any survivor can become her.

The trade

She has the highest base health and base damage in the game, moves faster than everyone else, and jumps three times. In exchange she loses health continuously instead of regenerating, and that drain is the single constraint every item decision routes through.

Two quirks are worth knowing. The degeneration is proportionally weaker on Monsoon than on Drizzle, which is counterintuitive but real. And it consumes shields before health, so shield sources absorb it first — which makes them a partial answer rather than merely a buffer.

Assembling her

The practical route is a Bazaar visit via a Newt altar, at whatever stage you like. The Lunar Shop Refresher lets you reroll until the items appear, so it no longer requires Artifact of Command. With Command, any four non-equipment lunar pods will do.

Choose your starting survivor for what they contribute before and through the transformation. Captain is the standout, because his Microbots persist through it and block projectiles well — though turrets bought after transforming don't inherit them.

Never use a Cleansing Pool as Heretic. It strips lunar items, which means it strips her abilities.

Playing her

Her primary fires a large magazine of tracking shards that detonate after a delay. The reload timer resets with every shot, so continuous fire eventually creates a forced dead window — manage the burst rather than emptying the magazine at the wrong moment.

Her secondary is a charged projectile that damages continuously then explodes and roots, with the hold duration controlling travel distance. Her special applies stacking marks passively while off cooldown, then detonates all of them on every marked enemy simultaneously — through walls, at unlimited range. Bleed and drones do not apply the marks.

Her utility is the whole survival plan: it makes her intangible, speeds her up, and heals a large chunk of her maximum health per use. Fire it while the special is on cooldown so the special recharges during the fade and is ready the moment you return. It doesn't protect against shrine or fall damage, cannot be cancelled early, doesn't remove damage-over-time, and you can interact with objects but not use skills while faded. Too many stacks of it is a real downside — you spend more time out of reality than in it.

The optimal pattern is to stack marks on tanky targets with primary and secondary together, then detonate.

Countering the drain

How hard you need to counter depends on how early you transform. Raising the maximum health pool buys time against a fixed drain. On-hit and on-crit healing scales naturally with her high proc rates — the crit-heal item wants glasses built alongside it, capped at ten, or nine if you also hold one of the flat-crit greens.

Anything that increases health cost is lethal on her: the stat-boost tonic actively kills her faster, and self-damage equipment is out of the question.

Recap: she's a statline with a timer attached. Route to the Bazaar, refresh for the four lunars, pick a host survivor whose contribution survives the change, and spend every item slot on outpacing the drain. Use the fade as both dodge and heal, and detonate marks on the things that actually threaten you.

The core skill

Her signature utility is among the best skills in the game — it's simultaneously movement, damage and invulnerability. The catch is that it costs health continuously and auto-aborts below a health threshold, so its duration is bounded by how much damage you can afford to take from yourself.

The Transcendence trap

Transcendence is normally the best lunar in the game and it is bad on Seeker. Her core skill aborts below a health threshold, and Transcendence pins her health at one — so she is always beneath the threshold, and the skill terminates the instant it would cost her anything. Items

Flat damage mitigation is her single best common item — worth taking over most legendaries, because it directly extends how long she can stay in her core skill before the self-damage forces her out. Finding a printer for it is close to a win condition.

Recap: live inside the utility, buy flat mitigation to extend it, take the revive special on hard difficulties, and never take Transcendence. Her damage, mobility and defence are all the same skill, so anything that shortens it costs you everything at once.

The drone engine

His healing-drone ability grants stacking movement speed, attack speed and shields, which means the drones are simultaneously his damage and his defensive layer. Spammed at short intervals the buffs stay stacked continuously, and on high difficulty that stacking is what keeps him alive.

The obvious consequence is that gold spent on drones is spent on your build. Where most survivors treat a drone as a nice extra, Operator should be routing purchases toward them deliberately — and the drone-upgrade items that improve every drone you own scale correspondingly hard.

Recap: spend gold on drones because drones are the build, keep the ability loop running for the stacking buffs, and hold enough personal survivability to survive the moment your drones die.

Stages & Routing

The stage graph is not a straight line. Several detours give you items without advancing the difficulty coefficient, and several others commit you to ending the run whether you meant to or not. Knowing which is which turns routing from a series of accidents into a plan.

The shape of a run

Stages repeat in bands of five, with an end stage after each set. Every band offers several environment variants and one gets picked per run; the DLCs added more options at most slots plus an entirely parallel branch. The number attached to the stage matters more than the environment name — several routes and secrets are gated on absolute stage number rather than on which map you're standing in.

Portals only appear after you complete the teleporter event. If nothing has spawned, the boss isn't dead. That single rule stops a lot of confused searching.

The blue portal to the Bazaar can appear randomly on any stage, and a Newt altar buys one outright. The void portal appears only after looping. The celestial portal is guaranteed after a loop rather than random.

Green versus red doors

Some detours return you to the loop and some are one-way. The Bazaar, Void Fields, Gilded Coast, Bulwark's Ambry and A Moment Fractured all let you continue looping afterwards. The Moon, the Planetarium, Void Locus and A Moment Whole do not — entering any of them means the run ends there, win or lose. Take the returnable ones freely; take the terminal ones only when you intend to finish.

The free detours

Gilded Coast is the standout because the clock does not run there — no time cost, no stage penalty. It's four guaranteed chests plus a boss item, gated only on beating Aurelionite. Enter via an Altar of Gold on any stage if you can afford it without grinding, or via the Bazaar's stage-selection seer. If you can beat the boss, there is no reason to skip it.

Void Fields is the purest expression of the risk-versus-time trade. It costs real time and real danger — a hostile enemy type is added to the pool for the rest of the run, and enemies picking up crowbars there become instant one-shot threats — but it yields a lot of items and gives you genuine choice about which, which is worth more than the raw count. Go when you are one or two items from a build coming together, or when you are far enough behind that you need the injection.

Void Fields has a quiet routing side effect worth exploiting: entering and exiting it during stage 1 or 2 makes the game treat your stage 3 as a stage 4, which means a legendary chest can spawn there.

Bulwark's Ambry is reached from the Compound Generator on Sky Meadow, sits on an island below the main area, and returns you to the loop. Monsters don't spawn on the generator island, though they will follow you down.

The Bazaar is the hub for everything else, and its real value is as a decision point rather than a shop. It costs you nothing on the clock, so use the visit to take stock: review your inventory, decide what build you're actually going for, judge how far ahead of the curve you are, and decide whether Void Fields is the right next move.

Stage selection

The Lunar Seer in the Bazaar guarantees your next environment for a small lunar coin cost, removing the variant roll. There's a catch worth knowing: the guarantee only holds if you leave the Bazaar through the blue portal. Exiting via any other portal re-randomises the destination.

This matters for more than convenience. It's the reliable route to the specific stage-4 map you need for a survivor unlock, and after looping begins it becomes the only way to reach several environments that the normal teleporter no longer offers.

Stage-by-stage priorities

Stage 1 is where you drop in, and it's the only place a Fuel Array can be obtained — which matters for one survivor unlock that requires carrying it several stages without dropping below half health.

Stage 2 hides two permanent unlocks on the base-game maps, both worth doing once. The desert map has a sealed chamber behind an ancient gate requiring two pressure plates pressed simultaneously, drawn from eight possible locations — a Radar Scanner marks them. Solo, you need weighted objects to hold them: explosive pots, an Engineer turret, a Loader pylon, a Captain beacon, or Effigy of Grief all work. Inside are two unique elites that drop both Bands and permanently add them to the item pool. The swamp map hides an altar under a cliff; destroying the skeleton hanging over the pit permanently unlocks a legendary. Both are one-time investments that improve every subsequent run.

Stage 3 carries the game's most time-sensitive secret: a locked chest that must be reached before the run clock hits ten minutes. It always contains a Preon Accumulator, and opening it the first time adds that equipment to the pool permanently. The display counts down; a negative number means you already missed it. This is a concrete reason to keep the early stages brisk — the reward is permanent and the window is absolute.

Both original stage 3 maps have guaranteed Newt altars, which makes stage 3 a reliable Bazaar access point when you need one.

Stage 4 maps all contain a legendary chest. This is the natural point to convert accumulated gold into a red item, and arriving earlier makes it cheaper since prices scale with the coefficient. The right sequencing is to buy a stack of cheap whites first and then use teleporter event money for the big chest, rather than hoarding from arrival and going without items in the meantime.

Stage 5 holds the Compound Generator for artifact access, and its teleporter is the fork: aligned to the moon it sends you to the final stage, aligned to the planet it loops you.

Newt altars and where they hide

Altars appear at fixed candidate locations rather than spawning randomly, so learning the spots for the maps you see most is a permanent improvement to your Bazaar access.

The spawn rules vary by map and variant in ways worth knowing. Some maps roll zero-or-one, meaning you can genuinely find none. Others guarantee exactly one. A few guarantee one and then roll additional ones on top. And at least one candidate location can be rendered inaccessible by terrain — a cave whose entrances have collapsed.

The practical habit is to check altar locations while you're already exploring for chests, rather than making a dedicated trip. An altar found on the way costs nothing; an altar hunted for costs coefficient.

The final stage

Commencement gates its boss behind a pillar-charging phase, and the pillar types are not equivalent. Some charge slowly with no complications. Some emit shockwaves that don't damage but push you away and apply a crippling debuff — jumping repeatedly dodges them. Some shrink their radius as they charge and lose progress rapidly if you leave the zone. And the fastest ones drain your health for the entire duration, which is dangerous on its own and much more so under difficulty modifiers that convert damage into permanent health loss.

Two features of the map are worth planning around. The cauldron cluster on one end offers a set of item trades including a variant unique to this stage that converts one red item into three of a displayed item — a genuine build-finisher. And a Shrine of Order always spawns on a floating rock behind one of the pillar areas, which can either complete a run or destroy it depending on how concentrated your inventory already is.

After the boss dies, returning to the starting teleporter ring reveals a frog. Interacting costs a lunar coin, and with the void content enabled, doing so ten times opens a portal to a second, harder ending — a deliberate choice to extend the run rather than escape.

The Path of the Colossus

The newer DLC added a parallel branch entered through green portals, which behaves differently from the main line. Its final environment contains a shrine that either returns you to normal progression or ends the run as an alternate ending.

Access is fussier than it looks. Completing that final environment removes the green ball from subsequent teleporters, and the gold realm stops generating green portals afterwards. The other environments on the branch remain reachable via the Bazaar's stage selection, or through the green ball attached to the shrine that appears on the first two stages — the same shrine on later stages only ever carries a gold ball instead.

There's also a one-per-run hidden event in the Bazaar: if you haven't completed the branch's final environment, small collectibles may generate at fixed locations around the realm. Three of them spawning triggers an audio cue, and shooting all three opens a green portal on the path between the blue portal and the shop.

Looping

Looping restarts the stage bands at dramatically higher difficulty, since the stage multiplier compounds across the entire run rather than resetting. The second loop's economy is completely different from the first: a single teleporter event funds essentially every interactable on the map, so the correct pattern flips to teleporter-first, shop-after.

Several routes only open once you're looping — void portals appear at stage-gated points across the bands, and the celestial portal becomes guaranteed rather than random. Conversely, some environments become unreachable by normal teleporter after the loop begins, accessible only through stage selection.

The decision of whether to loop at all is really a decision about what you want from the run. Looping is where enemy scaling starts to genuinely outpace player scaling, and where the character of the game shifts from building a machine to seeing how long the machine holds.

Recap

Learn which detours return you to the loop and which end the run, and take the free ones every time. Keep stages 1 through 3 brisk enough to catch the timed chest, use Newt altars found incidentally rather than hunted deliberately, and treat the Bazaar as your planning checkpoint. Arrive at stage 4 with gold to spend and a plan for the legendary chest, and decide before Sky Meadow whether you're finishing or looping.

Stage Economy

Gold is the resource you convert time into and items are what you convert gold into, but the exchange rate is not fixed and the routing between them matters. Understanding which container is worth opening, which shrine is worth praying at, and how printers choose what to eat is most of the difference between a run that snowballs and one that stalls.

How prices and income move

All gold-priced interactables scale with the difficulty coefficient, but the price is locked in when you enter the stage rather than recalculated continuously. That means the cost of everything on the current map is already fixed — spending twenty minutes there doesn't make the chests more expensive, though it does make the next stage's chests more expensive.

Enemy gold drops scale the same way and also step on stage change. The net effect is that the ratio between "gold available" and "chest price" stays roughly constant, so opening loot is fundamentally a question of how much time you're willing to spend killing versus how much difficulty that time buys — which is the same trade that governs everything else.

Multiplayer inflates prices substantially per additional player, which is one of the reasons large lobbies must clear maps rather than skim them.

One free source is worth naming: barrels scale with difficulty like enemy drops and are uniquely not locked down during the teleporter event. When everything else is caged, barrels are still openable, which makes them a real income stream during a long boss fight.

Which container to open

Not all chests are equal and the differences are large enough to reorder your route.

A basic chest overwhelmingly gives a common, with roughly a fifth chance of an uncommon and a low single-digit chance of a legendary. The category-specific chests cost modestly more and guarantee an item from their category, but roll the same rarity distribution — so you're paying for control over what kind of item, not what quality.

A large chest costs double and guarantees at least uncommon, with a genuinely significant chance — roughly one in five — of a legendary. That is a dramatically better rate per gold than a small chest for red items specifically, and it's why large chests should be prioritised when you're hunting reds rather than volume.

The dedicated legendary chest guarantees a red at roughly sixteen to twenty times the small-chest price. Two stage-4 maps each have a guaranteed spawn location for one, and learning those locations is the backbone of getting reds reliably. Affording it usually means farming, so weigh the difficulty cost — but it's also exactly what the teleporter event's payout is for.

Multishops offer three items of identical rarity at the price of the equivalent chest, and buying one closes the rest. The value is control at chest prices. Note that the random "?" terminal does not roll a better rarity — it just hides the item.

The adaptive chest costs double a small chest and runs a cycling slot machine you stop by interacting again. The cycle accelerates the longer you wait, so read early; wait too long and you get a random result anyway. Pausing the game mid-cycle to read the current item, then unpausing and interacting immediately, is a reliable way to actually pick.

Equipment barrels always give equipment, which makes them a straight skip when you're already holding something you like.

Two containers are free. Cloaked chests are genuinely free items and genuinely rare — Radar Scanner marks them, and they get caged during the teleporter event, which makes the event the easiest time to spot one. Rusty lockboxes appear only if someone is holding a key and only from the next stage after picking it up; they roll the large-chest distribution, which makes a single key close to a free uncommon-or-better every stage.

Shrines

Shrine of Chance starts cheaper than a chest and rises roughly a third per attempt, shutting down permanently after granting two items. It can output anything including equipment. After three or four failures the price has run away from you — walk off rather than chasing.

Shrine of Blood converts health into gold at a rate based on the health actually lost, so it pays far more on a large health pool. Its escalating cost across three uses is steep, and it refuses to activate if your current health is below the required fraction. It's excellent for immediately affording nearby chests and dangerous without healing or while under pressure. Captain's healing beacon and medical drones both exist partly to make this exploitable.

Shrine of the Woods creates a permanent healing zone, and each additional activation increases only the radius, not the rate. The heal is small next to what item combinations produce, so its real value is coverage — worth activating if it sits near the teleporter or another place you'll be fighting for a while.

Shrine of the Mountain doubles the teleporter boss director's credits and doubles the event's loot. Multiple shrines stack additively rather than multiplicatively — two shrines give three loot items per player, not four.

The key insight is that the doubling applies to the current credit pool, and that pool scales with stage and difficulty. On stage 1 the pool is tiny, so doubling it produces a marginally harder fight for a fully doubled reward — an extremely cheap trade. On a late loop stage the same doubling applies to an enormous pool and can produce a fight you simply lose. Always slam early shrines; think carefully about late ones. Note they don't spawn on the third stage of a loop.

The extra loot also carries a chance to be replaced by a boss item if a boss capable of dropping one appears, so mountain shrines are a real route to boss items rather than just volume.

Shrine of Combat spawns a group slightly above current difficulty for no reward beyond their gold. Early it's a fine way to convert time into money without waiting; late it can dump a genuinely lethal pack on you.

Shrine of Order is the biggest gamble in the game. It counts your items by rarity class, picks one item you already own in each class, and converts everything else in that class into it. Thirty commons becoming thirty Crowbars wins the run outright; thirty commons becoming thirty War Banners ends it. It discards every synergy you've built. Take it when your inventory is already concentrated enough that most outcomes are acceptable, or when the run is lost anyway.

Newt Altar guarantees a blue portal after the teleporter event, and must be activated before the teleporter starts or the coin is wasted.

Scrap, printers and cauldrons

This system is misunderstood more often than any other, and the misunderstanding is expensive.

Scrap is not a currency. A piece of white scrap is simply another white item sitting in your inventory. Its one special property is that it gets consumed before real items when something takes an item of that rarity. That's the entire mechanic, and it's why scrapping is useful: it converts junk into safe feedstock so a printer eats the scrap instead of your good stack.

The colour rules are strict and asymmetric. Printers are rarity-locked and consume only matching-colour scrap — green scrap is invisible to a white printer. Cauldrons and traders convert upward, so a green-item trader accepts white scrap and a red-item trader accepts green scrap; the cost colour is the tier you're trading up from, not the output tier.

The consequence catches everyone out at least once: red scrap is nearly dead weight. No trader accepts it, and only a red printer — which is very rare — can use it. Scrapping reds you don't want is usually a mistake.

The other critical rule is that when a printer or vat pulls a random item from your inventory, it rolls over every item individually, not per stack. A stack of thirty is thirty times as likely to be eaten as a singleton. If you want to feed the printer your one junk item and keep your thirty syringes, the odds are overwhelmingly against you — which is exactly the problem scrap solves.

Conversion vats trade several lower-rarity items for one higher-rarity item, and in raw count that's usually a bad deal. Whether it's actually bad depends entirely on what's being consumed versus what you gain, and the random-sourcing risk applies here too.

One practical warning: burn your scrap before the final boss. During his item-stealing phase your items return progressively as you damage him, and leftover scrap comes back first — creating a long stretch where you're being given nothing that helps while the fight continues. And never feed a used revive item to a scrapper; it has no corresponding scrap tier and the machine simply hangs.

Drones

Drones do some killing for you, unreliably, and unlike items they can be destroyed. The general rule is items first, drones with spare gold — with the notable exception of Captain, whose passive protects them and inverts that ordering entirely.

They fall off hard late. By the deep stages everything's damage is enormous and you have almost no way to protect them, so they die unnoticed in large fights. Their genuine value is early-to-mid as a distraction and a damage sponge, though drawing enemy fire is not always what you want.

Among the types, missile drones operate more independently because their projectiles home and re-seek new targets when one dies, making them good against swarms. Gunner drones harass air effectively and get stuck on terrain. Medical drones are excellent early specifically for recovering from shrine-of-blood usage, while the emergency variant only helps allies who are actually in trouble rather than topping up healthy ones. Incinerator drones hit far harder and die far faster.

Turrets carry over between stages, appearing around you on arrival, which makes them better value than their fragility suggests. The large repairable turret found on the middle stages costs a great deal but focuses targets down fast — worth doing at least once, and worth watching, since its hitbox invites return fire.

The equipment drone is a special case: it permanently consumes your equipment and periodically fires it. That's excellent with offensive equipment and completely wasted on self-targeted ones, and you don't get the equipment back when the drone dies.

Engineering legendary density

If your goal is reds specifically rather than items generally, several items compound into a genuine engine.

The delivery item spawns a special two-terminal multishop once per stage, and unlike a key it is not consumed — so it keeps delivering every stage for as long as you hold it. Each stack raises the legendary chance of the delivered items, and the odds of at least one red climb steeply with stacks: a single copy is near-negligible, around ten copies is roughly a coin flip every stage, and heavy stacking pushes it well past that.

That pairs with the refund item, which does more than return gold — buying from a multishop no longer closes the other terminals. Without it, a delivery that rolls two legendaries only ever gives you one. With it, both are yours.

The bonus-item chest modifier grants an extra roll from the chest's own loot table, with all stacks consumed on the first chest you open each stage and regenerating each stage. Sequencing is therefore everything: spend them on the highest-rarity source available. On a stage-4 map that means walking to the guaranteed legendary chest first, since every extra roll then comes off a table that's guaranteed red. Everywhere else, prefer a large chest over a small one.

The shrine-luck item raises the chance that a successful Shrine of Chance yields a higher rarity, and past a threshold it guarantees a pool that excludes commons and equipment entirely. Because it's a random roll, Clover rerolls its failures too.

The teleporter event

Activating creates a bubble that at least one player must stay inside for the charge to progress, and it charges faster with more players inside. Everything except barrels gets locked behind a cage for the duration — which is exactly why banking gold beforehand and spending it afterwards is correct.

The charge caps just short of complete while any boss lives, so bosses must die regardless of the timer. Off-screen bosses are marked with an arrow. Boss loot spawns on the teleporter the moment the bosses die, even if the charge hasn't finished.

Default loot is one uncommon per player, all the same item, with each item independently able to roll into a boss item — but only from a boss the event itself spawned. Mountain shrines add one extra per player each.

Orbs circling the pedestal announce which portals will open afterwards. There's no limit on how many can appear, and they don't overwrite each other, but you can only take one.

Leaving converts remaining gold into experience. Kills made while the gold is draining also convert; kills made after it has fully drained carry a small amount of gold into the next stage, which is a minor but free head start.

Recap

Prices lock on stage entry, so the map you're standing on won't get more expensive. Prefer large chests when hunting reds and learn the guaranteed legendary spawns. Scrap the tier you intend to spend and never hoard red scrap. Slam early mountain shrines and think hard about late ones. Buy drones with spare gold unless you're Captain. And bank your money before the teleporter, because the event pays for the rest of the map.

Lunar & Void

These are the two item categories you should approach with opposite instincts. Lunar items carry real downsides and should only be taken when you have a specific reason and intend to build around them. Void items are usually free upgrades and should be taken by default — unless you're specifically building around the item they'd overwrite.

The lunar economy

Lunar coins persist between runs and accumulate at a reasonable rate through normal play, which means treating them as a scarce hoard is a mistake. The healthy approach is to bank up to a comfortable stockpile — somewhere in the fifty-to-eighty range — and then spend freely, because at that level a handful of purchases per run costs you nothing you won't earn back.

Lunar items are not permanent progress. They last only until you win, die, or feed them to a Cleansing Pool, so nothing you buy represents a long-term loss. Permanent character unlocks, by contrast, cost a trivial fraction of lifetime coin income and are pure gain — buy those without hesitation.

The other thing coins purchase is access. Even if you never buy a single lunar item, opening a Newt altar to reach the Bazaar buys you next-stage selection and the cauldron trades, both of which are frequently worth more than any lunar pod. Pods cost one coin each and are cheaper per item than Bazaar purchases, so the two are genuinely different transactions rather than one being strictly better.

Which lunars are worth it

Transcendence is the single most run-defining lunar in the game and on hard difficulties it's close to a priority target in its own right. Converting your health pool to regenerating shields solves an entire category of problems: shields recover on their own without you needing to find healing, they refill instantly at the start of each stage, they absorb the health costs of shrines and pillars, and they're immune to the modifiers that punish healing. Routing to the Bazaar specifically to look for it is a legitimate plan.

Its cost is that it makes every healing item in your inventory dead. That's usually a gain rather than a loss — the dead healing items become free scrap for printers — but audit your build before taking it. Several survivors are actively harmed: REX loses the self-heal that funds his kit, Seeker's core skill aborts permanently, Engineer's stationary-turret healing strategy stops working, and Void Fiend's corruption control changes shape entirely.

Shaped Glass is exponential damage at exponential fragility, and its reputation depends heavily on what else you're holding. Paired with Transcendence the halving penalty applies to a shield pool that regenerates on its own, and printing shield generators rebuilds what it took — which is how a genuinely dangerous item becomes reasonable. Without that support, the honest assessment is that eventually you cannot dodge everything, and on a long run it will kill you. It's also the classic rescue item for a run that's already failing: if you're behind, the damage may pull you back ahead, and you can play carefully afterwards.

Gesture of the Drowned halves equipment cooldown at the cost of firing your equipment automatically, which turns it from a downside into an engine depending on what you're holding. Combined with charge-extending items it produces near-continuous equipment uptime, which is how missile launchers become permanent, capacitors become constant, and the stat-boost tonic's affliction downside effectively stops mattering.

Brittle Crown is discussed in the pacing section as an economy item, and it's worth reiterating that its downside is small if you spend continuously and large if you hoard.

Purity is covered in detail under items — genuinely good on cooldown-oriented survivors with no proc items, genuinely run-ending otherwise.

Three lunars are widely considered dead weight regardless of build: the self-damaging meteorite, the crippling effigy on any survivor who fights inside it, and the healing item whose penalty outweighs its benefit.

The Heresy set is a special case: four lunars that replace individual skills and, collectively, transform you into a different survivor. Taking one or two of them is a skill-replacement decision; taking all four is a run plan.

The Cleansing Pool

The pool consumes a random lunar item and returns a pearl — usually the standard version, occasionally the stronger one. It's generally an upgrade over a junk lunar, but the constraint is that finding the pool and having a lunar you want to discard on the same stage is rare.

The critical warning: never use it as the Heretic, since stripping lunar items strips her abilities.

Void items and how corruption works

Each void item corresponds to an existing item, and once you hold the void version, every future pickup of the original transforms into the void version. That lockout is permanent for the rest of the run, and it — not the stats — is the real cost. You're not just gaining an item, you're deleting a base item from your run.

The default rule is take them. Void items are generally powerful, often strictly better than what they replace, and the corruption only matters if you were specifically building around the parent item.

Acquisition

Void cradles cost half your maximum health to open and release infestors that convert nearby enemies into dangerous elites. So each one costs health and a fight. On difficulties where damage taken becomes permanent, open them only after the teleporter event, with the area cleared.

Void seeds spawn at stage start and produce a wave of corrupted elites, but typically offer several cradles — which makes them a very good deal relative to the fight. They're also worth a surprising amount of gold, especially if you claim kills while the normal and void enemies are still fighting each other, and the stalks inside contribute meaningfully too. That income is enough that you can often start the teleporter immediately on arrival and let seed money plus event money fund a complete sweep of the map without ever waiting on enemy spawns — a substantial time saving.

The one time to skip a seed is when it's crowded with dangerous enemies and far from the teleporter, where the fight costs more than the items are worth.

The key-corrupting void item is the cleanest source of all: it spawns a cache each stage that costs no health and releases no infestors, and is consumed when opened.

Which corruptions to accept

Most are upgrades. The healing fungus corruption is the clearest — the base item heals while standing still, which is a losing proposition for anyone who isn't Engineer, while the void version heals while sprinting, which everyone can actually do. It's best on survivors who attack while sprinting. The only argument against it is that under a shield build you'd rather keep the base item as printer fodder.

The missile corruption removes the random chance entirely — you fire missiles on every hit as long as your shield holds, and it grants its own shield to make that easy. Under a shield-heavy build it's a bigger damage increase than the original with none of the randomness, and it chains with the lightning corruption spectacularly, since each lightning strike can trigger further missiles.

That lightning corruption converts an area item into a single-target one: repeated strikes on the same enemy rather than chaining outward. Take it when you already have crowd control from your kit and want boss damage; keep the original if crowds are what kills you.

The block corruption trades random blocking for guaranteed blocking on a recharge. It's largely preference, since a deep stack of the original is already consistent — but it has one inarguable edge: its cooldown registers as a debuff, which means a cleansing equipment can remove it. With enough equipment cooldown reduction that's permanent uptime, which is as close to invulnerable as the game gets.

The bleed corruption applies a delayed detonation that hits hard with attack speed but cannot itself proc items, so it falls off in chain builds. The band corruption groups enemies into a black hole before detonating — much better crowd control, but on a doubled cooldown, so the originals are sometimes simply better.

The special-charge corruption is usually worse than its parent, because equipment is universal while special skills vary wildly in quality — take it only when your special is genuinely your best skill. The slow corruption gates immobilisation behind a random roll where the original always applies its slow, making it a downgrade on an already-weak item.

The revive corruption gives you an extra life and converts every corruptible item you own on resurrection. In solo that's a much better deal than it sounds — a second life is worth far more than item purity — and it doubles as the fastest way to obtain a specific void item you can't otherwise find.

The ones to think hard about

The clover corruption upgrades several random items a tier each stage, which sounds excellent and generally isn't. Low-tier items are more useful than their rarity suggests — movement speed commons in particular are what let you dodge, and you cannot dodge with mobility that got upgraded into something else. It also destroys one of the best items in the game to exist. Avoid unless you're deliberately farming logbook entries.

The crit corruption swaps crit for a small instant-kill chance on non-boss enemies. Against crowds that's devastating and against bosses it does nothing — so it's a looping item, and a poor choice if you're heading for a final boss.

The boss-item corruption summons void allies who act as bullet shields, with the significant catch that void enemies explode lethally on death — including on you. Bringing it into a realm where every enemy is a void enemy turns the stage into a minefield.

Recap

Spend lunar coins freely once you've banked a working stockpile, and treat Bazaar access as the main purchase rather than the pods. Prioritise Transcendence unless your survivor is one of the handful it breaks. Take void items by default, open cradles when the area is clear, and treat void seeds as an income source that funds an entire stage. The only corruptions worth refusing are the ones that delete an item your build genuinely depends on.

Staying Alive

Late in any long run, enemies hit for more than your entire health bar. That single fact reorders everything about defence: healing stops being a survival plan, mitigation becomes the answer, and the items that block one hit outright become worth more than items that restore ten times as much health.

One-shot protection

The mechanic underpinning all late-game survival is that no attack — with one exception — can kill you outright if you're at or near full health. A hit that would have deleted you gets capped instead, leaving you alive on a sliver.

The practical consequence is that being topped up is itself a defensive layer, and a free one. Every point of health you're missing is protection you've already spent. This is the real argument for carrying some reliable healing even in a build that doesn't otherwise want it — not to survive the hit, but to restore the protection before the next one.

The protection has limits worth knowing. It doesn't help against multi-hit attacks, against damage over time, or against several enemies connecting simultaneously — all of which read as instant death regardless. And void explosions bypass it entirely, which is why void enemies remain lethal no matter how healthy you are.

On Eclipse the curse modifier disables part of your health bar with every hit taken, which also disables the protection — making items that flatly negate a single attack substantially more important there than anywhere else.

The defence ladder

Ordered by how much they actually save you:

Kill things before they attack. This is genuinely the best defence and genuinely unavailable most of the time in a first loop. It becomes real once your build comes online, and it's why damage items are defensive items in disguise.

Don't get hit. Movement speed is the meat of defence in this game. Hopoo Feather deserves specific mention as one of the strongest damage-avoidance items available, because vertical mobility defeats a startling proportion of enemy attacks — most things in this game aim horizontally.

Mitigate. After dodging, mitigation beats healing, and the gap widens as the run gets longer.

Heal. Last, for a specific reason covered below.

Two kinds of mitigation

Defensive items split into two families that reward completely different play, and knowing which you're buying matters.

Low-uptime, high-impact items negate one hit entirely then go on cooldown. If you're good enough to take at most one hit per cooldown window, these are effectively invincibility, which means they scale with your skill. Safer Spaces is the standout — it blocks a full hit regardless of that hit's size, for free, on a cooldown that can be reduced. Oddly-Shaped Opal is the same idea with a cooldown that can't be shortened and only partial negation, with hard diminishing returns past three or four stacks. Warped Echo belongs to the same family.

Permanent or near-permanent uptime items reduce damage continuously but by less. Repulsion Armor Plate subtracts a flat amount from every incoming hit, which makes it superb against high-rate low-damage attacks, multi-hit attacks and burns, and nearly worthless against a single enormous hit — so it's excellent early and falls off hard late. Rose Buckler reduces damage while sprinting, and since you sprint almost constantly it's effectively permanent; each stack is worth a substantial chunk of effective health, especially on agile survivors.

Personal Shield Generator increases effective health through a different mechanism than damage reduction, which means the two multiply rather than overlapping. Kinetic Dampener reduces damage specifically while shielded, so a few shield generators turn it into near-permanent mitigation.

Tougher Times sits awkwardly between the families: it blocks damage completely and ignores damage type entirely, but on a random roll. Statistically it's very strong and in the moment it's unreliable — it may simply not trigger when it matters. Its hyperbolic curve has heavy diminishing returns per stack, but because it's damage-type-agnostic the tail still matters: doubling a large stack meaningfully cuts total damage taken even when the percentage moves only a few points. If you find a printer for it, going all-in is a legitimate plan.

Flat maximum-health items are comparatively weak against the percentage-based and mitigation options, though they do help the one-shot protection threshold and reduce the proportional cost of curse damage.

Why healing ranks last

Some reliable healing is vital, because it restores your one-shot protection. But as a survival plan it fails at exactly the point you need it: when every enemy hits for more than your total health, healing only saves you if you can get back above the protection threshold between one hit and the next, which is frequently impossible.

Compare directly: a single-block item plus a partial-block item buys you two extra attacks outright, unconditionally. Healing rarely matches that in the same window. Very heavy healing is a viable survival plan, but you need noticeably more of it than you'd need mitigation for the same result.

On Eclipse this gets worse — healing is halved, then curse damage is layered on top, which brings it close to useless.

One structural note: the DLCs added most of the game's genuinely powerful defensive items. Base-game-only play really does come down to avoiding damage rather than mitigating it, which changes what "playing well" means.

Elites

Elites are ordinary enemies with an added trait, and the traits are almost all about your position relative to them rather than raw numbers. Each one adds a decision where previously there was none, which is why a single elite in a group changes how you approach the whole fight.

Glacial leaves a visible sphere on death that bursts after a moment and freezes anyone inside. That turns "kill it" into "kill it, from where, and with what escape available" — the correct habit is either killing them at range or deliberately keeping a movement option in reserve before you commit.

Blazing leaves fire trails while moving and applies damage over time. It's far more dangerous to melee survivors than ranged ones, and against a group of them a melee character's entire approach has to change. Fighting at the extreme edge of your reach is the practical answer.

Malachite suppresses your healing, which turns an area into a planning problem and shuts down any build whose survival model is regeneration. REX and other sustain-driven survivors should treat them as priority targets rather than obstacles.

Celestine hides nearby monsters completely. Removing enemy visual information changes the entire spatial dynamic of a fight — you cannot dodge what you can't see, so the correct response is to disengage and reposition rather than fight blind.

Overloading works on timing rather than space: the top half of its health regenerates if you leave it alone. Damage normally persists in this game, so you can retreat, recover and return — this elite removes that option and makes finishing it a priority. It also leaves a lingering effect when it hits you.

Two newer elite types are worth knowing in their current form. The twisted variant now telegraphs a large charged attack above its head, which creates a readable window — kill it before the attack fires, or dodge the resulting wave. The gilded variant was made less lethal but gained a gold-steal, so the punishment for a long fight is now economic: kill it quickly or pay for the delay.

Elites cost the spawn director six times a normal monster for the common types and thirty-six times for the healing-blocking and invisibility ones, which is why the latter two appear so much later — and why seeing them tells you your coefficient has crossed a real threshold.

Enemies worth specific habits

Stone golems and their boss version fire a tracking laser that is not reliably dodgeable — the boss version tracks perfectly, even through invisibility. Don't try to outrun it; break line of sight instead. Another large enemy's body will block it, terrain works, and even small cosmetic props near the teleporter are enough.

Prioritise ranged enemies over melee ones. Melee enemies telegraph, travel, and can be kited; ranged enemies deal damage the moment they have line of sight.

Check behind you periodically. Flying enemies accumulate unnoticed while you're facing a fight, and fifteen of them at your back is simply death.

When overwhelmed, climb rather than run. Most enemies don't spawn on high cliffs, so height changes the composition of what's chasing you rather than just the distance.

The retreat decision

Plan to flee. A moment will come in every long run where the correct play is to disengage and recover, and it arrives because you either overestimated your own power or underestimated the enemy's — both of which are easy to do when your damage numbers look enormous.

Incoming damage is hard to judge accurately, and negative status effects stack up faster than they feel like they should. The counter-intuitive part is that in a game about becoming a god, recognising and taking retreat windows is what preserves the run — and the whole run's invested time is what you're protecting.

The related discipline is fighting for loot rather than for kills. There's no reward for clearing a wave that doesn't stand between you and something you want. In practice this is the most common way experienced players still lose time: grinding endlessly-respawning enemies that yield nothing proportional to the difficulty their existence is costing.

Building the unkillable stack

If you want to deliberately assemble maximum survivability rather than take what's offered, the layers that genuinely compound are: heavy block chance, a single-hit negation item with reduced cooldown, sprint-based damage reduction, a shield pool, and shield-conditional mitigation on top of it. Those multiply because they operate on different mechanisms.

The remaining failure modes even at that point are healing suppression, void explosions, and being hit by several things in the same instant — none of which more of the same items will fix.

Recap

Stay near full health so one-shot protection stays live, and prefer mitigation to healing once enemies outscale your health bar. Buy single-hit negation if you can avoid repeated hits, and continuous reduction if you can't. Treat each elite type as a positioning problem rather than a bigger enemy, break line of sight against tracking attacks, and be willing to walk away from a fight that isn't standing between you and loot.

Bosses & Endgame

Teleporter bosses are a tax you pay every stage. The final bosses are different — they have specific exploitable mechanics, and knowing them turns fights that seem to demand an enormous build into fights that demand the right handful of items.

Teleporter events

The event itself is covered under stage economy, but two combat habits matter. Clear any boss that spawned naturally on the map before starting the event, so you aren't fighting two boss-tier enemies at once. And bank your gold beforehand, since everything locks down while the bubble is up.

Boss selection is largely random per stage with some bosses excluded from some maps, so you can't route around a specific matchup — but you can prepare for the general case. Boss loot drops the moment the boss dies, even if the charge hasn't completed, so there's no reason to hover protectively over the teleporter afterwards.

If killing bosses quickly is your bottleneck, look for overlaps: on the map with the additional secret boss, you can start the teleporter and fight both simultaneously, abandoning the optional one if it goes badly.

The final boss

Reaching him means aligning the last teleporter with the moon rather than the planet, and the approach is worth taking slowly. On the way to the arena there are lunar pods along one side and a cauldron cluster along the other — the last chance to convert items into what you actually need. Judge those trades before committing, because once the fight starts you have what you have.

The arena is ringed with ramps, and they matter more than they look: they let you skip shockwaves and flame lines outright, which is disproportionately valuable for low-mobility survivors.

Phase structure

Phase one is his basic moveset. He teleports to you and swings, which you dodge by moving back toward where he first appeared rather than away from him. His overhead smash has a brief wind-up, so stepping off the impact point is enough. At range he fires tracking needles, dodged by strafing. Once he's taken enough damage he starts jumping to the arena centre for a radial shockwave — jump over it, or stand on the ramps where it doesn't reach.

Phase two removes him entirely and replaces him with waves of lunar chimeras. This is a pure damage check on your build. Prioritise the flying ones — they're the ones whose attacks actually connect reliably — and use the pillars that rise during this phase as cover while baiting them into range.

Phase three returns him, more aggressive, with two additions. His smash now leaves a lingering flame column that damages you in the air as well as on the ground, so jumping clears the shockwave but not the pillar. His centre slam summons rotating lines of flame that sweep around him and explode, repeating four times with each rotation faster and in the opposite direction. Walking sideways to slip between the lines works, standing on the ramps works, and both are easier grounded than airborne.

Phase four is where the fight becomes strange. He steals every item you own and gains their effects; damaging him returns them gradually. Two things follow from this:

First, you can damage him during the steal animation, and that's the single best burst window in the fight. Have your equipment and cooldowns ready as phase three ends, and dump everything into the animation.

Second, after the steal, play defensively until most of your items are back. Surviving raises your eventual damage output more than aggression does while you're stripped. And pay attention to what he took — if he's holding your close-range punishment items, don't fight him at close range.

He also periodically kneels and fires orbs in eight directions while dealing a percentage of his own health to himself, which is free damage if you can survive the pattern.

Exploitable mechanics

Several properties make him substantially easier than his health bar suggests.

He is vulnerable to freeze executes. Survivors with a freezing skill can effectively delete a large fraction of his health without dealing the corresponding damage, which is why the freeze-oriented skill choices on several survivors are recommended specifically for this fight.

He takes fall damage, and at higher difficulty levels he moves fast enough to launch himself off the arena ramps chasing you. Baiting that is a real strategy, and it can be amplified by dragging him back down at speed with a pulling ability.

Crowbar-based one-shots work because each phase begins with him at full health. Stacking crowbars and armor-piercing rounds on a survivor with a single enormous burst deletes a phase outright — but the entire thing collapses if you chip him first, because any prior damage drops him below the crowbar threshold. Nothing but the big hit, from the very first frame.

The arena ceiling has an invisible barrier, and several survivors can reach it. Sitting up there dodges nearly everything he does, and no additional enemies spawn at that height — which converts the fight from a dodging problem into a damage-delivery problem. Flying survivors can do the same by hovering above his windmill attack, which doesn't reach the ceiling.

Staying constantly above his head in phase three also suppresses supporting enemy spawns, which is why the "stay in the top of the arena" approach works for high-mobility survivors even without item support.

He cannot steal certain resources — accumulated skill-based stacks aren't items, so survivors whose power lives in stacking buffs rather than in their inventory lose far less to phase four.

Escaping

His death starts a four-minute countdown to reach the shuttle, with flame columns appearing and void enemies spawning along the route. High-mobility survivors can stay above the dropship area entirely — just watch that you don't drift out of the radius, and plan the landing, since dying to fall damage after surviving the fight is an ignominious end.

Escaping unlocks a survivor, and doing it on the hardest initial difficulty completes an additional challenge and unlocks a lunar item.

The alternative endings

After killing him, returning to the starting teleporter and interacting with the frog repeatedly opens a portal to a second, harder final fight rather than escaping. That's a deliberate extension of the run, not a shortcut.

The obliteration ending is reached through the celestial portal and simply ends the run — unless you're carrying the specific lunar item that redirects you to a genuine boss fight instead. That distinction is worth knowing before you commit, since the portal is one-way either way.

The newer final boss

The DLC's boss sits at the end of a prerequisite chain that isn't signposted. You need to kill the gold realm's boss and hold onto its dropped seed — the seed must remain in your inventory through the fight, since it's the unlock condition rather than merely a key. Then find and activate the shrine that spawns on loop-start stages, activate it a second time when prompted, kill the mini-boss that appears, and take the green portal. Two stages later you arrive at the arena.

Entering the gold realm via the Bazaar is more reliable than hoping for a gold altar to spawn.

The fight starts with a climb to the summit. Gold pillars along the way grant barrier, so take them; meteors and regular enemies are hazards to avoid rather than fights to win. Survivors with strong vertical mobility can skip the climb entirely.

The fight itself is about movement. His large laser hits extremely hard and standing still is what kills you; the pillars scattered around the arena work as cover when movement alone isn't enough.

In the third phase the gold realm's boss joins as an additional enemy and your skills are locked. The answer is to find the golden pillar and break it, which restores your kit — then fight both together.

The wave mode

The endless wave mode is a different game with a different item logic, because you choose an item from three after every wave. That choice is the mode, and the standard priorities shift.

Mobility beats raw health more decisively here than anywhere else — enemies hit hard enough that added maximum health is close to irrelevant next to not being hit. A single double-jump item repeatedly saves runs.

Damage is disproportionately valuable because faster kills mean fewer enemy attack windows, and the waves scale relentlessly. Elite execution matters early because elites are the scaling threat.

Several items are traps here. Flat maximum-health items look attractive and aren't. Block-chance items are clutch but suffer badly from over-stacking when you could have taken damage instead. Chain items only pay off on rapid-fire survivors — on slow-attack survivors they simply don't trigger often enough to justify the pick, while crowbar-style single-hit multipliers do the opposite.

Sustain survivors dominate the early waves, and turret-based healing in particular can make a team close to unkillable given enough stacks — with the caveats that its practitioner is fragile against map hazards and that certain stages' area damage wipes turrets faster than the healing replaces them.

The far end of the curve

Deep looping — past roughly stage thirty — changes the game's behaviour in ways worth knowing if you intend to go there.

Displayed enemy level caps, but the underlying coefficient keeps climbing: prices keep inflating and certain bosses' health keeps growing on each visit. Combat shrines eventually stop being activatable at all.

The spawn table hollows out from the bottom. Normal enemies vanish, then bosses become the baseline, then elite bosses, and finally only the toughest single enemy type appears because nothing else meets the director's minimum cost. By the deep stages some maps have no spawns at all, teleporters sometimes have no boss to kill, and the correct play is to activate and leave.

Gold and health values eventually overflow into negative numbers, which is cosmetically alarming and practically irrelevant — late enemies drop so much that you can buy anything regardless.

The relevant strategic lesson is about build composition. Chain-explosion engines that clear entire maps from a single kill are enormously effective right up until the spawn rate drops, at which point they have nothing to chain from and you discover you have no primary-weapon damage at all. Anyone planning a deep run needs genuine direct damage — armor-piercing stacks in particular — alongside the chain engine, plus a very large block-chance stack, because at that depth the survivability items that rely on healing or one-shot protection stop functioning.

Recap

Clear natural bosses before starting teleporter events, and bank gold beforehand. Against the final boss, use the ramps, treat phase two as a damage check, and burst him during the item-steal animation. Freeze executes, fall damage and untouched-health multipliers are all legitimate ways to skip most of his health bar. For the wave mode, prioritise mobility and damage over health. And for deep loops, bring real weapon damage rather than relying on a chain reaction that runs out of fuel.

Eclipse

Eclipse stacks eight purely detrimental modifiers on top of the hardest normal difficulty, one added per victory. None of them are individually catastrophic. What makes the climb hard is that each one quietly invalidates a strategy you'd been leaning on, and by the top you're playing a game where healing barely works, gold is scarce, and every hit you take is permanent.

The eight modifiers

Starting health halved

Your current health is halved at the start of every stage. Alone this barely registers, but it compounds badly with the healing modifier — with no healing sources you may simply never reach full health for the entire run, which means never having one-shot protection.

Shields recover instantly on stage start, which makes shield generators and Transcendence quietly excellent here. It also devalues ally-based items, since recruited drones and allies spawn at half health too — and hits Engineer specifically, whose turrets arrive fragile, until the healing fungus makes it irrelevant.

Teleporter radius halved

The impact is far larger than the number suggests, because halving a radius cuts the volume by a factor of eight. It doesn't change what items you want; it changes how you run the event.

The adaptation is to kill the boss first, then charge. Most bosses spawn slightly outside a shrunken radius and simply won't come in, which strands melee survivors in particular. Highly mobile survivors feel claustrophobic — anything that flings you a long distance now flings you out of the charging zone. Stand-your-ground survivors care least.

The radius-shrinking lunar item becomes actively harmful here rather than a clean time saving.

Fall damage doubled and lethal

Falling off the stage under ordinary circumstances now kills you. The severity depends almost entirely on your survivor: anyone with fall immunity or a fall-breaking utility barely notices, while survivors with poor vertical control suffer badly.

The item answers are the landing-stomp legendary and Transcendence, both of which make it a non-issue, with a double-jump green regaining a lot of relevance. Several mobility equipment and one of the Heresy lunars also solve it. Partial-block items help against moderate falls but won't save you from a long one — don't treat them as a substitute.

Enemy speed increased

Mostly manageable, with the danger concentrated in mobile high-threat enemies that can now close distance faster than you can retreat. It raises the value of your own mobility and of raw damage, since killing something before it repositions is now more valuable than out-manoeuvring it.

It hurts survivors who lead their shots and survivors whose entire model is maintaining distance. Aggressive close-range survivors barely notice.

Unusually, this is the one modifier with a genuine upside: it's fast enough to let you bait the final boss into launching himself off the arena ramps for serious fall damage.

Healing halved

This is frequently the most dangerous of the eight, and it's the one people underestimate. It doesn't just reduce your recovery — it invalidates an entire build category. Healing-conversion items and the excess-healing-to-barrier legendary both drop dramatically in value, and any survivor whose survival model is regeneration is now playing a different game.

The correct response is to shift wholesale to shields and dodging, keep only your very best healing item and scrap the rest, and lean on healing drones as compensation. Rejuvenation is worth having specifically if you don't have Transcendence.

Survivors who kill threats quickly adapt easily. Survivors with drawn-out encounters suffer disproportionately.

Enemy gold reduced

This only affects direct enemy drops, and its effect is to slow everything down — which is functionally an enemy power buff, since your item accumulation falls behind the clock.

The adaptation is to shift income toward the sources it doesn't touch: combat shrines, blood shrines and barrels all gain real value, as do gold-generating items. Blood shrines specifically become viable again if you carry a damage-negation item that blocks the health cost.

Enemy cooldowns halved

Often rated the hardest of the eight, and hard to prepare for because enemy cooldowns aren't visible. Some enemies become dramatically more dangerous and others feel identical. Bosses are the notable case, since their highest-threat moves are exactly the ones on long cooldowns.

There's no item answer — the adaptation is behavioural. Better target prioritisation and constant strafing, because there are simply more simultaneous attacks in the air at any moment.

Permanent damage

Taking a hit that's a significant fraction of your maximum health permanently reduces that maximum, scaled by how large the hit was relative to your pool. Small chip damage does nothing; big hits cost you the rest of the stage.

Crucially, all accumulated permanent damage clears when you travel to a new stage, and also clears on any revive. That makes revive items far more valuable than usual — and one of the equipment-slot revives has the additional property of not being stealable during the final boss's item theft, making it the only way to survive dying early in that phase.

The counters are flat damage reduction, which you genuinely cannot have too much of, and anything that raises maximum health, since a bigger pool means each hit costs a smaller fraction. Barrier chips away at the permanent loss. One block item currently nullifies the permanent component of the hit it absorbs.

This modifier pigeonholes several survivors. Anyone whose kit costs health takes permanent damage from their own abilities and must switch to their non-self-damaging skills — which for at least one survivor means playing their worst loadout. Blood shrines become largely unusable, and void cradles need a block item to be safe.

Pacing under Eclipse

Two failure modes bracket the correct answer. Hyper-rushing — teleporter in thirty seconds, off stage 1 in under three minutes with two items — leaves you as weak as having no items at all, while the enemies levelled up anyway from the stage transition. You have not gained a position, you've skipped one. At the other extreme, forty minutes on stage 1 hunting every last container leaves you hopelessly behind the coefficient.

The metric to track is items per stage, at least for the first two. Seven minutes on stage 1 with eleven items is an excellent result. Four minutes with one item is a disaster regardless of how fast it was. Efficient item acquisition genuinely does outpace the clock — it has to, or nothing you did would matter and enemy scaling would always win.

There's a real argument for full-clearing on Eclipse specifically, beyond raw item count. A bigger pool means more printer fodder, better odds of finding a printer for something you can stack heavily, better odds of finding a pillar-skip equipment before the final stage, and better odds of having the mobility to handle the final boss when you get there. The strongest runs are the ones with the strongest synergies, and synergies emerge from volume.

Stage 5 is the exception to the money-then-items loop. By then a stage's worth of items is a much smaller relative power spike, so activate the teleporter immediately and buy afterwards. The real power spike on stage 5 is scrapping and printing, which you can do safely once the boss is dead.

Don't loop. It's too risky and it makes the climb take far longer for no benefit.

Item priority

The ordering that holds across the climb is mobility, then damage, then damage negation, then healing. More mobility reduces your need for both of the latter two and speeds up looting simultaneously. Having little or no mobility by stage 5 is close to a guaranteed death.

Healing is last because it's halved, and building around it will get you killed.

Transcendence deserves its own line: it single-handedly counters three of the eight modifiers and helps substantially with a fourth. It also mostly invalidates your healing items, which under this ruleset is a benefit — they become free scrap. Routing to the Bazaar at least once per run, if only for this, is a reasonable standing policy.

Beyond that, a few items carry Eclipse-specific weight. A single-hit block item lets you use blood shrines and open void cradles safely, which restores two economies the modifiers took away. Barrier-on-kill plus shields chips away at permanent damage. The damage-multiplier watch is less likely to break as your health pool shrinks, and the protective green preserves both watches and consumables. A double-jump green is worth taking over almost anything in a multishop, and you want at least one for the final boss.

Drones are worth buying — not all of them, but enough to keep aggro off you. Several survivors benefit heavily.

Skipping the pillar phase

This is worth taking seriously, because a large share of Eclipse losses happen on the final stage where the shrunken teleporter radius meets suicide-rush enemies. Skipping the pillars entirely removes that failure mode.

Three equipment do it consistently. The flying one is the least skilful and most forgiving, needs no knowledge, lets you float above the boss's sweeping attack, and trivialises the escape sequence — the escape zone is effectively an infinite vertical cylinder, so once the countdown starts you can simply fly. The explosive one is middling, actually deals damage on the way, generally needs a charge item to reach the top, and its real value is elsewhere. The tunnelling one is arguably the best: it skips the stage unconditionally, and with a charge item it also lets you zip across to check the cauldrons at almost no time cost.

Two of them can also clip you outside the boss arena, which is unintended behaviour and may not survive future patches. From outside, the boss has very few answers — though the flying adds pressed against the wall can still shoot past it, and his sweeping attack still reaches, so you need a way to get above the stage as well. Melee survivors can't exploit this without a ranged skill replacement.

Many survivors can skip the pillars with no equipment at all, using their own kit — which frees the equipment slot for a damage option instead. It's worth checking whether yours can, because the difference between spending your equipment on movement and spending it on damage is substantial.

A separate trick: some enemies damaged heavily on their very first frame can have entire phases skipped. The reliably consistent case is the final boss's item-stealing phase — landing a killing blow that triggers a high-damage band effect just as he arrives at the arena centre can stagger or outright kill him before the theft happens.

What to skip

The DLC's alternate route is a net negative for Eclipse purposes. The alternative main stages are a sidegrade — neither better nor worse — while the boss at the end is a serious additional variable, with a devastating laser combined with adds and a phase that seals your abilities. A run capable of clearing it would have had less trouble with the standard final boss, which most players have far more experience against.

The recommendation is to clear the shrine that starts the route — the mini-event's item choice is genuinely worth the ninety seconds — and then stay on the normal path. Doing that shrine before the teleporter is correct: it returns a lot of gold, and it lets you kill the teleporter boss afterwards and still take the portal if you change your mind.

Similarly, the alternate final boss beyond the moon isn't reachable in a way that matters here, since it requires beating the standard one first.

Habits worth building

Leave chests near the teleporter for last. Scrap after the teleporter event rather than during — being caught mid-scrap by something that snuck up on you is a surprisingly common death. Open void cradles after the event unless you have a block item to absorb the health cost.

Visit the Bazaar when convenient, even just to trade scrap or to skip a stage you dislike. But be careful about becoming dependent on lunars — runs where the ones you want don't appear will lose if your whole plan assumed them.

If you're carrying a slow effect, deliberately don't attack the final boss while he charges you on the higher levels — slowing him prevents him building enough speed to hurt himself on the ramps.

Which survivors climb easiest

Assessments differ, and the disagreements are informative rather than contradictory — they mostly reflect whether the rater leans on mobility or on damage.

The consistent top picks are survivors with mobility, range and some form of invulnerability built into the kit: the auto-aiming ranged survivor with three dashes, the double-primary bruiser with a huge health pool and two equipment slots, the beacon survivor whose shrine manipulation and drone stack solve his own weaknesses, and the flying mage who can freeze the final boss and skip pillars trivially. The grapple survivor is near-universally rated highly for the simple reason that fall damage doesn't exist for her and her damage scales with the movement she was doing anyway.

The consistent strugglers are survivors whose model is healing through damage — permanent damage and halved healing hit them from both directions simultaneously — and survivors who can't remove a high-threat target quickly, since every extra second of a fight is another set of halved-cooldown enemy attacks.

The one broadly-agreed hard case is the health-spending survivor, who is forced onto his weakest skills by the permanent-damage modifier and loses his defining loop to halved healing at the same time.

None of this is prescriptive. Every survivor can clear every level; the difference is how much luck each one needs.

Recap

Each modifier removes an option rather than adding a threat, so the climb is about noticing what you can no longer rely on. Track items per stage rather than minutes per stage. Buy mobility first and healing last. Get Transcendence if you can, secure a pillar skip before the final stage, and remember that every point of permanent damage clears the moment you change stages.

Artifacts

Artifacts are permanent unlocks that change the rules of a run. Some make it easier, some harder, and several do both — the interesting ones are the ones that change what skills the run actually tests rather than simply turning a dial.

Unlocking them

All artifacts are unlocked the same way. On the fifth stage, beneath the main map, sits a compound generator with a grid of projectors and an input terminal. Entering a specific three-by-three pattern opens a portal to a challenge realm; clearing that realm grants the artifact.

The patterns themselves are scattered across the game as environmental details — one on each of several stages, one in the hidden realms, and, memorably, several that aren't in the game at all: one on the settings screen and two in promotional material outside it. You are not going to find those by exploring, so looking them up is the intended path for at least some of them.

The generator island is otherwise quiet — monsters don't spawn there, though they will follow you down from above.

The three that change everything

Command lets you choose every item drop. It's the most transformative artifact in the game and the most contested.

The case against it is straightforward and worth taking seriously: this game's difficulty is its randomness. Some runs are doomed from the start and others are handed to you on stage 1, and Command removes both. Once you can pick, an ideal item list exists, and following it every run turns decisions into repetition. Genuinely difficult runs are ones where you had to compensate for a bad draw with play skill — which is exactly the skill that stops developing if you never face a bad draw.

There's a related argument about what item variance is actually for. An early legendary makes a run easier relative to other runs, but you still have to handle map awareness, positioning, movement and aim to finish it — chance doesn't make the game easy, it makes one run less hard. Meanwhile, the accidental synergies you stumble into are a large part of what makes any given run memorable, and Command deletes those by construction.

The case for it is equally real. It makes unlock runs dramatically easier, since you can cater your build to whatever challenge you're attempting. It makes short sessions productive when you can't afford a doomed run. And on a difficulty where individual mistakes are punished heavily, it's a legitimate way to express what a survivor is actually capable of rather than what the item pool allowed.

The honest resolution: reasons for Command are situational rather than universal. If you want a specific unlock, use it. If you want to get better at the game, most of your runs shouldn't have it.

What it definitely enables is degenerate engines. The clearest is a scrap item that returns every stage, used to pay the Bazaar's legendary vendor with items you never actually lose — enough stacks turns each Bazaar visit into several free legendaries. The other is the auto-kill loop: an enormous stack of the instant-kill crit corruption, backed by rerolls, with an equipment that triggers your on-kill items continuously, on-kill items that generate real kills, and kill-based cooldown reduction to close the loop. Once assembled it plays itself.

Sacrifice removes chests entirely and makes enemies drop items on death. Whether that's a gain or a loss comes down entirely to drop rates, and those vary enormously by enemy.

The rates scale with enemy strength: the weakest trash sits under ten percent, mid-tier enemies land in the teens, the tougher regular enemies reach the twenties and thirties, and the heaviest enemies approach or exceed a third to a half. Elite status raises the chance substantially — roughly ten points per elite tier, so a two-tier elite of a mid-strength enemy drops nearly as often as a plain boss.

Teleporter-spawned bosses drop noticeably less than naturally spawned enemies of the same type, which is a real consideration if you were counting on the event to pay you.

The strategic value is that killing for items doesn't advance the difficulty coefficient the way completing stages does. That makes long farming sessions on an early stage genuinely viable — you're buying items with the cheap resource instead of the expensive one. It's also why it pairs so naturally with Command in large groups, where the goal is to accumulate power before the stage transitions start compounding.

Glass multiplies your damage enormously while cutting your health to a fraction. It is the purest expression of the game's core tension and the fastest way to learn positioning, because nothing survives a mistake. It's also the standard tool for unlocks that require killing something extremely fast.

The rest

Honor makes every enemy an elite. That sounds like pure difficulty, and it is — but it's also the most reliable way to farm elite-specific drops, and it forces you to learn the elite counterplay properly rather than avoiding it.

Swarms doubles spawns and halves enemy health. It's a straightforward buff to any build that kills through chain reactions or area damage, since more bodies at lower health means more kills to chain from — and a straightforward problem for single-target survivors. It's excellent as a farming artifact.

Kin makes every enemy on a stage the same type. This is enormously useful for targeted farming and for challenges that require a specific enemy, and it's a genuine difficulty modifier in both directions — a stage of one harmless enemy is trivial, a stage of one dangerous enemy is not.

Evolution gives monsters items between stages, which compounds over a long run and makes the late game qualitatively different rather than just bigger.

Vengeance spawns a copy of you carrying your items on a timer. Its interest is that it scales exactly with your own power — a strong build fights a strong opponent — which makes it the natural counterweight to an otherwise-unopposed god build, and a real threat to a fragile one. Killing them drops items.

Enigma gives you a random equipment that rerolls every time you fire it. It removes equipment planning entirely and replaces it with improvisation, which is either its whole appeal or its whole problem depending on your taste.

Metamorphosis assigns you a random survivor. Its practical use is forcing you to learn the parts of the roster you'd otherwise never pick.

Dissonance lets monsters appear outside their normal environments, which mostly means seeing dangerous late-stage enemies early. It's a difficulty increase disguised as variety.

Soul makes defeated monsters leave wisps behind, adding a persistent extra damage source to every kill — which compounds nicely with chain-explosion builds.

Spite makes enemies drop explosives on death, which punishes exactly the melee-range play that killing things quickly requires.

Frailty doubles fall damage and makes it lethal — the same modifier the Eclipse ladder applies, so it's a reasonable way to practise for that specific problem.

Chaos enables friendly fire in both directions. Death ends the run when any player dies. Both are group artifacts with little solo meaning.

Delusion reactivates chests after the teleporter event with a catch attached, and Devotion replaces purchasable drones with an egg-based ally system that grows over the run — both change the economy rather than the combat.

Choosing a set

The useful mental model is that artifacts fall into three groups. Some make runs easier — item selection, enemy drops, doubled spawns with halved health. Some make them harder — universal elites, monster items, fall damage, doppelgangers. And some simply make them different — random survivors, random equipment, single-enemy stages.

If your goal is a specific unlock, stack the first group and get it done. If your goal is improvement, the third group teaches more than the second, because it forces adaptation rather than just demanding more of the same skills. And if your goal is farming, the combination of item drops from kills plus doubled spawns plus item selection turns an early stage into an item factory that costs you almost nothing on the difficulty clock.

Recap

Artifacts unlock through pattern codes on the fifth stage, several of which are found outside the game entirely. Item selection is transformative and worth using deliberately rather than habitually. Enemy item drops are best understood through their scaling with enemy strength and elite tier, and their real value is that killing doesn't advance the clock the way travelling does. Everything else is a lever for either practising something specific or farming something specific.

Tech & Tricks

A collection of things the game never mentions: movement exploits that skip whole phases, build patterns that trivialise the run, and small habits that quietly save minutes every stage.

Skipping the pillar phase

The final stage normally requires charging four pillars before the boss becomes accessible. Skipping straight to the arena removes several minutes of exposure and, on hard difficulties, removes the situation that ends the largest share of runs.

Three equipment do it regardless of survivor. The tunnelling one is the best — fastest, safest, and usable the moment you leave the starting area by placing a tunnel directly to the arena. In a group it's the only one that lets everybody skip. The flying one is the best fallback, and its speed scales with your movement speed, which makes it faster than it first appears; it needs no cooldown-reduction items at all, since you can wait on top of the tree while cooldowns refresh. The explosive one is the weakest and generally needs a charge item, though survivors with strong built-in mobility can manage without.

Beyond those, a broad set of items enables skips by giving you height or airtime. Cooldown reduction and extra-charge items are the biggest lever — several survivors can skip outright once their utility cooldown is short enough to spam, or at least reach a sloped resting spot on the tree to recover. Extra jumps let you stall in the air. Raw movement speed helps every method.

One survivor-agnostic technique worth knowing is trimping: jumping on a slope with the landing-stomp legendary plus a couple of slope-launch items produces a great deal of height for anyone.

Roughly half the roster can skip with no items at all, using their kit alone. That's worth checking for your survivor, because it converts your equipment slot from a movement tool into a damage tool for the boss fight — a significant upgrade.

Two caveats. In a group, everyone has to reach the arena for the fight to begin, so only skip if the whole team can. And on difficulties with lethal fall damage, several of the skip routes will kill you without fall protection — bring a block item or fall immunity.

Fighting from outside the arena

Two of the skip equipment can place you outside the boss arena entirely, which is almost certainly unintended and may not survive patching. The explosive one detonated at specific spots along the arches drops you out of the stage. The tunnelling one is more controlled: after reaching the arena, lay a tunnel running from inside to outside without crossing the wall, then rush in, trigger the fight, and run the tunnel backwards.

From outside, the boss has very few answers. The exceptions are the flying adds, which can shoot past the wall when pressed right against it, and his sweeping attack, which still reaches — so you need a way to get above the stage as well. Melee survivors can't use any of this without a ranged skill replacement.

Several survivors reach the arena's invisible ceiling instead, which achieves the same thing more elegantly: nothing spawns at that height and almost nothing reaches you.

Movement tech worth learning

Animation cancelling is the most broadly applicable. Most attack animations are longer than the actual damage frames — once you hear or see the hit connect, the attack is finished and you can act. Chaining an ability immediately off the end of an attack string is free damage on almost every survivor with a melee or charge-based kit.

Air stalling — using an ability mid-air purely to slow your descent — extends your time out of reach and buys cooldowns. Several survivors have a skill that incidentally does this: hovering, glaive throws, downward-firing recoil, and grapple attacks all work.

Slope launching is how several survivors gain height without spending mobility skills. Any slope-launch item plus a jump produces vertical distance, and one survivor can use it twice per jump thanks to a double jump.

Charging while moving applies broadly: many charged abilities let you sprint after the charge has begun, so you can dodge while building damage rather than standing still for it. On the momentum-scaling survivor this is not merely safe but a damage increase.

Recoil movement works for anyone with a downward-firing high-rate attack — enough attack speed converts firing at the ground into flight.

Build patterns that break the game

These need item selection to assemble reliably, and they're worth understanding as illustrations of how the multiplicative layers actually compound.

The one-shot build caps crit, then stacks everything that multiplies a critical hit: crit damage items, boss damage, full-health damage, and proximity damage if the survivor is close-range. Capped crit is the foundation because every downstream multiplier applies to every hit rather than sometimes. Paired with a crit-heal green and a barrier-from-overhealing legendary, the same crit cap that produces the damage also produces continuous lifesteal and permanent barrier — so the build is simultaneously a one-shot machine and effectively unkillable.

The linchpin is auto-firing your equipment: a healing equipment that fires continuously on a short cooldown keeps the overheal-to-barrier conversion permanently saturated. That's what turns "lots of healing" into "cannot be damaged".

The chain-explosion engine stacks kill-explosion items, full-health damage multipliers to make those explosions lethal, elite executes, and on-kill projectiles. One kill wipes the map, each corpse produces more projectiles, and those kill the next spawns. Levels beat themselves. Its failure mode is specific and worth knowing: it stops working when the spawn rate drops, because there's nothing to chain from — which is exactly what happens deep into a loop.

The equipment loop uses an equipment that triggers your on-kill items, on-kill items that generate real kills, and kill-based equipment cooldown reduction. Each component feeds the next, and enough charge items make it continuous.

The common structure across all three is that each item makes another item better rather than adding to a total. That's the thing to look for when assembling a build organically — not the strongest individual items, but the pair where one produces the events the other consumes.

Small habits

Set corpse cleanup to immediate and maximum corpses to zero. Busy fights become dramatically more readable when the battlefield isn't littered with bodies, and there's a substantial performance gain in exactly the moments that need it.

Leave chests near the teleporter for during and after the event, since the event's income covers them. The small experience loss doesn't matter — your level falls behind the ambient level eventually regardless.

Scrap after the teleporter event rather than during. Standing at a scrapper interacting with menus while enemies approach is a surprisingly common way to die.

Learn to spot the teleporter from a distance — it emits particles visible well beyond the model itself, which is much easier than looking for the shape.

On stages with a repairable turret or optional secret boss, decide immediately rather than deliberating. The deliberation costs more than either decision does.

If items are poor and you can't hit your stage-1 pace, restarting is a legitimate and efficient choice. Run quality is largely decided in the first loop, and four minutes is cheap.

Recap

Learn whether your survivor can skip the pillars unaided — it frees your equipment slot for the fight that matters. Practise animation cancelling and air stalling, since both apply everywhere. Build toward items that feed each other rather than items that are individually strong. And fix the small things: corpse cleanup, chest sequencing, and knowing when a run isn't worth continuing.
