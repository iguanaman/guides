Walk of Life

How the game actually works — the systems it never stops to explain, written to sit open beside you while you play.

Turn & Time

Walk of Life is a board game wearing a life sim's clothes. Every round you get a budget of time, and absolutely everything — working, studying, shopping, and crucially walking between buildings — is paid for out of that budget. Understanding the time economy is understanding the game.

Dating this guide

The game changed heavily across Early Access (Oct 2025 – Apr 2026) and has kept changing since 1.0. Claims are tagged current where they reflect the v1.3 build, older build where a system has since been reworked and the detail may be stale, and player-reported where the source is community testing rather than a developer statement.

The shape of a round

A round is one in-game day (the game also calls them turns or weeks depending on the screen). It resolves in a fixed order:

  1. Report cards — the consequences of last round land: rent notices, pet penalties, pension checks, bust checks, whatever you earned.
  2. Choice event — a life event fires, offering up to four options. You pick one for a reward or a punishment.
  3. Your turn — spend the time budget however you like.
  4. End of round — your character eats (if food is available), attributes and pet attributes are tallied for score, and stress punishment applies if it wasn't cancelled.

Your turn ends when the clock runs out, or when you end it deliberately. Ending early wastes the remainder — there is no carry-over — so the skill of the game is spending the last scraps of a turn on something rather than nothing.

Time is the real currency

Each action advances your personal timer by a different amount. A shower is nearly free; yoga costs roughly a twelfth of your whole day; a full relax-and-sleep sequence at home costs about two time blocks. Movement is charged the same way — the map's geography is a tax, and route planning is where most of the skill lives.

This is why transport, teleports and the Chailand Metro matter so much: they aren't convenience features, they're direct purchases of extra actions per round. It's also why the two maps play very differently. Calicornia is compact and forgiving; Chailand is deliberately larger, splitting the apartment tiers and the mall into separate buildings scattered across town, and the Metro exists specifically to make that size survivable.

Travel is committed

Once you click a destination the path locks in. You cannot cancel or redirect mid-walk, so a misclick across the map costs you real time. Confirm before you commit. player-reported

Eating: the upkeep nobody reminds you about

You must eat once per round. Your character eats automatically at the end of the turn from whatever food is available — home fridge stock, or a meal bought during the day. Miss it and you take a penalty that shrinks your next turn's time budget, which compounds: a hungry round is a shorter round is a worse round.

The rules around what counts as food are the single biggest source of confusion in the game:

  • Only the chicken-leg icon counts. At Clucker's, shakes, sodas and protein drinks feed your attributes but not your hunger. If the menu item doesn't carry the food icon, you haven't eaten.
  • One "first meal" per round. Free food from a random event, an elevator gag, or a role bonus consumes the day's meal — after which a purchased meal no longer registers as eating. This quietly breaks achievement runs and secret missions that count meals.
  • Groceries need a fridge. Food bought at Moo Moo Mart spoils by next round unless you own a fridge. Your home screen shows the food and pet-food counters; one food is consumed per day automatically.
  • Some food skips the fridge entirely. Clucker's meals, academy coffee, arcade pizza and the Chailand Metroburger are eaten on the spot with no storage requirement.
There is no "have you eaten?" indicator, and there never will be

The developers have refused this outright: forgetting to eat is treated as a core part of the game, and an indicator would reduce play to following a checklist. Track it yourself. current

Nothing can actually kill you

There is no fail condition. You can starve for an entire game, let every attribute bottom out, and still reach the final round and be scored — there's even an achievement for starving a whole run. Difficulty in Walk of Life is expressed as score pressure, never as elimination. That reframes solo play: you aren't surviving, you're optimising against your own previous best.

Simultaneous turns

Sequential play is the default and a six-player game at full length runs for hours. Simultaneous turns compresses that: report cards and choice events still resolve player-by-player in turn order, then everyone acts at the same time, and a round barrier holds the fastest players until the lobby finishes. A six-player ten-round game runs in under twenty minutes.

The mode changes tactics as well as pacing. Contested resources become live races rather than turn-order questions — sprinting to the pawn shop for furniture stolen in a crime wave, or beating a rival to the park to lock the building they're walking towards. A score log tracks the lobby in real time and each pawn shows its owner's remaining turn time.

Pausing, saving and ending early

  • Vote to End Game lets a lobby finish the run early; scoring resolves at that point.
  • Bathroom Break is a pause button for online games.
  • Saves support multiple slots, manual saving every round, local and online games, and Steam Cloud. Loading a multiplayer save opens a join screen and lets you force-start with missing players removed.
  • The continue button hides behind "start a new game" — an unintuitive placement that convinced many players the game had no save system at all.

In short: a round is a time budget, movement spends it as freely as actions do, and eating is a mandatory tax you have to remember without any prompt. Nothing can kill you, so the entire game is a race to convert time into score before the round counter runs out — which makes route planning, transport and the 20/30-round tuned lengths the frame everything else sits inside.

Attributes

Four bars govern your character: Health, Happiness, Hygiene and Stress. Three of them you keep up; the fourth you keep down. They are not a side system — they are tallied for score at the end of every round, and letting them slide costs you points continuously rather than all at once.

How attributes score

At the end of each round the game reads your three attribute bars and your pet's bars, and awards score based on where they sit. The bars carry visible slits marking scoring thresholds: finishing a round just below a slit wastes it, finishing just above banks the higher tier. Because this happens every round rather than once at the end, a character held at a comfortable level all game outscores one that spikes to full on the last day.

That per-round tally is also why the classic life-sim exploit doesn't work here. In the developers' previous game you could max health and happiness early then ignore them, or bottom them out and pump them at the end — the design was deliberately changed so that maintenance, not timing, is what pays.

Practical consequence

End your round with attributes above a threshold, not merely alive. A cheap top-up action in the last scraps of a turn is often worth more than a bigger action you don't have time to finish.

Health

The most painful attribute to lose and the slowest to rebuild. It drains from working, from eating (most food costs a little health), from tier 3 education, and from the additional academy courses. Life events that offer health as the sacrifice should generally be refused once you're past the early game.

The efficient restore is the gym's Let It Burn at $175, which returns roughly half the bar in one action — expensive in cash but cheap in time, which is the trade that matters. Work Out at $50 returns very little and is mostly there for quests and secret missions. Long-term, health furniture (a desk chair, a fancy TV) and the home relax action are what actually keep the bar full. Buy health furniture before other attribute furniture.

Happiness

Resilient early and brutal late. It barely moves in the opening rounds, then drains fast once you're working a tier 4–6 job, and education drains it too. The cheapest large restore is feeding the crows in the park at $50; a Clucker's shake at $75 is close behind and costs almost no time. The academy staff coffee perk gives happiness plus a free meal for no money at all, which is why the academy job is so quietly strong.

Furniture covers happiness well in the late game — an overheating computer and a home library together largely solve it, which is convenient because you buy both for education anyway.

Hygiene

Drains fastest of the three early on, then slows down as the game goes; working at the academy or the bank barely touches it. It's also the cheapest to restore, which makes it the attribute you sacrifice when a life event forces a choice.

The gym shower at $30 is once per round and the workhorse of the early game — shower whenever hygiene drops below roughly half, and always top up before a long working stretch. Washing hands in any bathroom is a near-free filler action. The bank's Wash Hand job perk is strong enough that hygiene stops being a consideration at all once you work there.

Hygiene can lock you out of your own turn

You cannot sleep at zero hygiene — you're too dirty. During a heat wave this becomes a genuine trap: relaxing raises hygiene but is itself blocked at zero, which can strand a turn with no legal way to end it well. Keep a buffer.

Stress

Stress works backwards from the others: it climbs, and only matters once it crosses a line. At stress level 4 and above you take a punishment to happiness, health and time, and the punishment scales with how high the level has gone.

The critical rule the game never states plainly: any action or condition showing the green stress-reduction icon cancels that round's punishment entirely, no matter how absurd your stress level is. You do not need to bring stress down — you need to touch a stress-reducing action once per round. That single fact separates competent play from struggling play.

Attributes as a resource to spend

Because every attribute contributes score at each round's end, the park's exchange actions are worth understanding: bathing in the pond trades a little health for a larger chunk of hygiene, pushing you over more scoring thresholds than you lose. Pushing the big rock trades hygiene for health. With the Giant Stone Head from the pawn shop, all park activities are boosted by 200% and the park turns into a genuine attribute engine rather than a curiosity.

An attribute at zero interrupts action combos

Roles that build on consecutive identical actions — the Workaholic's three-in-a-row especially — have the sequence broken if an attribute bottoms out mid-combo. Top up before starting a long working stretch, not during it.

In short: health is precious and rebuilt with money, happiness collapses late and is rebuilt in the park, hygiene is cheap and is what you sacrifice, and stress only needs one green icon per round to be neutralised. Score is read from all four every round, so the target is a comfortable steady state above the threshold slits, not a dramatic finish.

Work & Education

These are one system pretending to be two. Education exists mainly to unlock jobs; jobs exist mainly to fund education and furniture. The gate between them — which degree opens which tier — is the spine of a standard run, and it's the thing the game explains least.

Work level versus job tier

Two separate numbers are easy to confuse:

  • Work level is your career experience, raised by working. It rises through levels 1 to 8; there is no level 9, and at level 8 you can no longer work for further progress. Level 8 is near-impossible to reach in a normal game and doesn't award enough score to chase.
  • Job tier is which job the office will give you, tiers 1 to 6. Tier is gated by your work level, your education level, and sometimes your clothing tier.

Work level 7 is the meaningful breakpoint for score. A well-played full-career role reaches it around round 22 or later; roles with a work penalty may not get there before round 28. In competitive games it often isn't worth chasing at all — once you're at work level 6, secret missions and the arcade convert your remaining rounds into more score than another promotion would.

Absence gets you fired, not performance

Go four consecutive rounds without working and you lose your job. It's the passage of rounds that fires you, so a stretch of study or questing has a hidden clock on it. Dead-end jobs still grant 1 XP, so a token shift resets the counter.

What working costs

Every work action spends time and attributes, and both scale with the tier. The higher-paying jobs are not simply better — tier 4 VexCorp starts generating stress on top of everything else (roughly ten work clicks equals one stress level at that tier), so taking it before you can absorb the stress is a trap. This is the central tension of the career track: the jobs that pay for your furniture are the same jobs that destroy the attributes the furniture exists to restore.

Job perks are what actually differentiate workplaces. Every workplace has one, and a good perk can be worth more than the salary difference — the academy's free food and the bank's hand wash both remove an entire upkeep category from your round.

The education ladder

Nine education levels exist, and nine degrees are obtainable in a single long game. Studying costs time and drains happiness; tier 3 study also costs health. Two gates sit on the ladder that have nothing to do with study:

  • Education tier 2 requires a computer at home.
  • Education tier 3 requires a computer and a home library.

That makes the $300 overheating computer and the $1000 home library mandatory purchases rather than optional furniture, and it's why they're the only two pieces worth buying before your career is established. Schools are split into separate chambers, each holding a tier and its diploma courses; empty chambers now say so rather than leaving you guessing.

Money

Money scores almost nothing directly. It's a conversion resource: cash becomes furniture, education, clothing and pets, and those become score. Cash sitting in your pocket at the end of the game is a wasted round somewhere earlier.

Rent

Rent falls due every four rounds, at roughly $150 / $250 / $400 depending on your apartment. It escalates by $50 each time it comes due, on every difficulty, which eventually makes the middle apartment cost more than the fancy one did at the start.

Missing it is severe and stacks: you forfeit the rent score category, and the landlady pawns your furniture the following round to collect. Persistent non-payment gets you evicted to the park, where every home-only action disappears. You must also clear any owed rent before you're allowed to swap apartments.

Keep the rent in your pocket, not the bank

Once you're banking seriously, withdraw and hold the rent amount before the due round — more on hard difficulty. Deposits and withdrawals cost no action, but the bank being closed by a Tourist Invasion on the wrong round costs you the whole category.

The bank

  • Base interest is 4% per round on your balance. Deposits and withdrawals are free actions, so there's no reason to carry idle cash once you're at the bank regularly anyway.
  • Interest sources stack. The Bean Counter trait adds 4%, and a pet trait adds up to 3% more.
  • System Failure at the Bank is a global event that lifts interest to 40% for a single round — 44% with Bean Counter. It is the single largest money swing available.
  • Banking protects cash from crime waves but does not protect furniture or inventory items, which is why security matters more than banking discipline.
Two bank quirks worth knowing

Taking the double-interest perk during the bank-failure event has been reported to set interest to 8% rather than 80%, losing you the event's 40% baseline. And the quest that asks you to lend the banker money never pays it back — treat that cash as spent. player-reported

The pawn shop

The pawn shop both sells you goods and buys yours. Its stock rotates, so what's available differs between games and visits, and a further random item was added in v1.3.

Selling pays roughly 30% of value — cut down from around 60%, which killed the old buy-cheap-and-flip loop. That loop now loses money against simply playing normally, so pawn to raise emergency cash or to clear an unwanted diploma, not as an income engine.

Slot economics matter for score. You can hold two $200 items, two $400 items, and two from the $600/$800 band. In the top band always take the $800 item — 240 score against 180 for the $600. The Banjo passively adds one mall-furniture slot and one pawn-item slot, taking you from one and two to two and three, which both saves money and raises your odds of seeing every item in a run.

The shop also carries a cheap mall-electronics slot. These are drastically underpriced and should never be skipped — the Pal1000 security system at $120 sells for $700 at the mall, which is close to free money and full security in one purchase.

The stock market

Stocks trade at the bank. Unlike the developers' previous game you must sell manually — holdings persist until you act, so exit timing is entirely your problem. Stock profits now contribute score as well as cash.

The mechanism that matters: the game rewards roughly 10% per round a stock is held, paid out as accumulated interest when the sell price finally exceeds what you paid. A position that's temporarily down is therefore not a loss — holding through it is the intended play. Being up is the moment to think about gambling for more.

Stocks are stealable

A stock position occupies an inventory slot and can be taken during a crime wave or raccoon raid if your security bar isn't full.

The lottery

Tickets are $35 at Moo Moo Mart, one purchase available per round, and pay $950–1100 on a roughly 3% chance. Buying several tickets rolls 3% independently for each, but multiple hits still count as a single win — you cannot multiply the prize. If two players win in the same draw the prize splits. The jackpot was raised and the odds tightened in the same patch, so it's a bigger, rarer payout than older guides describe.

It's not a serious income line unless you're stacking the modifiers: the Lucky Cat Figurine adds 3%, a well-cared-for cat adds up to 4.5%, and the Mystic's Virgo round raises it sharply. The other reason to buy is the secret mission that scores whoever bought the most tickets — a cheap category to dominate if it's in play.

Other income lines

  • Quests pay cash and score; rewards were increased substantially in v1.3 so that taking one is a reasonable use of a round for any role, not just the Adventurer. Hard quests run around 135 score and $270.
  • Fishing sells at the pawn shop and contributes score. Specific fish are also required to qualify for the fishing courses.
  • The Magnet Rod dredges money bags and junk instead of fish — but money bags score nothing directly, only converting into furniture and attribute score after further setup time. player-reported
  • Pension checks arrive every two rounds for the Senior, unconditionally.
  • Artwork is the Artist's entire income, pawned at the shop, with value scaling on experience.
  • Artifacts are the Scavenger's, likewise pawned.
  • Job perks that hand you items — the mall's lost and found, Moo Moo Mart's free lottery ticket — are small but free money.

In short: pay rent on time because the penalty is compound, bank everything you aren't about to spend because interest stacks, treat the pawn shop as a source of function and cheap electronics rather than a flipping engine, and hold stocks through dips but never through a crash. Cash is only ever a step on the way to furniture, degrees and pets.

Home

Your apartment is the single biggest score category in the game and simultaneously your most vulnerable asset. Furniture scores, furniture restores attributes, and furniture attracts burglars — the three pull against each other constantly.

Apartment tiers

Housing runs from a crappy basement up through a studio or adequate house to the fancy apartment. Movement is bidirectional — you can downgrade back down mid-game, though you must clear any owed rent before swapping either way.

Tier does more than change rent. The Relax at Home action scales with your apartment, so a fancy apartment gives a much larger multi-attribute restore for the same time spent. It also sets your baseline security. On Chailand the first two tiers occupy their own separate map locations with their own landladies, so climbing the housing ladder physically relocates you across town — moving up changes your commute as well as your rent.

The standard timing is to move to the fancy apartment around round 8 and pay rent from there. On hard difficulty it's often better to move to the studio at round 8 for flexibility and take the fancy apartment around round 12, once income can absorb the higher rent escalation.

Furniture and score

Furniture is where most games are won or lost. Each item's score is visible before you buy — the info box at the side of the furniture store shows a star rating, and more stars means more score. Players consistently find furniture to be the largest single differentiator on the end screen.

The buying discipline that matters: buy almost nothing before your career is established. The exceptions are the pieces the education ladder demands and the piece that protects everything else — the overheating computer at $300, the burglar trap at $300, and the home library at $1000. Everything else delays your degrees, and degrees gate the jobs that pay for the furniture.

Once you're in a high-tier job, buy cheap attribute furniture first: it's the most cost-efficient and each piece refills a chunk of a bar. Since the computer and library already cover happiness, prioritise health pieces — a desk chair gives a large health return, and a fancy TV gives a large amount of everything. Duplicate furniture arriving as a quest reward is auto-pawned rather than wasted.

Security and crime

Crime is not a random event you might dodge — the crime wave left the global event pool and became a guaranteed core system present in every game, with its frequency raised at the same time. Security is the attribute you manage against it, shown as a blue meter in your apartment.

During a crime wave your money, furniture and inventory items — including stock positions — each carry a percentage chance of being stolen unless the security bar is full. The counterintuitive part: buying gaudy, expensive furniture lowers your security, because it attracts burglars. Every non-security purchase eats into the bar, so security is a running cost of getting rich, not a one-time upgrade. The purchase UI shows whether an item raises or lowers security before you buy.

Pets

Pets were reworked substantially and most older advice about them is wrong. The current rules:

  • Score comes from end-of-game state only, not per round. Every pet starts neglected; care raises it to content, then pampered. You can buy a pet in the final rounds and still max the pet score category.
  • The three states are thresholds across all bars. Neglected is 0–50%, content above 50%, pampered above 75% — and every bar must clear the threshold. One neglected bar forfeits the whole tier, which is why skipping baths quietly costs you the category.
  • Traits are the reason to buy early. Each pet grants a passive trait that scales with care level, strongest at pampered. An early pet is an engine; a late pet is pure points.
  • The adoption round is mandatory upkeep. Feed, bathe and play on the round you buy, or penalties start immediately.

Care is cheap in money and, since v1.3, half as expensive in time — playing and bathing had their time cost halved. Pet food doesn't need refrigeration; one press of basic or ultra food covers four rounds, one press of soap gives three baths. Premium food is only marginally better than basic and generally isn't worth the premium. The efficient rhythm is to push each bar comfortably above the second line, then let it drift down to that line before topping up rather than interacting every round.

Pet stock rotates each turn. A cat and a dog are always available; the third slot is random, so if you want a specific trait you re-roll by revisiting the store. Well-cared-for pets also hand you positive cards at the start of a round — attributes, money or stress reduction.

Roles

Roles exist to stop the game being solved. Each one reroutes some part of the standard education-job-furniture climb, and a few abandon it entirely. Tap any role for its full mechanics.

Balance is a moving target

The developers have run repeated tuning passes and say more are coming. As of the current build the community reads Artist and Workaholic as the top of the spread and Hacker as the bottom, with the Artist confirmed by a developer as due for a nerf. Take role power rankings as a snapshot, not a rule. player-reported

How roles score

Each role carries its own role quests — a private scoring track alongside the shared one. These were standardised to five per role (the Adventurer excepted), and their score now ramps, so higher-tier role quests are worth substantially more than low ones. Farming the easy end of your role's chain is worse than pushing deeper into it.

Role-related score is shown in its own category on the end screen, and hovering any score there breaks it down per category.

Roles that were cut

Two designs reached development and were dropped, and knowing why explains some odd corners of the current game:

  • The Angler reached internal testing before Early Access. It was cut because balancing fishing to stay viable for one specialised role made it worthless for everyone else, and because the loop touched too few locations. Its features were broken out and given to all roles instead — the master rod, the magnet rod, gated fishing education and fishing score all exist because the Angler didn't.
  • The Trader would have learned one random stock's direction each round plus a government bailout recouping losses, letting it bait rivals into a stock about to crash. Cut partly for being one-dimensional and partly because secret information works online but breaks in local play — the same limitation that already constrains secret missions.

Both may return eventually. A community vote decides which new roles ship first; that's how the Hacker beat the Artist to release.

In short: the vanilla roles reward knowing the standard route cold, the disruptive roles replace part of it with a private economy, and every role has its own five-quest scoring chain that pays more the deeper you push. Pick a role whose economy you can actually run, because most of the weak results come from playing a specialist role as though it were a Workaholic.

Heat and the bust

Every hack raises heat. Getting busted costs you all accumulated hacking bonuses, not just recent ones — so the more you've stacked, the more a bust destroys. The rule as clarified by the developers:

How the bust check actually works

At the end of your round a small red arrow resolves against your heat bar. If the arrow lands on any filled amount of heat, you're busted; if it misses, you're safe. It is not a threshold you stay under — low heat can still bust you, which is why players kept getting caught at heat levels that looked harmless. The widget was reworked to make this legible. v1.3

Heat decays by laying low: a full round without hacking lowers it, and enough clean rounds drive it to zero. There is no active heat sink — nothing you can buy or do to force it down, which is the role's most-criticised feature. Players have asked for purchasable options such as a VPN or a house move; none exist.

Getting busted now plays a siren the entire lobby hears, and the chance of being caught hacking the Job Office specifically was reduced. v1.3

Community consensus places the Hacker at the weak end of the roster — the payoff is real but the risk isn't controllable enough to plan around. player-reported

The first role that cannot take a job at all. The job office is simply closed to it, so the entire wage economy is off the table and the role survives by making and selling art.

Inspiration

Entering any location pops a widget — the Artist's equivalent of the work button. Each location holds a quantity of inspiration:

  • Higher inspiration means faster art progress, so location choice is a rate multiplier rather than flavour.
  • Making art drains that location's inspiration, which is the built-in anti-camping mechanism — you have to keep moving.
  • Inspiration refills slowly between rounds, faster the more education you have. Education is what makes previously-used locations reusable sooner.

Payout

Finished artwork is pawned at the pawn shop. Value scales with the Artist's experience — so there are two scaling stats doing different jobs: education drives throughput, experience drives price per piece.

Because the role is already crossing the map constantly, picking up VexCorp quests to complete en route is close to free score.

Current power level

The Artist is widely considered overtuned, and a developer has confirmed it's being adjusted. A benchmark solo run on normal difficulty reported a car before round 10, the fancy apartment before round 15, the entire electronics and furniture catalogues before round 30, and work experience level 6 by round 30. Two structural advantages drive it: art income scales sharply once a car removes the travel bottleneck, and the role earns money and work experience from the same action, so there's no tradeoff between income and career progress. A narrower diagnosis from players is that the real outlier is simply that the Artist never needs to buy clothes, removing an upkeep cost every other role pays. player-reported, dev-confirmed nerf pending

Events & Quests

Three separate randomisers run alongside your plan: global events that reshape the whole board, choice events that fire at the start of your round, and quests that hand you optional score. They exist specifically so the game can't be solved with one memorised line.

Global events

Global events are world-wide modifiers, not per-player ones, and each has a visual presence on the map — blimps, tourists, meters — so you can read the current state off the board rather than a menu. Active events are viewable any time via the Newspaper button in the HUD.

Frequency is a setup option running up to Extreme, and events occur more randomly than they once did. Global events begin from round 3. older build — in earlier versions, if nothing fired on round 3 then round 4 was a guaranteed crime wave; crime has since left the event pool and become a permanent core system, so that specific fallback may no longer apply.

Heat Wave reworked — movement pressure

Current version v1.3: a fever meter appears at the top of your screen and fills as you move around the map. Fill it completely and you pass out from heat stroke — your round ends immediately, which is among the worst things that can happen to you in this game. Heat is now a tax on traversal, so a heat wave round is a round of short routes and detours.

Counterplay is cooling actions: a shake at Clucker's, bathing in the pond, and a new action standing in front of the freezer at Moo Moo Mart.

Previous version pre-v1.3: every action and every movement cost extra hygiene, including actions with no normal hygiene cost. It was scrapped for being one-dimensional and for generating bugs — notably breaking bots, which couldn't parse why actions had become impossible. Older guides describing heat wave as "a hygiene tax" are describing this version.

Under the old version the park's Bathe in the Pond was massively buffed during the event and was the strongest answer available; hand washing worked as a small last-action top-up, and the bank's Wash Hand perk covered it comfortably.

The zero-hygiene trap

You cannot sleep at zero hygiene, and relaxing — which would raise hygiene — is also blocked there. During a heat wave it's possible to strand a turn with no good way to end it. Keep a hygiene buffer before the event bites.

Economic Crash 4 rounds — the biggest disruption

Four things happen at once:

  1. Every player loses their job. No exceptions.
  2. The job office offers only tier 1 and 2 jobs. This is the actual cost — re-employment is normally trivial, so the tier cap is what hurts. A tier 6 career drops to burger flipping until it lifts.
  3. Prices rise at Clucker's, the gym and the mall, and stocks crash.
  4. A soup kitchen opens in the park — free food, but the queue is long, so you pay in time instead of money.

It lasts roughly four rounds, the longest of any event. Priorities while it runs, in order: quests first (score plus decent money, and completely unaffected), then education (also unaffected), then working only if you have actions left or badly need cash, then attribute maintenance.

Job choice during a crash matters more than usual. With stress under level 4, tier 2 VexCorp is the best available — good money, good XP, and the teleport lets you work to the last scrap of time then jump to the park for soup, which you take as your final action because the queue is so slow. With stress at level 4 or above, take tier 2 academy instead and simply sleep to neutralise the penalty. Tier 2 Moo Moo Mart is a reasonable gamble thanks to the free lottery ticket and its proximity to the park.

Keep money in the bank — interest is unaffected. Never buy stock during a crash, and if event frequency is high, don't hold stock at all. Buy afterwards as prices revert.

Tourist Invasion 2 rounds — rotating location lockout

A cruise ship arrives and tourists occupy one location per round, locking it, then move to a different location the next round. Two rounds, two locations. It's the most frustrating event in the game because sometimes there simply is no counterplay — it can close the job office right after you're fired, or the bank on the round you needed cash for rent.

The answer is redundancy rather than reaction: don't build a plan that depends on one specific building being open on one specific round.

Kaiju Battle two-faction tug of war

Giant avatars of the stock market and the fast food industry fight, with a meter showing who's winning. Pick a side by visiting the Bank or Clucker's. Allegiance is measured relatively — rewards go to whoever aided one side more than the other, so contributing to both is possible and net contribution is what counts.

Two contribution channels:

  • Propaganda posters, collected at Clucker's or the stock market and hung around town. Only one poster per location, so the two sides overwrite each other constantly. It's contested tempo, not a stockpile — late placement likely beats early.
  • Repeatable side-specific quests, which are uncapped and grindable.

Outcomes reshape the economy for the rest of the game rather than just paying out. Clucker's winning crashes the stock market and hands its supporters gift cards; the stock market winning sends stocks soaring, makes Clucker's more expensive for several rounds, and hands its supporters treasure.

Position, then contribute

The developers explicitly invite manipulating the market ahead of the result. The real play is backing the side you can profit from, not the side you prefer — but rewards require having aided the winner, so your holdings and your contributions need to point at the same outcome.

Smaller events flash sale, fishing frenzy, system failure, crime wave

Flash Sale

20% off all mall furniture — and the discount was later increased. This is when you buy the overheating computer, burglar trap and home library. It stacks with the Haggler education trait and the snake pet's mall discount for up to roughly 45% off. It does not affect pawn shop items.

Bank interest jumps from 4% to 40% for one round, or 44% with Bean Counter. The single biggest money swing in the game if your balance is sitting there when it fires.

Crime Wave

No longer a random event — it's a guaranteed core system in every game, with frequency deliberately raised. Money, furniture and inventory items each carry a theft chance unless your security bar is full. See the Home tab for the security numbers.

Choice events

A choice event fires at the start of each round, offering up to four options, each a reward or a punishment. They're written for comedy, but they're a real decision layer — dozens were added in v1.3, including role-specific events for every role that previously lacked them.

Two setup toggles now exist: show each option's outcome before you pick, and turn choice events off entirely. The first turns the system from memorisation into genuine strategy — you choose what fits your current state rather than what you happen to remember. If you dislike randomness, both are worth setting.

Quests

Quests are issued at VexCorp and were introduced specifically to stop the game being solvable — they're randomised per run, so some align with what you were already doing and some force a detour. Each is one or more objectives inside a time limit, paying score and cash.

  • One quest of each duration class per day — short, medium and long.
  • No repeats until you've completed VexCorp's whole list, at which point they start coming round again.
  • Hard quests generally carry a three-round gate, so accepting one late strands you. Working backwards from a 30-round game, accept by round 25 and round 28 to finish in time. They pay roughly 135 score and $270.
  • Abandon Quest lets you drop one rather than being locked in. v1.3
  • Cash rewards were raised substantially so that taking a quest is a reasonable use of a round for any role, not just the Adventurer. Quest score, by contrast, has been reduced twice.
  • Chailand-specific quests and Chailand variants of existing quests were added, because quests tuned for Calicornia landed as either trivial or brutal on the larger map.
Quest shuffle

Because hard quests carry three-round gates, accepting one immediately can strand you for two rounds doing nothing else. Squeezing an easy quest in first buys you flexibility. There's a second use: the game avoids re-offering a hard quest you just finished, but completing an easy one in between can bring it back into the pool. It's a gamble — better score, or a repeat that wastes a round — and it's mainly worth it in late-game solo play or during an Economic Crash. Against other players, secret missions are the better use of the time.

Quest counters don't reset

Completing a quest does not reset per-round action counters. A newly drawn quest requiring an action you've already used that round — anything once-per-round, like listening to an NPC or a specific park activity — is simply unfinishable until next round. On a final turn, that can make a drawn quest impossible. It's a known rough edge rather than a bug the game acknowledges. player-reported

Secret missions

Three secret missions are chosen at the start of every game and revealed at the end. Each is a hidden competition, and only the player who best met the objective is rewarded — winner takes all, no shared or threshold payout. You can fulfil one accidentally and still be paid.

To find out what they are, hunt the raccoon that hides in a random bathroom each round. Finding it opens the dossier; players who haven't looked see it redacted, and there's no way to share what you've learned. Because the raccoon relocates every round, the hunt repeats rather than being a one-time unlock.

Missions are decided by raw action count — the player who fires the most qualifying actions wins. That means mobility converts directly into secret-mission win rate, since time spent travelling is time not spent spamming.

Multiplayer & PvP

The base ruleset is entirely non-interactive — nobody can lock your buildings, force draws on you, or steal your cash. All of that arrives only if competitive mode is switched on at setup, which makes PvP a separate game rather than a layer on top.

Modes and player counts

  • Up to 6 players. Local play is turn-based on one screen rather than split screen despite what the store page implies — each player takes their turn with the full display, and one controller passed around is enough.
  • Online play supports regions for Asia Pacific, Europe, North America and South America, with Steam invites and a server browser showing map and game mode.
  • Local co-op cannot be taken online. Remote Play Together works, but only for bringing in people who don't own the game.
  • Solo works either against bots or with no opponents at all.
  • There is no character customisation beyond avatar selection — the roster is fixed.
Matchmaking is effectively empty

Players consistently report zero public games available. Online play in practice means friends or the official Discord, which is also where people organise the achievement that requires playing against someone who already has it. player-reported

Bots

Bot history matters because advice about them ages badly. They launched close to useless, were retrained into something that trounced newcomers, and now have three difficulty levels — set separately from game difficulty, and in local games settable per bot, so you can mix them. Bots work online as well as locally, participate in PvP, and understand the difficulty settings.

Their known limitations, which are genuinely exploitable:

  • Bots only ever pick the Workaholic. Teaching them other roles is planned but not shipped.
  • Bots don't attempt hard quests — they'll take easy and medium ones.
  • They handle the larger Chailand map poorly, and have historically done things like buying a metro pass and never using it.
  • A developer has confirmed they're "a little dumb" and being improved, and a new mode is in development specifically to add challenge to single-player.
Bots as a mechanical switch in solo

Some effects need an opponent to target. Turning a single bot on — even one you have no intention of competing with — is what makes PvP-tagged cards and effects fire at all. If you're playing solo and want the full ruleset active, add a bot. player-reported

PvP sausages and the raccoons

With competitive mode on, players accrue PvP Sausages passively over time — they aren't purchased. They're spent at the raccoons in the park, which is the only place any PvP action can be taken, so every act of aggression costs you a trip across the map.

There is no "PvP button"

Two of the most common questions in the community are variations of "where is PvP?". It is not a mode toggle you press mid-game — go to the park, open the raccoon tab, choose attack.

The arcade contest

On Chailand the arcade is a three-way leaderboard fight. Only the player holding a machine's high score at the end of the final round converts it into score, so positions can be stolen right up to the end and defending is open-ended by design.

The mechanic most players miss is the hidden gamer skill stat, which rises every time you play and every time you use the arcade job perk. The tactic that falls out of it: commit to one machine and spam it to build skill, then near the end play the other two machines once each — your inflated skill posts scores on them that opponents who spread their plays can't match.

Simultaneous turns

Available online and, since v1.3, in single-player against bots. Report cards and choice events resolve player-by-player in turn order, then everyone acts at once, with a round barrier holding the finished players until the lobby catches up. A six-player ten-round game runs under twenty minutes against several hours sequentially.

The tactical shift is that contested resources become genuine races: getting to the pawn shop first for furniture stolen in a crime wave, or reaching the park to lock a building before the person walking toward it arrives. A score log tracks the lobby live and each pawn shows its owner's remaining time.

Saving, rejoining and pausing

  • Manual saves every round, multiple slots, local and online, plus Steam Cloud.
  • Loading a multiplayer save opens a join screen, and you can force start with missing players removed.
  • Bathroom Break pauses an online game; Vote to End Game finishes a run early with scoring resolved at that point.
  • Disconnected players show as a robot head inviting them to rejoin from the online menus.
  • Saves live at C:\Users\USERNAME\AppData\LocalLow\Porcelain Fortress — copying that folder is how players run parallel sessions. player-reported

In short: PvP is opt-in, lives entirely at the park raccoons, and runs on a sausage economy where the profitable actions are curfew and raid rather than the disruptive ones. Keep three sausages in reserve so you can always break a goose, use lockdown defensively against events rather than players, and remember that the mayoral vote and the arcade leaderboard are attacks that don't look like attacks.

Efficiency

What is $50 actually worth? What does a point of happiness cost? This tab collects the exchange rates the game never states, so you can judge whether an action is worth taking rather than guessing.

How to read this tab

The game does not publish conversion rates — there is no stated "1 happiness = $X". Everything here is derived from listed prices, stated effects and community testing, and rankings are more reliable than absolute figures. Where a number is an inference rather than something the game or its developers stated, it says so.

The core insight: time is scarcer than money

Money becomes abundant once your career is running; time never does. A round is a fixed budget and every action, including walking, spends it. That single asymmetry decides almost every efficiency question in the game:

  • An expensive action that costs little time usually beats a cheap action that costs a lot of it.
  • Anything that reduces travel — a bus pass, a car, a teleport, metro tickets — is buying actions, which is why transport pays back faster than almost any furniture.
  • Attributes that score at the end of every round mean a small cheap top-up now is worth more than a large one three rounds later.

The Jacuzzi trick

The relax-and-sleep pairing costs about two time blocks, which is a large slice of a round. Score per dollar

Furniture is the one place the game shows you the rate directly: the info box in the furniture store displays a star rating per item. Use it. Beyond that, two hard numbers are worth memorising:

  • Pawn shop top slot: an $800 item scores 240; a $600 item scores 180. Both occupy the same slot, so always take the $800. That's 0.30 score per dollar against 0.30 — identical rates, but the bigger item wins because the slot, not the money, is the limiting resource.
  • Cheap electronics at the pawn shop are the outright best score-per-dollar in the game and shouldn't be skipped on sight. The Pal1000 at $120 that sells for $700 at the mall is close to free money and full security.

In short games the ranked buy list on the Home tab is a score-per-cost ordering — that's exactly what it was built as. Do not use it in a 30-round game, where you can afford everything and want the attribute engine instead.

Buy order

The most common way a run goes wrong is spending early money on things that feel like progress. The efficient ordering, and why:

  1. Bus pass ($200). Buys actions every round from here on.
  2. Overheating Computer ($300). Not optional — education tier 2 requires it, and education gates your entire job ladder.
  3. Burglar Trap ($300). Buy before roughly round 8, because that's when crime waves start landing. Losing furniture and cash costs more than the trap does.
  4. Clothing as job tiers demand it — casual around tier 3, fancy or business around tier 5. Clothes decay on a round clock, so a clothing store job's dry-cleaning perk removes this cost entirely on Chailand.
  5. Car ($1200) at around work level 4.
  6. Home Library ($1000). Required for education tier 3, which is what unlocks the bank.
  7. Attribute furniture, health pieces first, once a high-tier salary is running.
  8. Pal1000 ($700, or $120 at the pawn shop) once your furniture collection has eaten your security bar.
  9. Fridge — optional and late, or never. Attribute furniture outscores it, and it slows your tempo.
  10. Pet in the final five rounds, unless you specifically want its trait running all game.
Fridge or Segway? Neither, first

When a purchase competes with a purchase, ask which one keeps paying. Transport and the education-gate furniture pay every round for the rest of the game. A fridge pays once per meal and costs you the rounds spent stocking it. Attribute furniture pays every round-end tally. Pure score items pay once, at the end — which is why they're last unless the game is nearly over.

Rent: is paying immediately most efficient?

Yes, and it isn't close. Missing rent doesn't merely delay a payment — you forfeit the rent score category and the landlady pawns your furniture the following round to collect, which converts your biggest score line into cash at the pawn shop's terrible 30% rate. Persistent non-payment evicts you to the park, which removes every home action.

So the efficient behaviour is to hold the cash rather than to earn it later: keep $150 / $250 / $400 depending on your apartment on hand the round before it falls due, more on hard difficulty, and bank the rest. Because rent escalates $50 every due date, budget upward as the game goes rather than assuming a flat figure. Note also that you must clear owed rent before swapping apartments, so a missed payment blocks your housing upgrade too.

Getting value from the last scraps of a round

Turn remainders are where good players quietly out-score everyone. With less than about a twelfth of a day left, don't wander — take a filler:

  1. Wash Hands at Clucker's, the mall or the academy — some hygiene, essentially free.
  2. Touch Grass in the park — a little happiness, once per round.
  3. A job perk if you're standing in your workplace anyway.
  4. Sleep if stress is at level 4 — cancelling the punishment is worth more than any small gain.

Two related techniques. Attribute exchange: with a scrap of time and nothing to do, park actions trade one attribute for another, and bathing in the pond nets more scoring thresholds than it costs. Academy free food: when a whole round goes to studying and your free-food bonus can't fire, temporarily switch to a lower-tier academy job purely to eat for nothing — even at high stress you only need to sleep to dodge the punishment.

Things that look efficient and aren't

  • Pawn shop flipping. Payout was cut from around 60% to 30% of value; the buy-cheap-and-resell loop now loses hundreds per cycle against just playing.
  • The lottery as income. $35 for a 3% shot at ~$1000 is roughly break-even before you account for the action, and multiple tickets can't multiply a win.
  • Magnet fishing money bags. They score nothing directly and only convert into furniture and attribute score after further setup time.
  • Chasing work level 8. It's near-impossible to reach and doesn't award enough score to justify the rounds.
  • Buying an expensive bed at tier 6. A tier 6 job pins you at stress level 4 regardless — sleeping cancels the punishment for free, so the bed buys nothing.
  • Premium pet food. Marginally better than basic for a real price premium.
  • Stocks under high event frequency. Crash odds turn the whole track negative.

In short: spend money to save time, not the reverse. Buy transport and the education-gate furniture before anything that merely looks like progress, defend health because it's the expensive bar and spend hygiene because it's the cheap one, pay rent the moment it's due because the penalty compounds through your furniture, and fill every turn remainder with a free action rather than a walk.

Undocumented

Rules the game hides, actively refuses to explain, or explains wrongly. Several of these are the difference between a run going strangely and a run going wrong.

Traps that will catch you once

Playing with matches can burn down a shop

The quest that asks you to play with matches in a toilet carries a 50/50 chance of closing the building you did it in — it catches fire. It was implemented as a joke, then forgotten, and it's still live with no signposting whatsoever. Do it inside Clucker's and you can lock yourself out of food for the round. A developer confirmed the mechanic and admitted the game should probably show flames on the building so people can connect the two. dev-confirmed

Barry zeroes your happiness

Mayor Barry's description says he does nothing. Players have documented player and bot happiness being reset to zero every round from roughly round 16 of a 20-round game, verified by deliberately re-electing him, with the effect stopping when he resigns. An official patch "lessened" Barry's happiness drain, but the late-game wipe has never been acknowledged or documented. player-reported

The "did I eat?" problem is deliberate

There is no eating indicator and there will never be one — the developers have said so directly, on the grounds that forgetting to eat is a core part of the game and an indicator would reduce play to following a checklist. Compounding it: only the first meal of a round registers, so free food from a random event, an elevator gag or a role bonus consumes your day's meal and makes a purchased meal not count. This silently breaks achievement runs and any secret mission that counts meals.

The Shopaholic's coupon card stamps each purchase, which makes it an accidental meal log — the closest thing to a tracker the game offers.

The banker never pays you back

The quest where you lend the banker money is not a loan. Players have chased it for twenty turns and always get "maybe next week"; a developer confirmed he's never repaid anyone either. Treat the cash as spent the moment you hand it over. dev-confirmed

Hacker busts aren't a threshold

Players kept getting busted at heat levels that looked safe, because it isn't a threshold system. At the end of your round a red arrow resolves against your heat bar; if it lands on any filled heat you're busted, if it misses you're safe. Low heat can still bust you — you're reducing the odds, never eliminating them until heat is zero. There is also no active way to reduce heat: passive decay from a clean round is the only sink that exists.

Living in the park removes home actions

Eviction is the only way to end up in the park, and while you're there every home-only action disappears. The way most players discover this is failing to find the option to assemble the dinosaur skeleton — which can only be done at your apartment.

Heat wave can strand your turn

You cannot sleep at zero hygiene. Relaxing raises hygiene — but relaxing is also blocked at zero. During a heat wave that combination can leave a turn with no good ending.

Systems the game simply doesn't tell you about

  • The Golden Throne — a fifth, gated bathroom inside the bank, requiring education level 2 and tier 2 clothing. It's the clearest proof that clothing tier gates rooms, not just jobs.
  • Adventurer levels are never described before you earn them; traits are only explained after the fact. The Mystic's zodiac cards, by contrast, are documented in the in-game notebook — the inconsistency is a known community complaint.
  • The Drifter has no job history UI beyond the job office checkmarks. The character sheet shows currently held traits, not which jobs you've already burned.
  • Secret missions are won on raw action count, so mobility converts directly into mission wins — time spent travelling is time not spent spamming.
  • Attribute bars have scoring threshold slits. Ending a round just below one wastes the round's tally.
  • Furniture score is visible before purchase as a star rating in the store info box — most players never notice it.
  • Arcade gamer skill is a hidden stat that rises with every play and every use of the arcade job perk. Since v1.3 the chambers show your exact Gamer Score.
  • The Senior's movement penalty applies while driving. It's a role-level multiplier, not a walking effect, so a car doesn't fix it. player-reported
  • Money bags from magnet fishing score nothing directly — they only become score by converting into furniture and attributes.
  • Interest sources stack rather than overriding each other.
  • Metro tickets are consumed every round whether you ride or not.
  • The tutorial sends you to return a fish to the pet store, which is inside the mall on Calicornia. Nothing says so.
  • Language is changed in-game, under Settings → General settings, not through Steam.
  • Your save is hidden behind "start a new game." Enough players missed it that many concluded the game had no save system.

Known rough edges

  • Quest counters don't reset when a new quest is drawn, so a quest asking for a once-per-round action you've already used is unfinishable that round. Drawing "do all five park activities in one round" on a final turn is the classic case.
  • Pet penalties in the early game fire repeatedly for some players even with all meters above half and the pet attended each turn. Whether that's the intended cost of investment or a bug remains unresolved with no developer answer.
  • The double-interest perk during the bank failure event has been reported as setting interest to 8% rather than 80%, losing the event's own 40%.
  • Economic Crash has overrun its duration in at least one build, staying active far beyond its usual four rounds.
  • The game doesn't know it's a solo session. Competitive scoring tasks still evaluate as if contested — "touch grass the most" remains a task you have to perform even when nobody could possibly beat you.
  • Travel cannot be cancelled once you click a destination.
  • Bathroom hand washing was reported incomplete on Chailand in an older build; the achievement tied to it was later fixed. older build
  • The 50-round ceiling is a real content limit — players report it isn't enough to see everything, and there's no endless or marathon mode.

What's likely to change

Worth knowing which parts of this guide have the shortest shelf life. The developers have publicly committed to: further role balance passes (with the Artist explicitly named), more roles chosen by community vote, more pets including some with entirely new traits, more choice events and global events, new game modes including faster games and co-operative play, an additional map, and mod support, which slipped before 1.0 and has no date. Bot behaviour is improved in most major patches.

Two cut roles — the Angler and the Trader — may eventually return, but sit behind ideas the team prefers.

In short: the game withholds information on purpose in places and by accident in others. The costly ones are the matches quest burning a building, Barry's silent happiness wipe, the first-meal-only eating rule, and the hacker bust being a dice roll rather than a threshold. Everything else on this page is the sort of thing you'd otherwise learn by losing a round to it.
