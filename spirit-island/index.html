❧Core Strategy

Every spirit, every game, shares the same underlying puzzle before you specialize into a playstyle: read which win condition your table is actually racing toward, spend tempo on disrupting the invader pipeline rather than just mopping up after it, and know which resources are worth defending versus worth losing. The principles below apply regardless of which spirit is in front of you.

Fear Is a Win Condition, Not a Side Effect

Spirit Island has two parallel win paths: clear every Invader off the board, or advance the Fear track until a Fear card itself ends the game. New players default to board-clearing and treat Fear as flavor text, but the Fear track is usually the faster, more forgiving route — the board-clear requirement actually loosens as Terror Level rises (eventually you only need to clear cities and towns, not every last Explorer). A power that deals 2 damage and generates 2 Fear is often a better play than one that deals 4 damage and none. Check the Fear track's current tier before choosing your line for the round, and don't discount a card just because its damage number looks small.

Disrupt the Pipeline, Don't Just React to It

Invaders run Explore → Build → Ravage every round whether or not you act. You cannot out-race that pipeline by only killing what's already on the board — a land you ignore this round explores again next round regardless. The efficient target is upstream: killing or moving an Explorer before it builds prevents both the Build and the Ravage that would have followed. Ask "what enters the pipeline this round" before asking "what do I kill."

Defense Before Offense, Every Round

Before playing any card, identify which lands will actually Ravage this round — that's the damage you cannot undo. Secure or accept those first; only then look for offensive or disruptive plays elsewhere. The common beginner failure mode is chasing satisfying Invader kills on turn 1 while a fully preventable Ravage goes unaddressed, because killing feels productive and defending doesn't.

Fast vs. Slow Power Timing

Fast powers resolve before the Invader phase and can stop this round's Ravage outright. Slow powers resolve after Invaders act, so they set up future rounds rather than saving the current one. Confusing the two — relying on a Slow power to save a land that Ravages this round — is one of the most common and most game-losing rules errors in the game.

Coastal Chokepoints Early

Invaders only ever enter the board via coastal lands. Spreading Presence evenly across the map wastes early tempo — concentrating on coastal lands in the first few rounds, before Towns fill those regions, chokes off inland spread for the rest of the game. The same Presence placed on the coast in round 1 is worth far more than in round 4, because it prevents an entire lineage of future Explore/Build/Ravage cycles rather than answering one.

Fast Spirits, Slow Spirits, and Why Both Win

Some spirits (Lightning, Shadows) come online almost immediately and punch hard from round one; others (Earth, Keeper) are deliberately slow, spending early turns building a board-wide engine that pays off from the midgame on. Neither is strictly better — a fast spirit buys the table time for a slow spirit to ramp, and a slow spirit's eventual ceiling can outscale what a fast spirit sustains solo.

Energy Is the Real Bottleneck, Not Card Count

Having a hand full of powerful cards is worthless if you can't afford to play them. Energy income (growth track, innate effects) determines how many of your options are actually live each round — track it as deliberately as you track board threats. When a growth choice trades presence/cards for energy, weigh it against whether you're currently option-constrained or energy-constrained.

"Always Thin the Biggest City" Is a Trap — Usually

Big Invader cities look like the highest-value target, but a card that clears a stacked city is often a card that could have prevented three smaller Ravages elsewhere for the same cost. Default to prevention; escalate to demolition only when a city is about to cascade Blight, sits on a Fear-card condition you need, or blocks a Terror Level threshold.

Solo Demands Self-Sufficiency; Multiplayer Rewards Specialization

Solo, every gap in your spirit's toolkit is a gap nobody else covers. Multiplayer changes the underlying puzzle rather than just scaling it up: spirits that struggle to be self-sufficient often perform much better with a partner, since you can informally trade tempo ("I clear this, you clear that"). Don't judge a spirit's multiplayer strength by imagining it trying to solo-cover the whole island.

Let Difficulty Level Set Your Risk Tolerance

At low difficulty, losing an undefended, Dahan-free, Blight-free land is a real, affordable choice — spend your tempo building your engine instead of firefighting everything. At high difficulty, cascading Blight and faster escalation punish the same "let it go" calls that were free at lower levels. Recalibrate risk tolerance to the difficulty you're actually playing.

Expect an Early Struggle — That's the Shape of the Game

Games follow a predictable arc: Invaders are ahead for the first several rounds, there's a midgame inflection point, and spirits pull ahead from there as engines come online. Don't panic or overcommit resources trying to prevent early damage that isn't fatal — strategic patience early is what lets your spirit reach the power spike that actually wins the game.

Talk Before You Plan

Two minutes of table talk before each planning phase — which lands Ravage this round, what each spirit can actually do about it, what's falling through the cracks — consistently outperforms silent parallel play. Coordinate in outcomes ("that mountain is handled"), not full inventories of your hand.

Identity: the game's purest burst-destroyer. Lightning doesn't hold territory or grind down invaders slowly — it periodically detonates a Town or City via its innate, Thundering Destruction, then spends a turn or two rebuilding the fuel for the next detonation. Reputation as the easiest spirit in the base game to pilot competently is earned: the card-selection logic barely depends on reading card text.

Core Strategy: the Engine

Lightning's real constraint isn't energy or actions — it's card scarcity. Its card-play track is the best in the base game, but it has no way to gain new cards outside Reclaim, so most turns it has more plays available than cards worth playing. That inverts the usual deckbuilding logic: prioritize acquiring any playable card over holding out for a strong effect, and when picking minor powers, weight energy cost and element (Fire/Air) far above the printed effect.

The innate itself scales hard with cards committed: 3 Fire+2 Air kills a Town, 4 Fire+3 Air kills a City, and 5 Fire+4 Air+1 Water kills two settlements in one activation. Reaching the top tier wants 4-5 cards in a turn, exactly what Lightning's play track is built to support.

Growth-Track Priorities

Three viable shapes, not a single correct line: Destroyer of Towns minimizes presence growth for consistent 2-town-per-turn destruction via heavy reclaiming — highest sustained impact but card-luck dependent. Stable Card Play builds energy/hand consistency before leaning on the innate, which doesn't fully come online until around turn 5 — lower ceiling, far more forgiving. Riding the Storm deliberately alternates weak 1-card turns with explosive 4-5 card turns, banking presence and Sacred Sites during the quiet turns — most destructive over a full game, but requires comfort with turns that look like you're doing nothing.

Key Powers & Combos

  • Swiftness of Lightning (special rule): every Air element lets you treat one Slow power as Fast. Keep Air in reserve as a tactical resource — a Slow power that resolves after invaders often outperforms its Fast conversion because it had more setup time.
  • Shatter Homesteads is Lightning's single most valuable starting card — denies builds and threatens towns from range, independent of the innate.
  • Gather + Push combo: pull a Town adjacent to a City so one innate activation levels both.
  • Letting invaders build up before striking means a bigger kill and proportionally more Fear when the hammer falls.

Common Mistakes

  • Playing Raging Storm reactively just because energy is idle — it's weak solo and shines paired with an ally finishing off damaged invaders.
  • Chasing card-play growth before the energy to actually fund those plays.
  • Neglecting Sacred Site range math until it's already a problem late-game.

Weaknesses & Pairings

Explorers are Lightning's blind spot — it has no efficient way to clear them and will accumulate Blight without help. Pairs well with spirits that patch that gap (Keeper of the Forbidden Wilds, Serpent Slumbering Beneath the Island).

Core Strategy: Juggling the Invaders, Not Clearing Them

Green does not need to keep every land clear. Its unique power, Choke the Land with Green, sacrifices its own presence to cancel a Build or Ravage — which means Green can deliberately let 1-2 lands (ideally coastal) build up indefinitely as long as you keep preventing the Ravage. Trying to actively clear every land wastes its efficiency; the spirit's value is triage, not cleanup.

Decide per-land whether stopping the Build or the Ravage is more efficient — if invaders present can't cause Blight even if they hit, let the Build happen and save your presence; if Blight is otherwise unavoidable, spend presence preventing the Ravage instead.

Growth-Track Priorities

Card-play growth generally edges out the Major unlock in value for Green specifically, since its game is played through frequent small-to-medium interventions rather than one big swing per turn. Presence sacrificed to Choke the Land is recoverable through growth — treat it as a renewable resource, not a permanent loss.

Key Powers & Combos

  • Gift of Proliferation and Growth Through Sacrifice — 0-cost, multi-element minors that let allies spread for the cost of Green's own presence. The backbone of its support identity.
  • Moon+Water combo turns let Green defend and attack the same turn, activating both innate tiers simultaneously.
  • Lightning + Green opener: Green accelerates Lightning via Gift of Proliferation; Lightning returns the favor. Both Fast — can destroy starting towns before invaders finish exploring.
  • Pairs especially well supporting fragile early-game spirits (Thunderspeaker, Shadows Flicker Like Flame, Vital Strength of the Earth) — hold support for a stronger ally until later, since early donations to an already-strong spirit have lower marginal value.

Common Mistakes

  • Trying to keep every touched land permanently clear — unnecessary and inefficient.
  • Under-using the multi-land juggling that is the spirit's actual power source.
  • Placing presence back onto the board before clearing space on growth tracks first.

Complexity Note

A strong Green turn might touch 4-6 lands at once, which asks a lot of short-term memory even though no single decision is individually hard — Moderate complexity is about orchestration, not any one power's text. Explorers are a genuine gap (no efficient solo removal), so it leans on allies or Choke-the-Land denial.

Core Strategy: Majors Over Minors, Sacred Sites Over Spread

Earth has the fastest energy track and the slowest card-play track of any base-game spirit — a shape that argues against cycling minor powers or forcing the innate, and strongly for a small number of expensive, powerful Major Powers played repeatedly. Building toward the innate competes directly with the "just take strong Majors" plan for the same budget — treat the innate as a nice-to-have to revisit mid-to-late game.

The passive, Earth's Vitality, gives every Sacred Site +3 Defend automatically — it no-sells Ravage damage if total invader damage is 3 or less, but the invaders are still there afterward. Treat every Vitality-blocked Ravage as a bought turn, not a solved problem — follow up with a Dahan counterattack or damage power, or that land keeps re-building until it exceeds 3 damage.

Growth-Track Priorities

  • Reclaim-and-place is uniquely efficient for Earth — one of the only spirits where Reclaim places presence and refreshes cards simultaneously, at range 2 (better than the "gain a new power" option's range 0).
  • Push energy to 3/turn, then take one extra card play, then push energy further (up to 8/turn), and only then chase a 4th card play — the starting hand already costs more energy than it can spend.
  • Maximize Sacred Site count above almost everything else — by end-game a well-played Earth can have 6, each independently defending 3.

Key Powers & Combos

  • Rituals of Destruction scales hard with Dahan present (up to 5 damage + 2 fear with 3+ Dahan). It's Slow, so only cast on a land not about to Ravage — the Ravage would kill gathered Dahan first.
  • A Year of Perfect Stillness (invaders do nothing this turn) scales well against adversaries with many simultaneous invader actions per turn (England is the standout example).
  • Guard the Healing Land combos with Rituals, and locks a land's Blight count where it is regardless of damage taken when played on an already-blighted land about to Ravage.
  • Because Earth doesn't need its innate's elements, it's free to pick Majors purely for raw effect strength rather than threshold-fit.

Common Mistakes

  • Trying to force the innate instead of treating it as an occasional bonus.
  • Over-protecting Dahan at the expense of Blight/presence management — the built-in 3 defense already makes most fights favorable.
  • Hoarding cards out of caution instead of cycling via Reclaim once the power suite is established.

Core Strategy: the Engine

Keeper's defining growth option lets it place 2 presence and gain a power card in a single pick — no other base spirit combines both. The early game is about affording that option repeatedly: end every turn with at least 2 energy banked, even if that means sacrificing a card play that turn to hit the number.

The tradeoff: most of Keeper's kit is Slow, so turns 1-2 are largely setup with little active impact, and one or two early Blight can be a real setback. This is load-bearing, not an oversight — survive turns 3-4 and the power curve takes off sharply from there.

Growth-Track Priorities

Element priority for the innate: Fire (need 2/turn, start with only 1), Sun (pairs with Plant to boost the primary damage power), Plant (maintain 3+/turn), Major Power thresholds, then Air. Roughly three-quarters of minor power draws include at least 2 of these key elements.

Key Powers & Combos

  • Punish Those Who Trespass is the primary damage engine — fuels off Fire/Sun.
  • Towering Wrath: build a network of non-adjacent Sacred Sites, clear lands between them with Punish, then Towering Wrath strikes for 6-8 damage scaling off adjacent shrines.
  • Sacrosanct Wilderness is worth grabbing early even off-element — covers gaps and adds strong Dahan-pushing utility.
  • Deliberately placing presence into invader-infested lands for an immediate 6+ damage Punish hit is a legitimate aggressive line — clean up the resulting Blight afterward.

Common Mistakes

  • Spending the last 2 energy of a turn on a non-essential play instead of preserving next turn's key growth option.

Weaknesses & Pairings

Fear generation is not what makes this spirit strong — over-focusing it is the most common way to underperform with it. The actual gameplan is support, utility, and Dahan protection. Because Bringer can't kill invaders directly, Dahan are its only source of real destruction until allies arrive — protecting them is a genuine strategic priority worth spending tempo on.

Growth-Track Priorities

Priority one: unlock the Moon element by turn 3 (turn 4 acceptable), since Bringer's innates depend on it. Default to gaining cards and spreading presence early; after Moon is secured, switch to "any element" growth or a 3rd card play depending on expected game length.

Fear Card Management (the core skill)

Try to earn one Fear card every turn. When flipping a newly-earned Fear card, avoid revealing one that would activate the same turn — pass on flipping it and let it trigger next round once you've set up for it. Sequencing which cards flip when is arguably the single biggest lever on Bringer's output.

Key Powers & Combos

  • Dread Apparitions grants Defend equal to Fear generated in that land this turn — converts the Fear engine directly into board defense.
  • Call on Midnight's Dreams can be spent for Fear, or discarded to instantly play a Major with an energy rebate scaled to Dahan present.
  • Group invaders onto lands where an ally can finish them, or onto Dahan-defended lands — Bringer loves a partner who has already damaged (not destroyed) a target.

Pairings

Bringer + Ocean's Hungry Grasp is frequently cited as one of the strongest two-spirit combos in the base game — Bringer's pushes funnel invaders toward Ocean's water, and Ocean's Drowning handles the kill Bringer structurally cannot.

Common Mistakes

  • Chasing Fear generation as the primary goal instead of a byproduct of good support play.
  • Losing Dahan early, removing the only real destruction the spirit has access to.
  • Mistimed Fear card flips that reveal an effect the same turn it activates.

Verdict: High complexity — not recommended as a first spirit. Weak solo, especially at high difficulty, since it has no invader-limiting lever beyond Dahan; excellent in multiplayer where allies supply the removal it lacks.

Core Strategy: the Drowning Loop

Ocean starts with essentially no passive energy income, but Drowning turns every kill into fuel for the next kill, bootstrapping its own economy purely through coastal destruction. Prioritize actions that funnel invaders into your ocean presence over pure damage powers — a drowned invader is both removed and paid for. The innate can reach its City-drowning tier as early as turn 2 with the right Water+Earth elements.

The tradeoff for this efficiency is range: Ocean's special restriction means it can only be pushed as far inland as the coast itself — essentially no reach beyond the shoreline on its own. This is the spirit's central limitation.

Key Powers & Combos

  • Swallow the Land-Dwellers destroys an Explorer, Town, and Dahan simultaneously — never target a land with your own Dahan present, it kills them too.
  • Tidal Boon gives allies +2 energy and pushes invaders toward your water for a follow-up drowning.
  • Call of the Deeps (Fast) gathers Explorers into oceans — its main value is prevention, canceling a Build before it happens.

Sequencing Priorities

  1. Kill Explorers during the explore phase to prevent Town construction in the first place.
  2. Let isolated Towns sit unaddressed if not urgent — one re-explored before drowning converts to more energy.

Common Mistakes

  • Placing two range-0 drowning powers on the same coastal land instead of spreading breadth across two.

The Engine: Massive Flooding

River's four starting powers all share Sun/Water elements, and Massive Flooding scales in three tiers off exactly those elements, topping out at 2 damage to every invader in a land range 1 of a shrine. River's special rule means a River presence in Wetlands counts as a shrine at just 1 presence, so shrines are cheap to establish — get enough card plays to dump your whole Sun/Water hand into the innate every turn.

River's Bounty is worth building around: it consolidates scattered Dahan (feeding a bonus Dahan + energy) and is River's energy engine simultaneously — almost always the correct reclaim target. Exception: in multiplayer, when a teammate is energy-starved, reclaiming Boon of Vigor instead to subsidize their turn can be better.

Prioritize card plays (unlocks Reclaim) over energy early. Target reaching enough card-play capacity by turn 4 that the innate's full element cost no longer eats your entire hand. Playstyle: a control spirit whose defining gimmick — placing Presence pushes Explorers/Dahan off that land — reads as a build-prevention tool first, damage-dealer second.

Core Strategy: Push-as-Control Beats Push-as-Damage

Pushing an invader off a land before Build resolves denies the build entirely, cheaper than using the same push reactively against an existing town. Read every Presence placement first as "what does this push stop."

The Core Tension

Placement competes with itself: build a Sacred Site for long-term thresholds, or hold this land open to push an incoming threat elsewhere. Treat this as a live tradeoff each growth phase.

Growth-Track Priority

Early innate thresholds are useful on their own. Treat a range/rate-boosting growth option as close to mandatory early if you want to reliably reach the top innate tiers.

Verdict: Moderate — the core "push stops a build" read is intuitive and immediately useful, though the placement-vs-Sacred-Site tension takes a game or two to internalize.

Playstyle: a damage/control hybrid whose special rule reduces the health of Towns/Cities sitting at its own Sacred Sites, converting marginal "can't quite finish this land" situations into one-hit kills.

Core Engine

The tiered innate's level 1 kills lone Explorers and is easy to hit almost every turn regardless of growth track — three of the four unique powers carry Fire+Air, feeding it passively.

Growth-Track Priority

Energy/Majors vs. Plays/Minors is a genuine, forgiving fork — both tracks cap at the same total, so neither starves the other. Innate level 1 stays cheap on either path, making Rising Heat unusually forgiving about which track you commit to.

Key Strategic Pattern: Consolidation

Push everything toward a single kill zone, ideally coastal — cheaper to solve with fewer powers than scattered small problems, and it throttles new Explorer sources. Combine with the Sacred-Site health-reduction rule by building your Sacred Site where the pile ends up.

Verdict: Moderate — the dual-track fork and consolidation logic take a game to click, but the Sacred-Site rule is simple to state with immediately visible payoff.

Playstyle: a disease-and-revenge spirit whose three special rules only make sense read together — "two spirits stapled together," splitting between spreading Disease and dealing scaled damage. Its early game exists purely to fund a mid-game power spike.

Core Thesis: Race to the Power Spike

Vengeance is weak and fragile before reaching roughly 3 presence on the energy track plus 3 card plays. Below that point expect a rough early game; above it, everything else is gravy. Don't judge turn 1-3 by "did I solve every land," judge it by "how many turns until the spike."

The Engine

Letting a Build happen preserves the Disease tokens the kit needs; presence destroyed by non-spirit actions adds a Disease token; Blight counts as Badlands for Vengeance's damage powers. Together: place presence into a land about to Ravage, let it die, harvest the Disease, spend it, while accumulating Blight buffs your damage innates.

Key Combo

Fetid Breath seeds Disease → Plaguebearers relocates the resulting stack into one consolidated land → Epidemics Run Rampant hits the pile next turn.

Common Mistakes

  • Blocking every Build reflexively — denies your own kit the Disease tokens it needs.
  • Playing Strike Low too early, before enough sacrifice-fueled Blight has accumulated.

☠Adversaries

Every adversary escalates through 6 levels via its own escalation-card track, and "difficulty" isn't a fixed number — community win-rate data and poll threads show the same adversary rated hardest by one table and easiest by another, because each one pressures a different axis (build rate, ravage damage, blight, explorer volume) and rewards a different spirit archetype. The difficulty tags below are rough community consensus at high level (5-6), not a guarantee — read each entry for why it bites before picking a counter. This set covers the 7 adversaries with the deepest community write-ups; others exist but aren't covered here in comparable depth.

How the engine ticks

England inverts the game's default rhythm. Its buildings carry bonus health, but more importantly, Explorers build regardless of whether you've touched them — prevention-based play loses most of its value since the build isn't conditional on your interference. Its escalation stacks on top: England can build even without invaders once a land is adjacent to two buildings, and its high-Terror stages punish you with further builds and steep fear-efficiency demands. It pressures the whole board simultaneously — generalist spirits get overwhelmed while specialists that lean hard into one of the two real win conditions do better.

Early vs. late danger

England ramps slowly rather than hitting hard immediately — early turns are comparatively survivable, rewarding spirits with a backloaded power curve. The real bite arrives mid-to-late: extra invader health and build-without-invaders escalation compound around level 5, and by level 6 terror requirements get steep. Watch coastal and repeatedly-ravaged lands — England's explore patterns keep re-seeding them.

What counters it, and why

  • Destroy-effect spirits (Ocean, Thunderspeaker, Lure) bypass the bonus-health problem by removing buildings outright rather than chipping damage.
  • Fear-race spirits (Bringer, Starlight, Many Minds, Shadows) out-pace England to a terror victory before its escalation forces a loss.
  • Isolate (Jagged Earth) is the sharpest single tool against England — one of the only ways to functionally stop a build rather than out-racing it.
  • Slow-to-awaken spirits (Serpent, Trickster) get an unusually good runway here — England's gradual ramp buys the time these spirits normally lack.

Common mistakes

  • Playing prevention-first as if England were a normal adversary — engage a land after the build resolves instead of trying to pre-empt it.
  • Relying on repeated small/capped damage to clear buildings — bonus health means you fall behind the build rate.
  • Using a "funnel everything into one crisis land" tactic — England's build-everywhere pattern doesn't create a single crisis land to solve.

How the engine ticks

The simplest adversary in the game — no extra invader health, no build-order twists, no bonus actions. Its entire identity is tempo: an unusually early, escalating Stage III card front-loads several Ravage/Build waves before spirits have normally developed. It functions as a pure speed check rather than a mechanical-interaction check. Crush the early towns/explorers and the rest of the game is nearly trivial; fall behind early and there's no mid-game runway to recover in.

Early vs. late danger

Almost entirely front-loaded. Levels 0-1 are tutorial-grade. Levels 2-5 are a fair testing ground. Level 6 is a sharp spike (multiple lands escalating turn one) severe enough that experienced players sometimes avoid known-weak spirit picks. There isn't much of a late game — if you survive the opening pressure, the rest is comparatively easy.

What counters it, and why

  • Fast, explosive spirits with minimal setup cost (Wildfire, Thunderspeaker, Lure, Trickster, Fangs, Green) — matchup quality correlates almost entirely with turns 1-4 strength.
  • Containment (denying explorers/builds outright) — BP has no way to cheat builds into existence, so denial shuts down its entire engine.
  • Cheap Dahan-based clears — no toughness boost on invaders, so "defend + a couple of Dahan" clears cheaply.

Common mistakes

  • Playing a slow-scaling combo/engine spirit as if there's time to pay off a long-term plan — BP's game length usually doesn't extend far enough.
  • Prioritizing blight-resilience plans — the game is typically won or lost before blight accumulation matters.

How the engine ticks

Sweden has no bolt-on loss condition — it just amplifies the normal one. Above a damage threshold, Ravages add an extra Blight; higher levels raise town/city damage so that threshold gets crossed almost automatically. "If they Ravage, you lose" — Sweden punishes failure to prevent Ravages disproportionately, since double Blight compounds badlands and cascades fast. It's front-loaded, not sustained — no scaling explore/build engine keeps pressure up late (level 5's blight-triggers-new-town rule is the one exception).

Early vs. late danger

Danger peaks early (turn 2-3 onward) and fades once you're past the opening — inverting the usual "ramp up" shape most adversaries follow.

What counters it, and why

  • Ravage-prevention / control (Trickster's Strife-cancel, Finder's pre-Ravage removal, River's push-before-Ravage, Green's Ravage-ignore) — act before the Ravage resolves, the only place Sweden's math can be defused.
  • Fear-rush spirits (Many Minds, Starlight) — still rewards racing a Fear-track win before the Blight math comes due.
  • Blight-benefiting spirits (Vengeance) — converts the adversary's own escalation into fuel.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming fixed "Defend N" values still hold — Sweden's boosted damage means old breakpoints frequently fail to fully stop a Ravage.
  • Leaning on blight-removal minor powers as your main answer — you never get ahead of the rate against double-blight lands.

How the engine ticks

France's difficulty runs backwards from the game's usual arc — it demands you already handle Towns and multiple Explorers per land from turn one; its low-level escalation is what actually hurts, not its high-level effects. Two pressures run in parallel: the escalating "plantation" engine, and the Slave Rebellion special event, a secondary win condition — Dahan mobilized into France's built-up lands ahead of that event can wipe multiple Towns in a single stroke.

Early vs. late danger

Front-loaded and multiplicative — unanswered Explorers become extra Towns, and multiple Explorers per land compounds fast. Turns 1-3 are the highest-leverage window for isolate/board-control tactics.

What counters it, and why

  • Prevention-focused spirits — flat-out Town-prevention or pre-build Explorer clearing removes France's fuel at the source.
  • Dahan/Fear-mobilization spirits (Thunderspeaker, Trickster) — pre-positioning Dahan/Strife into built-up lands converts an ordinary turn into a multi-Town wipe via Slave Rebellion.
  • Damage/board-wipe spirits — France stacks multiple Explorers per land, so per-target removal that's normally inefficient suddenly clears two Invaders at once.

Common mistakes

  • Playing a spirit whose kit scatters/relocates Invaders rather than destroying them — delay is specifically punished by compounding Town-count.
  • Treating France as a slow-burn adversary and stalling — its escalation front-loads pressure.

How the engine ticks

Russia's core threat is explorer volume and damage, not city/town density — its "little dudes" individually hit harder than normal. Defend is weaker than usual since blocking is less efficient than removing or stranding Explorers outright. Mobility matters more than usual too, since Russia's explorers spread and move aggressively.

Early vs. late danger

Escalation triggers land on alternating turns at high levels, paradoxically making top-difficulty Russia easier to manage for certain decks than mid-level Russia. Several event/fear cards at high difficulty can chain into effectively instant-loss states — part of Russia's difficulty is genuinely variance/gimmick-driven, a different flavor of hard than England's or Sweden's.

What counters it, and why

  • Lure — near hard-counter. Explorers deal no damage to Lure's beasts, and its kit is built around killing exactly the token type Russia floods the board with.
  • Broad, cheap AOE invader damage (Mist, Many Minds) — a small ping that clears an entire stacked Explorer group is disproportionately valuable against a swarm.
  • Isolate works as a release valve when a board state is drowning in explorers.

Common mistakes

  • Leaning on defend as a primary plan — boosted per-explorer damage makes blocking inefficient.
  • Rushing fear indiscriminately at high difficulty — pulling an extra Invader card can be catastrophic when explorer pressure is already elevated.

How the engine ticks

Scotland has no bonus invader damage/health, no invulnerability, no card-count reduction — difficulty comes purely from geography and card-sequencing pressure, a "universal tax" rather than a specific counter. Land #2 (coastal, double-city) recurs constantly due to invader-deck structure, compounding toward near-certain multiple ravages there. Level 5 adds extra blight-to-ocean, specifically punishing previously-dominant coastal plays.

Early vs. late danger

Below level 5, several "obvious" counters (Ocean, Stone) look dominant. At level 5+, the added ocean-blight rule flips that. Scotland also tends to run long, favoring backloaded/support spirits (Vengeance, Memory). Board-clear victories are unusually hard against constant town production — plan around needing a full fear-track win.

What counters it, and why

  • Isolate/prevention effects (Finder, Downpour, Green, Trickster) — attack the compounding mechanism directly.
  • Fast/early-elimination spirits — most danger accrues at the Ravage/Coastal-card stage, so ending the game before that point sidesteps the design.
  • Card-cycling spirits (Fractured Days) — Scotland telegraphs exactly when its worst card is coming.

Common mistakes

  • Trusting a spirit-vs-Scotland verdict without checking the level it assumes — level 5's ocean-blight rule can flip a "strong" verdict to "weak."
  • Ignoring Land #2 in favor of spreading attention evenly — it recurs by deck structure alone and deserves dedicated early answers.

How the engine ticks

Habsburg's identity is built around blight as a loss condition and escalation trigger — its own loss condition only counts blight removed from the Fear/adversary card itself, and its high-level escalations effectively force you to blight the board yourself to manage town durability. Its signature move is gathering multiple towns into defense-in-depth clusters. The strongest counters aren't just generically strong spirits — they're spirits indifferent to or positively rewarded by blight.

Early vs. late danger

Playable and fair for most spirits at moderate levels, but Stage 2 escalation adds heavy turn-planning overhead. At level 4, destroying towns on non-blighted land requires double attack power — a hard mechanical gate.

What counters it, and why

  • Stone — the standout counter. Can push blight straight to the box, bypassing the board-blight step, and fully exploits Habsburg's clustered-towns pattern. Reported as feeling "one-sided" at high difficulty.
  • Vengeance — damage output scales directly with blight present, so its power curve outpaces the invaders' growth curve.
  • Mist — Habsburg's town-gather/movement mechanic feeds Mist's fear-generation engine.

Common mistakes

  • Picking a spirit that needs clean, blight-free land without checking Habsburg's blight-liability mechanic first.
  • Underestimating level 4's double-attack-power gate for non-blighted land.

🔥Mechanics Deep-Dives

These are the mechanics that reward a deeper read than their card text suggests — keywords and systems that cut across every spirit rather than belonging to any one of them. Understanding when they're strong, and when they're a trap, separates a mechanically-fluent table from one that's just moving pieces.

Isolate — a scalpel, not a wall▸

Isolate (Jagged Earth) severs a land's adjacency for Explore, Build-related movement, and adjacency-dependent effects — it doesn't destroy anything, it just breaks the logistics link. Used once, speculatively, it's a minor add-on worth roughly a point of Fear or a bit more than Defend 1 — noticeably underwhelming, and new players who try it a couple of times often walk away unimpressed. It only becomes strong with volume or precision: spirits that can apply it repeatedly (Downpour Drenches the World repeating a land power, Finder of Paths Unseen's innate) or players who target it deliberately rather than guessing. Target Explorer sources, not destinations — isolating the land Invaders would explore from deterministically blocks Explore into every land adjacent to it, instead of gambling on a single destination. Isolate is also the answer to several adjacency-dependent Adversary rules (Scotland's coastal adjacency, Russia's post-Ravage movement) — check whether an Adversary's special rule depends on adjacency before dismissing Isolate against it. It does not affect Dahan movement, spirit targeting, or Blight cascade.

Blight as a resource, not just a countdown▸

Treat presence, Dahan, energy, and Blight as fungible resources whose relative value shifts by situation, rather than a checklist you're obligated to keep clean. A single Blight token, an individual presence marker, or Dahan losses (as long as enough survive to retaliate) are usually acceptable losses — over-defending every last point costs more tempo than it saves. The real danger isn't isolated Blight, it's cascades: a land can stack 3-4 Blight from repeated neighboring cascades, and once every land on the board is Blighted, any further Blight triggers a chain that is effectively the true loss condition — not the Blight counter itself. Intervene actively when a land faces multiple Invader actions in one cycle, when Blight is clustering adjacent to already-Blighted lands, or when an action would flip the Blight Card. Otherwise, let it ride.

The Blight Card / Event Deck coupling▸

The Blight Card tracks Blight not yet on the island; its "Blighted Island" side has a pool size that acts as a hidden pacing dial. If you're running the Event Deck, a Blight Card is mandatory, since many Events reference island Blight state directly. If you're not running events, avoid thin Blight Cards (around 2 Blight/player on the blighted side) — those were balanced around the mitigation events provide, and without events they just make the post-flip phase brutally short. Solo tightens this further: a thin card that's manageable when several players share the response burden becomes especially punishing when one player covers every leak alone.

Reading the Fear deck — tiers and thresholds▸

The Fear deck is split into groups by Terror Level, with each Fear card printing multiple tiered effects — resolve the tier matching your current Terror Level, not the weakest top line. Using only the top effect once a stronger tier is unlocked is a common misplay that quietly costs a team real value. Destroying towns and cities is one of the most reliable Fear generators, which is part of why "always kill the biggest stack" and "prioritize Fear" often point at the same targets even though they're different justifications. Once a Fear win is realistically close, shift the whole team's priorities from territorial defense to maximizing Fear output.

Sacred sites as an investment, not a bonus▸

A sacred site is presence committed to a specific land to unlock a card's bonus effect there — it's a targeting requirement, not free upside, and it only pays off if you can actually deliver the matching power to that land on the turn(s) you need it. Placing a sacred site early to guarantee a strong Slow power lands cleanly later is a legitimate use of tempo, but only when that land will still be relevant when the payoff card comes due. Isolating a land that hosts a valuable sacred site is a solid way to guarantee it survives a Stage where you can't otherwise defend it.

Elemental thresholds — a hidden co-op layer▸

Major powers typically carry an elemental threshold that unlocks a bonus effect if enough matching elements were generated that turn; minors usually don't. Elements come from the card itself, growth choices, innate/presence-track effects, and — depending on the specific card's wording — sometimes teammates' plays too, a frequent source of table confusion (some thresholds check only your own elements, others check the whole board). Don't evaluate a major's "floor" effect in isolation: its real ceiling depends on what element support is available that turn, which is exactly why some cards function as deliberate co-op glue rather than solo-optimal picks.

Play vs. Use vs. Threshold (and why the distinction matters)▸

Three distinct things happen with a card, and conflating them causes real rules errors: Play is paying the cost and placing the card into play; Use is actually resolving its effect in the Fast or Slow phase; threshold is meeting its elemental requirement, checked at end of the Spirit phase. You can Play a card purely for its elements without ever intending to Use its effect. Less obviously, you can meet a threshold without ever Using the card at all — for example playing a Slow-phase card during the Spirit phase, having its threshold check succeed at end of Spirit phase, and then the game ending before the Slow phase would have resolved the effect. "Threshold met" and "effect used" are independent — don't assume one implies the other when tracking what a turn accomplished.

Aspects and Incarna (Nature Incarnate) — mechanic-level primer▸

Aspects are alternate configurations of an existing spirit — they can swap out special rules or innate powers, change setup, add new unique power cards, or grant Incarna access, letting a table get a materially different playstyle out of a spirit they already own without learning an entirely new one. Incarna is a physical token representing the spirit's avatar directly on the board; it counts as presence for movement purposes and is available to several new Nature Incarnate spirits directly plus a handful of older spirits via their Aspects. Practically, an Aspect changes not just numbers but the shape of the decisions a spirit asks you to make each turn — read an Aspect's changed special rule/innate before assuming a spirit you already know will play the same way.

Digital client quirks (Steam app — not tabletop rules)▸

A handful of interactions are worth flagging specifically for the digital client. Isolate: for each nearby action, the app asks you (per action, per instance) whether the isolated land counts as connected — not a fixed one-time toggle. Tokens that can count as multiple things (e.g. a sacred site counting as both animal and badlands): the app lets you decide per-token, per-action, which matters when an Event or Adversary effect targets/removes "animals" — opt individual tokens out to protect them. Sacrifice victories: an action resolves fully before win/loss is checked, so if a single action simultaneously satisfies a win condition and triggers a loss condition, the app rules it a win. These are implementation clarifications, not tabletop house rules — worth knowing if you play both formats.

⚲Team Building

Multiplayer Spirit Island isn't solo play with extra hands — assembling a table of spirits is a distinct puzzle, since each spirit's toolkit is deliberately incomplete and depends on the group to cover what it can't. A good lineup trades weaknesses for strengths rather than stacking redundant ones.

Pairing Principles

Cover the Gap, Don't Duplicate the Strength

Every spirit is missing something by design — a reliable answer to some Invader type, a way to clean up what it starts, raw damage, or range. The strongest tables aren't the ones stacking spirits that are all great at the same thing; they're the ones where each spirit's blind spot is another spirit's specialty.

Pair Fast Tempo with Slow Ceiling

A fast-acting spirit buys time for a slow-building spirit to ramp without the table losing control in the meantime. Two fast spirits can both flame out once Invaders scale past their ceiling; two slow spirits can both get run over before either comes online. Pairing tempo profiles smooths the table's power curve across the whole game.

Trade Tempo Informally, Turn to Turn

Multiplayer's real advantage over solo isn't just more hands — it's the ability to informally barter resources across turns: "I take an extra Presence placement and clear your Explorers next round." This is the main reason spirits that look weak solo often perform fine at the table. Build this into your team's shorthand explicitly rather than assuming it happens automatically.

Read the Adversary Before Finalizing the Lineup

An Adversary's specific triggers matter more than its raw difficulty number — a spirit that struggles with an Adversary's core engine is a worse pick than the numbers alone suggest, and vice versa. Where the table can see the Adversary before assigning spirits, lean the lineup toward whichever spirits directly answer its pressure points rather than picking spirits in a vacuum.

Coordination Overhead Scales Faster Than Player Count

Two-spirit tables can coordinate with a quick "I've got the coast" exchange; four-to-six-spirit tables need actual shorthand or planning grinds to a halt. Set shared priorities as simple rules and communicate in outcomes ("that region's handled") rather than listing which cards you're using — the goal is shared situational awareness, not a full inventory of every hand.

Notable Combos

Blight-Soak + Free-Swinging Damage

Pairing an aggressive, damage-forward spirit with one built to absorb or relocate Blight lets the aggressive spirit play without the team eating its Blight downside. A direct application of "cover the gap": one spirit's cost becomes the other spirit's specialty.

Relocation Feeding a Kill Zone

A spirit that can teleport or forcibly relocate Invaders can move problem targets into a zone another spirit reliably dominates. Any spirit with strong movement/displacement tools is a force-multiplier for any spirit with a strong but narrow kill zone, since it extends that zone's effective reach across the whole board.

Generator + Payoff (Beast/Token Economies)

Some spirits flood the board with a resource (beast tokens, Dahan, strife/disease markers) without much of a payoff themselves, while others convert that same resource into damage or control. Pairing a generator with its natural payoff spirit turns two individually-middling engines into a strong one.

Ramp Spirits Feeding a Slow, High-Ceiling Third

In three-plus spirit games, it's worth dedicating one or two spirits to accelerating a slow-but-high-ceiling teammate's presence/energy growth rather than insisting every spirit pull independent weight from turn one. A slow spirit can hit its power spike far earlier if teammates spend early tempo feeding its ramp.

The Pipeline Combo: Push, Kill, Control

A recurring shape: one spirit pushes/funnels Invaders toward a chokepoint, a second provides the reliable kill there, and a third adds Fear or board-wide control for whatever slips through. A repeatable, low-variance pattern — the underlying principle (funnel → finish → clean up) can be built from many different spirit combinations.

Treat "Best Pairing" Polls with Caution

Crowd-sourced "best pairing" rankings often measure thematic appeal as much as actual synergy — a top-voted pair can turn out to be "fine, but nothing special" once actually played. Use community pairing suggestions as leads to try, not proven optimal-play combos; verify what the two kits actually cover for each other, not the pairing's popularity.

✦Tips & Tricks

Smaller-grain lessons that don't rise to core strategy but repeatedly trip up new tables — misplays, sequencing tricks, and a few digital-client-specific gotchas worth knowing before they cost you a game.

Learn the Base Game Before Adding Modules

First games should skip Blight Cards, Adversaries, and Special Scenarios entirely — get comfortable with the core Explore/Build/Ravage loop and your spirit's own kit before layering on modules that change the math.

Don't Skip Your Spirit's Special Rule

Every spirit has a unique special rule beyond its power cards, and new players routinely forget to use it or don't realize it's central to how the spirit functions. For several spirits, the special rule — not any single card — is the actual engine the rest of the kit is built around.

Move Invaders, Don't Just Kill Them

Push/pull effects look weak next to direct-damage cards but are frequently the more efficient play: they can hit more Invaders per card, get threatened Dahan out of harm's way, and can prevent a Build or Ravage before it happens rather than punishing it after. Don't undervalue a control card just because its damage line reads as zero.

Stack Dahan Instead of Spreading Them

Dahan defense is a threshold effect, not a linear one. Two Dahan against a lone Explorer can end in a wash; three Dahan in the same spot can wipe it outright. Concentrating Dahan into fewer, thicker stacks beats spreading them thin.

Never Discard Your Starting Power Cards

Starting powers are deliberately elemental-diverse — that diversity is often exactly what an innate power's threshold depends on. When upgrading to major powers, replace cards you picked up mid-game, not your original starting hand.

Pick Cards for Elements When You're Threshold-Constrained

When you're close to unlocking an innate's elemental requirement, take the card that supplies the missing element over the card with the flashier effect — closing the gap can save an entire extra card play that turn. Earlier in the game, with more slack, pick for effect quality as normal.

Know Which Lands Are Cheap to Lose

An undefended land with no Dahan and no Blight yet is a safe sacrifice — the cost of defending it usually exceeds its value, especially early or at lower difficulty. Learn to distinguish "cheap to lose" lands from expensive ones (Dahan present, Blight already clustering, sacred site committed) so letting something go is a deliberate read, not an accident.

Physically Track Your Spent Resources

Place energy tokens directly on growth-track options as you gain them, place tokens on cards as you commit to playing them, and tap/exhaust cards as you activate them rather than doing it from memory. Per-turn state is dense enough that "which of my numbers is already spent" errors are one of the most common sources of accidental misplays.

Digital Client: Per-Instance Choices Aren't One-Time Toggles

Digital (Steam app) only. Some interactions ask you to decide per-action rather than setting a fixed state once — e.g. whether an Isolated land counts as connected for a given action, or whether a token that can count as multiple things counts as one or the other for a specific effect. Check the prompt each time it appears.

A Card Can Meet Its Threshold Without Ever Resolving

Elemental thresholds are checked at end of the Spirit phase, independent of whether the card's effect ever actually resolves in the Fast/Slow phase that follows. Don't assume "threshold met" means "effect happened" when reviewing what a turn accomplished — they're tracked independently.
