❧Core Strategy

Every spirit, every game, runs on the same underlying puzzle before you specialize into a playstyle: which win condition the table is actually racing toward, where to spend tempo against the Invader pipeline instead of just mopping up after it, and which resources are worth defending versus worth losing. Playing solo, all of this lands on one player instead of being split across a table, so the same principles matter even more — there is no partner to quietly cover a gap you left open.

Defense Before Offense, Every Round

The win condition text talks about destroying Invaders, and that's exactly what pulls new players toward chasing kills — while a fully preventable Ravage goes unaddressed. Before playing any card, identify which lands actually Ravage this round; that damage is the one thing you cannot undo once the Invader phase resolves. Secure or knowingly accept those lands first, then spend whatever's left disrupting future rounds, and only then look for offense against Invader pieces themselves — offense is a third priority, not a first one.

Playing solo this discipline matters more, not less: there's no teammate quietly covering the Ravage you decided to ignore while you went hunting Explorers.

Disrupt the Pipeline, Don't Just React to It

Invaders run Explore → Build → Ravage every round, continuously, whether or not you act — a Town that builds this round is a guaranteed Ravage threat next round. You cannot out-race that pipeline by only killing what's already sitting on the board; a land you leave alone this round explores again next round regardless. The efficient target is upstream: killing or pushing an Explorer before it builds erases both the Build and the Ravage that would otherwise follow it. Before asking "what do I kill," ask "what enters the pipeline this round" and "what does letting that stand cost me two rounds from now."

Coastal Chokepoints Early

Invaders only ever enter the island via coastal lands, then push inland from there unless stopped. The instinctive move — spreading Presence evenly across the map because it feels safer — is actually the weaker one. Pushing Presence hard into coastal lands in the first two or three rounds, before Towns fill those regions, bottles the pipeline at its source: a land you control there generates no new Invaders, so the interior stays clean, Dahan stay alive, and you buy breathing room to build toward Fear or your board-clear win condition. Fighting Invaders once they're already inland is fighting a tide that keeps refreshing — the Invader deck doesn't run out of cards, so you can't simply out-kill new landings appearing behind you. Coastal presence is easy to extend inland later and very hard to reclaim once Towns have built up on it, so the early rounds are when this decision actually gets made.

Fast vs. Slow Power Timing

Every power card resolves either Fast (before the Invader phase) or Slow (after it), and mixing the two up is repeatedly flagged as the single most common, most game-losing rules error new tables make. A Slow "prevent this Ravage" effect does nothing useful against a Ravage that already happened before it resolved; Slow damage kills pieces that already did their harm. The practical framework: if you need to stop a Ravage happening this round, you need a Fast Defend or Fast Damage effect — nothing Slow will do it. If you're setting up for next round, Slow is completely fine, since it resolves before the next Invader phase anyway. Fear generation is largely timing-agnostic either way, since the track advances independent of when in the round it happens. Read the speed icon before committing to a card, every time.

Fear Is a Win Condition to Pursue Deliberately, Not a Side Effect

Spirit Island has two parallel win paths: clear the board of Invaders, or advance the Fear track until Fear itself ends the game. New tables fixate on board state and barely track Fear, leaving a genuinely faster win condition unused. The Fear track has three stages — early on you need to clear the board completely, but once enough Fear accumulates the threshold drops to just towns and cities, and at the final stage the next Fear card drawn wins outright. Destroying towns and cities generates Fear as well as clearing them, and it's an easy thing to forget to track amid everything else going on — do it every time. A power dealing 2 damage and 2 Fear is very often the stronger play over one dealing 4 damage and no Fear, because it's progress on two win conditions at once instead of one. Ask each round what Fear you actually generated; zero or one is a sign the win condition is being left on the table. Fear cards themselves also have multiple tiers of effect printed on them, unlocked as Terror Level rises — read the tier that's currently active, not just the top-listed effect out of habit, or you'll use a weaker effect when a stronger one already applies. For solo play especially, leaning into Fear generation as the primary win condition rather than a nice-to-have is a strong, viable path — it lets a spirit with weak direct-damage tools still close out a game on schedule.

Innate Powers Are Worth Building Around

A spirit's innate power is its strongest, most distinctive tool, but it activates only once you've accumulated the right elements from cards you've played — it is not available turn one, and needs genuine presence and card development first. Early turns should be spent partly in service of feeding the innate's element requirements, not purely chasing the flashiest available effect. It's also an easy thing to actively forget exists once several other systems are being juggled at once — check the innate's requirements and effects at the top of every round, don't let it sit unused because you've stopped thinking about it. The same goes for a spirit's other unique special rule, printed separately from its powers — it's just as easy to let it lapse from memory mid-game, and just as costly to do so.

Pick Cards for Elements as Often as for Effects

Roughly half of good card picks are driven by the elements printed on the card's left edge, not by how strong its printed effect reads. Innate and major-power thresholds need specific element combinations, and missing just one forces an extra card play later to make up the gap — which pulls another card off your hand and delays everything downstream. When choosing between a flashier card and one with a needed element, the element-bearing card is very often the correct pick even though it looks like the "boring" choice on the page.

Let the Board Tell You What's Worth Defending

Not every land is worth spending a card on. A land with no Dahan and no Blight already on it is cheap to sacrifice — building it up to survive a Ravage often costs more in cards and Presence than the loss is worth, so let it go and spend that resource where it matters. Dahan can also defend a land without your help: two or three Dahan sitting on a land facing a single Explorer is typically self-sufficient — the Explorer builds, the Ravage kills a Dahan, and the surviving Dahan destroy the resulting Town and any remaining invaders on their own. If you don't have the cards or tempo to answer an Explorer site and the Blight risk isn't urgent, let the Dahan handle it and spend your actions elsewhere. Recognizing when a land defends itself is as important a skill as knowing how to defend one directly.

Reclaiming Cards Is Its Own Engine

Getting Presence off the card-plays track early both uncovers a spirit's reclaim ability and refills the hand — it's a growth priority in its own right, not just a byproduct of spreading. Reclaim lets key cards come back into hand repeatedly rather than being played once and discarded for the game, and for some spirits this becomes a genuine closing strategy: reclaiming the same card or two over and over just to keep firing an innate or a strong repeatable effect until the win condition is met. How efficient this loop is varies a lot by spirit — some have cheap, fast reclaim access that makes it excellent; for others the reclaim cost eats too much of the benefit to lean on it this hard.

Closing Out the Game: Three Distinct Paths

Beyond simply clearing the board as you go, there are a few recognizable ways a game actually ends. Repeatedly reclaiming a card to keep re-firing an innate power can carry a game to the finish on its own once the loop is set up. Picking up a major power can also close a game out, but it's costly to play and acquiring one means forgetting an existing power to make room — protect your starting powers when doing this, since they were chosen for a reason and usually carry the elemental spread your innate depends on; forget a minor power picked up along the way instead, or a power already sitting in your discard pile if you want to keep your full hand intact. Racing the Fear deck to empty is a third, fully legitimate path to a win, viable even when board control is slipping — the focus there is destroying towns and cities for the Fear they generate and keeping the Blight pool from running dry, but it has its own clock: stall too long and running out of Invader cards to explore with is a loss condition in itself, so it isn't a strategy to drift into passively.

Don't Undervalue Push and Gather Effects

Cards that push or gather Explorers or Dahan look weak next to flashier damage, Fear, or transform effects, but they're doing real work: moving Dahan out of a Ravage's path, or pulling Invaders out of a region entirely before they can Build or Ravage there. This is board-state control, not raw card-vs-card value, and it's easy to dismiss on a first read because the effect looks unglamorous. Weigh these against what they prevent, not just what they deal.

Energy Is the Real Bottleneck, Not Card Count

A hand full of strong options is worthless if there's no energy to pay for them — a spirit spent down to zero energy is effectively unable to act that round, and it's an easy mistake to make while chasing every good-looking play instead of maintaining a buffer. Energy also doesn't reset between rounds — it banks and carries forward — so treating it as a use-it-or-lose-it resource and playing too conservatively out of a mistaken belief it resets is its own trap. Track energy as deliberately as board threats, and when a growth choice trades Presence or cards for energy, weigh whether you're currently short on options or short on the energy to use the options you already have.

Fast Spirits, Slow Spirits, and Why Neither Is Strictly Better

Some spirits come online almost immediately and hit hard from round one; others are deliberately slow, spending early rounds building a board-wide engine that only pays off from the midgame on. Neither shape is strictly stronger — a fast spirit buys time for a slow spirit to ramp in multiplayer, and playing solo, a slow spirit's eventual ceiling can outscale what a fast spirit sustains on its own once the engine is actually running. Strength is also matchup-dependent rather than a single ranking: a spirit that struggles to damage Explorers directly will have a much harder time against an Explorer-heavy adversary than the same spirit would against a different threat profile, and a spirit built around powerful mid-island effects can find itself doing very little once Invaders have already pushed past the coast. Judge a spirit's power level against the threats actually on the table, not in the abstract.

Match Your Risk Tolerance to the Difficulty You're Playing

At low difficulty, an abandoned land with no Dahan and no Blight typically won't Ravage again for several rounds — that's real, affordable room to let things go and spend tempo building an engine instead of firefighting everywhere. At higher difficulty, escalation is faster and cascading Blight punishes the exact same "let it go" calls that were free at lower levels. The game's own difficulty ladder exists for this reason: the shared advice across new-player accounts is to play the introductory setup first — no Adversary, no escalation-variant Blight cards, no special scenarios — win at that level a couple of times, then add the first Adversary level rather than everything at once. The learning curve is real, but it's finite, and skipping straight to harder modifiers mainly means relearning the same lessons under more punishing conditions.

Expect a Hard Midgame — That's the Shape of the Game, Not a Sign You're Losing

Games follow a predictable arc: Invaders are ahead for the first several rounds, escalating steadily, while your own growth — more Presence, more card plays, more energy, more cards in hand — escalates in parallel and, if you survive the crunch, eventually escalates faster. The hardest stretch is that mid-game collision, not the opening or the endgame; panicking and overcommitting resources to prevent early damage that isn't actually fatal just burns the tempo you need to reach the point where your engine outpaces theirs. Recognizing that the midgame is supposed to feel tight is itself a useful piece of judgment, not just an incidental observation.

Talk Before You Plan, Every Round

Before committing to growth and card plays, share three things each round: what threats each of you actually sees (name specific lands and the specific Build or Ravage incoming), what each of you can cover this round, and — the important one — what's not covered, so the table can decide together whether to redirect or knowingly accept that Blight. The common failure mode isn't one spirit playing badly; it's everyone individually playing fine while covering the same lands and leaving several others undefended, because coordination broke down at the planning stage, not in execution. Two minutes of talk before each round feels slow and reliably wins games multiplayer loses without it. Playing with more than two spirits at once as a beginner multiplies this coordination burden fast, which is its own reason to start small.

Keep Physical State Visible

Recurring effects, ongoing conditions, and Blight totals are easy to lose track of amid whatever flashy play is coming up next — use reminder tokens for anything ongoing, and get in the habit of checking the Blight track deliberately rather than trusting memory. This is table-management discipline more than strategy, but games are lost to forgotten state as often as to bad decisions.

Identity: acts fast and hits hard, then has to recharge. Lightning doesn't grind invaders down turn after turn — it periodically detonates a Town or City with its innate, Thundering Destruction, then spends a turn or two rebuilding the fuel for the next blast. Solo, that rhythm is the whole game: a handful of sacred sites let one activation reach almost anywhere on the island, so the job is deciding when to strike and when to hold the charge.

Core Strategy: the Engine

Lightning's limiting resource isn't energy or presence — it's cards. Its card-play track is the fastest-growing and eventually highest in the game (up to 6 plays/turn, more than any other spirit), but nothing outside Reclaim refills the hand, so most turns you have more plays available than cards worth spending them on. That inverts the usual pick-a-power logic: when gaining new cards, energy cost and element (Fire, then Air, then Water) matter far more than the printed effect, because a card you can't afford or can't fuel the innate with won't get played regardless of how strong its text is. Favor cheap Fire/Air minors over Majors almost by default — a Major you can't reliably fund is worse than a stack of cheap cards you can always play.

The energy track and card-play track are badly mismatched: energy grows slowly (mid-pack) while card plays grow fast (best in the game), so over-investing in either one creates waste — too much energy with nothing to spend it on, or too many plays with no cards/energy to fill them. In practice this means swapping between the two growth branches rather than committing hard to either, and rarely needing the full 5-6 plays a turn since you'll usually be short on cards or energy to use them anyway. Reclaiming refills the hand but adds no presence to the board — since Lightning tends to empty its hand every big turn, it's easy to fall into a loop of reclaim-heavy turns that leave presence stalled. Sacrificing an early turn's output to grow presence instead is often worth it precisely to avoid getting stuck there.

Presence placement should be planned around sacred-site coverage from the start: the innate and Shatter Homesteads both need a site within range, so the goal is two sacred sites positioned to jointly reach every land on the board (on a 3-player island, 3 sites give full range-2 coverage regardless of starting position). A useful trick is a double-growth play — place the first new presence somewhere distant, then stack the second presence on top of it — to found a far-flung sacred site in one move rather than two. Get this coverage locked in early: once you're cycling through reclaim turns, opportunities to add new sacred sites dry up, and a gap in range can't easily be patched later.

Growth-Track Priorities

There's no single correct growth line — three broad shapes work, and which fits depends on temperament as much as board state. A destroyer-of-towns line minimizes presence growth and reclaims heavily to destroy two towns almost every turn — the highest sustained board impact and the best at keeping your area permanently clear, but it's slower to grow and leans on drawing a cheap Fire card early. A stable card-play line banks energy and empties the hand before reclaiming, building a comfortable rhythm at the cost of the innate not coming fully online until around turn 5 — lower ceiling, much more forgiving, and the better starting point if the swingy version feels too fiddly. A riding-the-storm line deliberately alternates weak one- or two-card turns with explosive four-to-five-card turns, banking presence and sacred sites during the quiet turns; it's the most destructive over a full game and picks up the most sacred sites for reach, but only works if you're comfortable with turns that look like you're doing nothing.

Key Powers & Combos

  • Swiftness of Lightning (special rule): every Air element among your played cards lets one Slow power resolve Fast instead. All four starting cards carry Air, so early on you can usually fast everything — but don't default to it. A Slow Shatter Homesteads timed to hit a town right after it builds (instead of one that's been sitting idle) often destroys more value than the same card played Fast on an emptier target. Reading whether a target is about to get more dangerous — a town about to become a city, explorers about to build — is the core skill in piloting Lightning well.
  • Shatter Homesteads is the workhorse starting card: 1 Fear plus a destroyed Town, at long enough range that one sacred site usually covers it in a 1-2 player game.
  • Harbingers of Lightning costs 0 energy and pushes 2 Dahan, generating Fear whenever a Dahan lands on a Town/City — with a full card-play budget, it's cheap enough to throw out almost every turn. Its strongest use: push Dahan into a land where a Town was just shattered and a lone Explorer remains, so the Dahan mop up the survivor before the Ravage does nothing (the town's already gone). It also works to push Dahan into a land another spirit is already defending, or to stack Dahan into a land about to Ravage for extra damage at the cost of losing one Dahan to counter-fire.
  • Raging Storm (1 damage to all invaders in a land) looks strong but injured invaders heal at end of turn, so alone it rarely finishes anything — it doesn't combo with any of Lightning's own other starting powers. Its real value is as a setup piece for another spirit's follow-up damage, or played Fast to wipe a land that only has Explorers present, denying that turn's Build outright.
  • Lightning's Boon (target spirit plays 2 powers Fast) is weak in solo play, since Lightning can usually already Fast everything itself via its own Air elements — save it for multiplayer, where it can bail out an ally stuck with a Slow power that needs to land before Ravage (e.g. rescuing another spirit's damage-prevention play that would otherwise resolve too late).
  • Gather and Push combos well late-game: pull a Town into a City's land so one innate activation levels both at once.
  • Letting a city sit and keep building before striking it means a bigger kill and proportionally more Fear when the hammer finally falls — patience compounds Lightning's damage instead of just delaying it.

Common Mistakes

  • Defaulting every power to Fast just because Swiftness of Lightning makes it easy — a Slow power that resolves after invaders act often destroys more (a town that's finished building, a stack that's grown) than the same power played Fast on a smaller target.
  • Over-growing the card-play track without the energy or cards to actually fill it, or over-growing energy with no card plays to spend it on — either one wastes the turn it was banked on.
  • Treating card effect as the primary factor when picking new powers — for Lightning, cost and Fire/Air elements matter more than what the card actually does, since most cards exist to fund the innate, not to be read for their text.
  • Chasing Thundering Destruction's top tier (needs 5 Fire + 5 Air + 2 Water, essentially every starting card plus a rare pickup) when it's rarely needed — three stacked cities in one land is uncommon, so the level-1/level-2 tiers do the real work most games.
  • Ignoring sacred-site range planning until it becomes a problem — once stuck in a reclaim loop, there's little opportunity left to add new sites.

Weaknesses & Pairings

Lone Explorers are Lightning's blind spot: nothing in its kit reliably snipes scattered Explorers before they build, and it can't push invaders directly except via Harbingers' Dahan push. Left unsupported it will accumulate Blight from stragglers even while crushing every Town and City in reach. It also has almost no way to gain extra cards outside Reclaim, so any ally or power that hands out bonus power cards (Gift of Minor Power, or allies like Keeper of the Forbidden Wilds and Serpent Slumbering Beneath the Island) is worth far more to Lightning than to most other spirits, since it directly loosens its tightest constraint.

Aspects

Immense doubles Energy gained per presence placed but halves card plays (rounded down), which sounds like it should push toward fewer, bigger Majors instead of Lightning's normal card-spam plan. In practice the fit is awkward: base Lightning is already one of the worst spirits at gaining new cards, so under Immense the first Major isn't available until Reclaim (turn 3 at the earliest) and can't be played again until turn 5 — where Major-focused spirits elsewhere can pick one up by turn 2 and keep playing roughly one per turn from there. Every Major gained also costs forgetting a card, and Lightning has few ways to stack Minors back up, so the hand caps out small — and every starting Fire/Air card forgotten to make room pushes the innate threshold further out of reach. Net effect: Immense is a real change of pace from base Lightning, trading its normal quick-cycling rhythm for a slower, more Energy-rich build, but it doesn't automatically turn into a smooth Major-toolbox spirit the way the aspect's numbers might suggest — expect some friction reconciling Immense's incentives with Lightning's naturally poor card economy.

Lightning's overall reputation as an easy, low-decision-making spirit to pilot is earned in the base game — the card-selection logic barely depends on reading card text, and a fixed sequence of "grow energy, grow card plays, reclaim, repeat" wins comfortably against easier opposition. The real skill ceiling is in Fast/Slow timing and knowing when a weak-looking turn is actually banking a bigger one.

Its special rule, Choke the Land with Green, lets it sacrifice its own presence to cancel an incoming Build or Ravage — and that presence comes back later through growth, so nothing is really lost. Combined with an innate that both damages buildings and defends a land, Green becomes the spirit that keeps three, four, even six separate lands from spiraling into Blight in a single turn, using small, cheap interventions rather than one big swing. Core Strategy: Juggle the Invaders, Don't Clear Them

The instinct to keep every land spotless is the biggest trap in playing Green. You don't need to. Choke the Land only needs to stop the Ravage — a Build can often be left alone, since a town or city sitting on a land you're not going to Ravage-block does nothing on its own. Deliberately picking 1-4 lands (a Jungle or Wetland is the easiest first pick) to let build up indefinitely, while reliably canceling their Ravage every time, costs you almost nothing: about one presence every few turns to restock what Choke spent. Everything you're not spending there is freed up to work the rest of the island.

The real skill is never spending two separate powers on the same land in the same turn. If Choke the Land already stopped that land's Build, don't also burn a Defend on it to stop the Ravage too — that's double-paying for one problem. Work out, per land, whether the Build or the Ravage is the cheaper one to stop: if the invaders already present can't cause Blight even if the Ravage lands, let the Build happen and save your presence for a land that actually needs it. If Blight is otherwise unavoidable, spend on preventing the Ravage instead. This flips the usual "stopping a Build beats stopping a Ravage" logic on its head specifically for Green, because Choke costs the same presence either way — spend it on whichever prevents the actual damage.

Think of a turn's work as juggling, not eliminating. "Dealt with" for Green just means a land won't cause a problem again until that terrain type resurfaces in explore — not that it's been cleared. A strong turn touches four to six lands: two big Ravages stopped, a land defended so Dahan mop up the invaders there, a new town destroyed outright — all through some mix of card plays, innate triggers, and Choke. That kind of multi-tasking, more than any single power, is what makes Green one of the strongest spirits in the game when played well. Start with one land you're consciously juggling while you learn the rhythm, then add more as the bookkeeping becomes comfortable — trying to track six lands' worth of plans before you're ready is how the spirit falls apart in practice.

Where a land has both an Explorer and Dahan present, look for the free option: Choke the Build and let the Dahan fight the Explorer themselves, or use the innate's Defend to protect the land and Dahan and let their counter-attack clear the invaders — either way you spend nothing extra on damage.

The Choke the Land Math Problem

Choke's real complication only shows up with Slow powers that need a Sacred Site to fire, because Choke removes presence before your other powers get to target with it. Fast powers are never a problem — their effect resolves before you'd sacrifice anything. But a Slow power planned for a Sacred Site can get quietly sabotaged if you Choke away the very presence that made it sacred.

Two habits prevent this. First, stack to three: more than two presence in a land is never wasted with Green, so deliberately over-place in built-up, troublesome lands to keep sacrifice fodder on hand without cannibalizing a Sacred Site you still need. Second, use a physical reminder (a token, a note) on any Sacred Site you've earmarked for a Slow power that turn — it forces a check before you Choke and saves you from wrecking your own plan mid-turn.

Support and Fear-Infrastructure Identity

Starting the Game

Unlike most spirits, which have two or more reasonable openers, Bringer has essentially one strong line: race to unlock the Moon element on the energy track by turn 3 (turn 4 is acceptable if card luck or board state demands it), because both innate powers depend on it and unlocking it early lets you start firing them every turn from then on. Early growth should default to gaining a card and spreading presence; if turn 3 arrives without the right elements, reclaiming one card to drop a second Sacred Site instead is an acceptable fallback, though it costs some Fear-bonus consistency from insufficient spread.

Sources differ on how fast to chase this. One approach prioritizes getting a first Major power online as early as turn 2 — using the Call on Midnight's Dreams growth-and-forget line to convert a big Dahan-fueled energy swing straight into an immediate cast — trading a slightly messier board for a Major a turn sooner. A more patient approach delays that same play to turn 3, spending turn 2 instead on consolidating three Dahan into one land and stacking Dread Apparitions with Night Terrors to wipe an entire Explorer-Town-City stack in a single turn, accepting a turn's worth of unprevented Blight in exchange for a cleaner, better-defended board when the Major finally lands. Neither is wrong: reach for the faster line when you need a Major's raw power online quickly (long game, weak draws elsewhere), and the patient line when your board can tolerate one messy turn in exchange for a much safer platform to build from.

Once Moon is secured, the next decision is two more growth steps to unlock "any element" versus three steps to a third card play. The any-element path is generally preferred since it helps qualify for Major power thresholds; the third-card-play route is only worth it if the game looks to run long. Fear Card Management: the Core Skill

Bringer's innate power lets it flip any face-down Fear card — not just the top of the deck, and not limited to cards it has already earned — face up, giving full knowledge of what that card will do before it triggers. Learning to use this well is arguably the single biggest lever on how much value the spirit generates. Aim to earn at least one Fear card every turn, so that once your Fear generation outpaces one card per round, cards are still getting flipped face-up rather than piling up hidden.

The subtlety is in timing: when a newly-earned Fear card would activate the very same turn you flip it, there's no time left to plan around the information, so it's often better to leave that one face-down and instead flip a different card whose effect you can actually prepare for next round. In multiplayer, flip Fear cards before resolving damage-dealing powers where possible, so the whole table can adapt to what's coming — for instance, skipping a defend on a coastal land once a flipped card reveals it won't Ravage this round.

Growth-Track Priorities

The defining early tension is that Thunderspeaker's starting hand costs around 6 energy against a weak early income track, and new cards only arrive via Reclaim — so the opening few turns are entirely about which resource to starve first to get the engine moving. No opening line gets everything fast; each is a different trade of tempo against smoothness, and which one is correct depends on the board and the adversary rather than any fixed default.

An energy-first line takes the early +4-energy growth options to fund the starting hand, accepting that the following Reclaim turn is thin (few cards, little energy) before a second energy push restores full strength — this is the smoothest curve but the slowest to first big turn. A double-presence line instead takes +2 presence whenever not reclaiming, using the innate's Dahan-gather-and-push every non-reclaim turn to fight small skirmishes immediately (an Explorer here, a won Ravage there) while still reaching a strong Reclaim by turn 3-4 — rougher on energy, but the army starts working sooner and the spirit is already ahead on the board by the time it consolidates.

A top-track-heavy line (favoring the +1-energy/+1-card-play growth options over presence) is deliberately weak on turns 1-2 — it can't afford much of the starting hand early and doesn't reach 3 card plays until turn 5 or so — but it pays off with strong Energy and both 4-Air/4-Sun element thresholds unlocked by mid-game, and reaching Reclaim-1 very late is an acceptable cost against adversaries that don't ramp up quickly. Favor this shape specifically against slower adversaries (England is the standout matchup) where there's time to let the long game develop.

A full-bottom-track line instead leans almost entirely on the bottom-track presence-and-energy growth option every turn it isn't reclaiming, reaching Reclaim-1 earliest of any line (as soon as turn 3-4) and ending with the highest card-play count of any opening — but no elements land on the presence track this way, which starves the innate thresholds and delays the 4-Air/4-Sun element spikes to very late in the game. Favor this shape when raw card-play volume and an early second Reclaim matter more than early innate access — for instance when the starting hand's elements alone are enough to limp through the first several turns.

A hybrid line splits the difference: heavier bottom-track growth turn 1 to fund playing two of the four unique power cards immediately, then top-track growth turn 2 to land the rest, reaching 3 card plays as early as turn 4-5 while still hitting 4 Air by turn 5 (though 4 Sun lags to turn 7). This is a reasonable default when no adversary-specific read pushes toward one of the more extreme lines above.

Whichever line is chosen, turn 1's card choice is a live read of the board, not a script: the "best" pair of unique powers on paper is worse than a weaker pair that actually has a target.   • Forcing innate element thresholds every single turn at the cost of raw damage output — many turns simply won't have a good target for every threshold, and that's fine; slow, well-timed turns beat rushed ones.
  • Engaging small skirmishes personally instead of delegating them to allies or leftover scattered Dahan.
  • Being overly precious with Dahan survival to the point of passing up a strong trade — losing Dahan mid-massacre is normal, not a sign of misplay.

Pairings: works especially well with Dahan-generating spirits (River Surges in Sunlight, Fractured Days Split the Sky) and Dahan-movers (Bringer of Dreams and Nightmares, Shadows Flicker Like Flame), or defensive spirits like Vital Strength of the Earth that hold ground it isn't built to hold.

Recap: mass Dahan and presence for one decisive strike, then move on rather than garrison; pick an opening line (energy-first, double-presence, top-track, full-bottom-track, or hybrid) based on adversary speed and board read rather than by default; read turn 1's card pair against the actual board instead of a fixed script; and accept Dahan losses in exchange for the carnage this spirit is built to deliver.

Identity: roll in with the tide, destroy everything reachable, roll back out. Presence placed in the ocean makes that board's water count as coastal land, letting powers reach it and pieces be pushed into it. Drowning — the special rule — destroys any invader piece that enters an ocean where Ocean has presence, trading its HP for energy at a fixed rate (HP needed per energy scales with player count), and only pieces destroyed this way pay out; damage from other sources doesn't feed the loop. That's the whole engine in one sentence: the Ocean hungers, and feeding it is the entire strategy.

Core Strategy: the Drowning Loop

Ocean starts with almost no passive income, so early energy is thin and every setback compounds — low energy means fewer drowns, fewer drowns mean less energy, and the spiral runs in both directions. The way out is treating every drowned invader as a fuel deposit rather than just a kill: pushing a coastal invader into the ocean beats destroying it outright even when another spirit could do the killing directly, because only the drown pays Ocean back. Once the loop catches, it steamrolls — more drowning buys more energy buys more cards buys more drowning.

The tradeoff is reach. Ocean's powers are mostly range 0 and slow, and its only presence expansion goes through water, so it has essentially no way to touch inland lands on its own. It is a finisher, not a controller: it rarely lacks targets, since invaders always land on a shore eventually, but it depends on allies or long-range minors to deal with anything that moves inland before the tide can reach it.

Opening: Building the Coast

Of the two innates, prioritize enabling Ocean Breaks the Shore over Pound Ships to Splinters — its tier 2 already converts a coastal city straight to energy plus fear, while Pound Ships' tier 2 is fear only. It hits that tier as early as turn 2 with the right Water+Earth elements, so early minor and card choices should bend toward clearing that threshold rather than building for Pound Ships at all.

Two viable turn-1 shapes exist. The self-reliant line takes the growth option that adds energy-track presence and spreads across as many boards as possible, banking a single energy rather than chasing drowned HP immediately; it plays Call of the Deeps to deny builds (ideally on an ally's board, since Ocean's own coast rarely needs the help) and leans on turn 2's Tidal Boon plus Swallow the Land-Dwellers to net a full city's worth of drowned HP regardless of what allies do that turn. The card-play-first line instead takes presence off the card track, which only pays off with an early energy gift from an ally, strong card luck, or a coastal town worth fighting for turn 1 — it forgoes some of Breaking the Shore's turn-1 potential in exchange for Pound Ships coming online sooner, and is slower on average even though a lucky draw makes it snowball hard.

A more scripted version of the same idea: set up in a coastal land that already holds a town, guaranteeing Ocean Breaks the Shore a turn-1 target since it can't reach anywhere else yet. Growth goes to two ocean presence plus an energy tick, which funds Tidal Boon — tell allies up front that moving invaders into your ocean is free energy for you. Call of the Deeps is worth holding for board D's wetlands explore or similar double-explorer setups, and notably can gather an inland invader as long as it still resolves on a coastal land. Turn 2 doubles up on range-0 drowning powers (Swallow the Land-Dwellers plus the upgraded innate), so presence placement that turn needs care: keep Swallow off any land with your own Dahan, since it drowns them too, and use the range-1 Grasping Tide to defend a land where Dahan can land a counter-kill instead. In 4-player games, push-then-place onto a land adjacent to the fourth board turn 2 to get presence everywhere at once, accepting a smaller reclaim later; in 2-3 player games, place the new presence off your starting board instead so one non-ocean presence survives reclaim there.

Reclaim is the recurring cost of this engine: it forces one presence back into each ocean, which can undercut whichever lands you actually wanted to act on that turn. Plan a turn or two ahead for which presence needs to stay put versus which can be sacrificed to reclaim, and expect to fall into a rhythm of pushing out on even turns and reclaiming on odd ones — presence that just pools in the ocean without being pushed back out stalls the whole engine.

Key Powers & Combos

  • Swallow the Land-Dwellers drowns an Explorer, Town, and Dahan together — devastating, but never target a land holding your own Dahan.
  • Tidal Boon hands an ally +2 energy and pushes a town plus Dahan — the standard way to repay an ally who's been feeding you energy, and doubles as inland reach when an ally uses it to push a target onto your land instead.
  • Call of the Deeps (Fast) gathers explorers into the ocean, denying builds before they happen; it's the one starting power that can reach inland, since the invader it gathers can originate anywhere as long as it lands in your ocean.
  • Grasping Tide is range 1 and easier to aim than the range-0 powers — best spent defending a land where Dahan can finish invaders off.

Minor & Major Power Priority

When drafting minors, rank Water+Earth combo powers first (always take), then push/gather effects (more feed for the loop), then long-range powers, then anything else with a useful Earth element. Long-range minors matter more in smaller player counts, where inland control is otherwise unreachable; in bigger games the coast alone is enough work to fill a turn. Entwined Power is an exception worth chasing even though none of Ocean's own cards synergize with it — it opens the door to strong majors once acquired.

Majors need to be funded by other powers, since they don't generate drowned HP themselves. Favor support and reach majors over pure damage ones — Ocean's innate already clears the shore of major threats on its own, so a big short-range damage major frequently has nothing left to hit. The exception is a major with serious inland reach, since that's the one thing minors and the innate structurally can't provide. Terrifying Nightmares is a standout pick: cheap fear plus a genuinely useful push.

Common Mistakes

  • Building toward Pound Ships to Splinters instead of Ocean Breaks the Shore — the fear-only payoff doesn't compare.
  • Using Swallow the Land-Dwellers on a Dahan-occupied land and killing an ally for nothing.
  • Placing two range-0 drowning powers on the same coastal land instead of spreading them across two, wasting reach.
  • Letting presence pool permanently in the ocean instead of pushing it back out as fast as it accumulates.
  • Only clearing your own coast and ignoring everyone else's — a self-focused Ocean ends up bored with a clean board while allies drown (figuratively) inland.
  • Panicking over a lone town sitting unaddressed on your coast — as long as blight isn't cascading, it's often better bait: let it re-explore, then Tidal Boon plus Swallow the Land-Dwellers nets a full city's worth of HP in one turn anyway.

Multi-Board Presence & Teamwork

Ocean's real ceiling is reached by shutting down other boards' coasts, not just its own — it doesn't need allies to function (invaders always land somewhere coastal) but it multiplies hard when an ally reliably pushes invaders its way. Dropping a single presence into an ally's ocean is often enough to let a strong pusher (River Surges in Sunlight is the standout) generate free drowned HP for you with no further coordination needed. Watch for slow, range-0 powers getting outraced by a Ravage — think a step ahead and hit invaders while they're still exploring or during a non-acting invader phase, not right before they're about to ravage and destroy the presence you needed to punish them.

Its one real weakness is range — plan around slow, short-reach powers by hitting invaders early, and lean on allies or long-range minors for anything that gets inland before the tide does.

The Engine: Massive Flooding

All four starting powers share Sun/Water elements, and Massive Flooding scales in three tiers off exactly those elements: tier 1 pushes a town or explorer, tier 2 adds 2 damage plus a push, tier 3 deals 2 damage to every invader in a land within range 1 of a shrine. That top tier is what River strives to unlock every turn — its land-clearing effectiveness is high enough that thresholding it every turn is usually worth prioritizing over gaining major power cards at all. Major powers exist mainly to handle lands with too many invaders for minors to manage efficiently, but River can already handle those via top-tier Massive Flooding, so stacking minors/card plays into the innate generally beats chasing majors.

Getting there needs card plays, not just elements: 4 unlocked card-play slots from the bottom track are the minimum to threshold the top tier every turn regardless of which minors get drawn. A standard sequence: 2 presence turn 1, 2 presence turn 2 (this unlocks Reclaim plus enough plays to hit tier 2 that same turn), a minor power turn 3, 1 more presence plus a minor turn 4, then Reclaim loop from turn 5 on — at that point all 4 starting cards played together reliably hit tier 3 regardless of draws. Pulling presence onto the energy track instead around turn 5 is tempting but usually a trap: max Massive Flooding needs your full starting hand played, and a mixed top/bottom split leaves too few card slots to spend the extra energy on anything, so it just banks uselessly. The only good reason to split off for energy is deliberately chasing a major power a turn or two earlier, and even then it delays reliable tier 3 unless the draws cooperate.

Wetland Presence & Shrines

River's special rule: a single presence in a Wetland counts as a shrine on its own, where every other spirit needs two presence in a land to get one. Prioritize Wetland placements over other land types whenever the choice exists — cheap shrines mean cheap range for the innate, and on multi-board games stacking Wetland shrines is one of the biggest reasons River scales well into bigger player counts. It also matters for sacred-site hunting more broadly: expanding into wetlands is the fastest way to rack up sacred sites for anything that cares about them.

Growth-Track Priority

Card plays first, energy second. Complete the bottom (card-play) track before the top — it unlocks Reclaim and gets the innate online fastest, and minor power picks should favor minors over majors whenever the growth option offers a choice between them. Element priorities on those minors: Water and Sun matter most (feeding the innate directly), Earth is a useful tertiary for tier 3, and beyond that the color drawn is fairly irrelevant unless hunting a specific major — Vigor of the Breaking Dawn (Animal) and The Trees and Stones Speak of War (Plant) are both cheap, strong majors worth bending a pick toward; Cast Down Into the Briny Deep (Moon) is excellent at threshold but generally out of reach for River without 9+ banked energy.

Energy runs tight because the innate's top tier costs a full hand's worth of Sun/Water/Earth. Play both River's Bounty and Boon of Vigor whenever reasonably possible to keep the reclaim loop funded — River's Bounty is almost always the correct reclaim target since it both consolidates Dahan and refills energy simultaneously; the one exception is a teammate sitting energy-starved, where reclaiming Boon of Vigor instead to subsidize their turn can be the better team play. If a turn's minor power picks both cost energy, spend the reclaim slot on River's Bounty those turns specifically to make sure the big tier-3 turn is affordable; if either minor is free, there's more slack to work with.

Key Powers & Combos

  • Wash Away — best spent on a land invaders just explored into that started as a town: left alone it becomes a city next turn and shrugs off most early interrupts, but Wash Away defuses it in one power before it locks in. Failing that ideal target, it plays like a mini Massive Flooding: push a town off a land about to ravage (leaves a weaker explorer, ideally to die to Dahan) or push an explorer off a land about to build, which is the more efficient use since it prevents the town from ever existing.
  • Flash Floods hits harder on the coast — 2 damage there versus 1 inland — making it the go-to for destroying a coastal town outright, finishing off a weakened city alongside tier-3 Massive Flooding, or stopping a build the same way Wash Away does. It's also the standard power to let go once majors start rolling in and better-elemented cards take its place, since 2 energy for what it does becomes the weak link in the hand.
  • River's Bounty gathers Dahan into a River-presence land and, once that land holds 2+ Dahan, creates a bonus Dahan plus energy — aim it at a land another spirit is about to defend, or one that needs its Dahan protected from an incoming ravage. Range 0, so plan presence placement with this in mind.
  • Boon of Vigor is free to play and pays out per card an ally played that turn, making it the team's biggest single energy gift — but on itself or in solo play it still nets a flat return worth taking every turn the reclaim loop needs fuel.
  • Push invaders toward the coast generally — it stops them from exploring further inland and groups them up, both of which set up a bigger tier-3 hit later. Deliberately building a "kill zone" this way (pushing several invaders onto one land ahead of time) is one of the strongest patterns available once Wash Away and tier-1/2 Massive Flooding are both online.

Weaknesses & Judgment Calls

No starting defense — anything picked up here comes from minor draws, and Dahan counterattacks are the main fallback, more reliable solo than in a crowded multiplayer game. Fear generation is also thin outside a big tier-3 kill zone, which can spike fear hard but isn't a source to rely on turn after turn. Accepting blight on a land you can't get to in time is often correct rather than a failure — call it blight-to-scale: a land you couldn't save this turn is fine to lose if doing so advanced the growth track or set up a stronger follow-up play.

The Signature Trick: Ignoring Range

Every power can pay 1 energy to ignore its range requirement entirely — this only removes the numeric range limit, not other targeting restrictions like needing a sacred site. Rain of Blood, for example, normally needs a land with invaders within 1 of a sacred site; paying the energy turns that into any land on the map with both invaders and Dahan present, so long as at least one sacred site still exists anywhere. Effectively: infinite range, in exchange for adding a Dahan-presence requirement to the target land.

This is the whole reason Shadows scales as a support spirit — with a teammate willing to set up big Dahan-versus-invader fights anywhere on the board, Shadows can reach in and tip them from across the map. That scaling is tied to player count and map size: with more players and a bigger board there are more distant fights worth reaching, and pushing Dahan into key spots becomes someone else's job so Shadows is free to just hit things. Solo, this ability is nearly wasted — a single spirit's normal reach already covers almost every land that matters, so there's rarely a target the range-ignore actually unlocks. On small maps generally, don't force a Dahan-support build around this; pivot to explorer-hunting or a major-power-focused game instead.

The Engine: Darkness Swallows the Unwary

The innate is fast (resolves before invaders act), uses Moon/Fire/Air, and is fully cumulative — unlike most starting innates. Tier 1 gathers 1 explorer (enough to stop a land from building); tier 2 destroys up to 2 explorers and grants fear (stops 2 lands at once — this is the bread-and-butter tier, and where the fear actually lives); tier 3 adds 3 damage on top, which is powerful but a much harder threshold to sustain than tier 2. The restriction is range 1 of a sacred site, bypassable via the pay-1-energy trick through any Dahan-occupied land.

None of the four starting cards alone supplies the 2 Fire tier 2 needs — only Mantle of Dread and Crops Wither and Fade carry Fire, so hitting tier 2 early means playing both of them the same turn, which spends the entire opening hand on setup with nothing left to recast next turn without a reclaim or other power. Because of this, prioritize Fire (ideally with Moon and Air too) when picking up new minor powers, so the innate becomes castable without needing to burn the whole hand every time. Reaching tier 2 reliably and often is the goal to build the early game around; tier 3 is a bonus for when the elements cooperate, not something to chase at the expense of everything else.

Growth-Track Priority: Presence Is Slow, Everything Else Is Fast

Shadows places presence more slowly than almost any other spirit — only 1 per turn, and never while reclaiming — but its energy and card-play tracks ramp unusually fast: two of the cheapest growth options push all the way to 3 energy/turn and 3 card plays/turn, faster than all but three other spirits, and those three need to burn their 2-presence growth options to keep pace. The standard reclaim rhythm (turn 3, 5, 7) badly wastes this — it delays the fourth presence placement to turn 6 and leaves the fast tracks half-exploited in the meantime. The fix is to deliberately delay the first reclaim: lean on "gain power card" and "place presence" growth options through turns 1-3, and land the first reclaim-all around turn 4 with enough energy and plays banked to immediately deploy a major power or run a strong turn. Played on the generic reclaim schedule, Shadows underperforms; delaying the first reclaim is considerably stronger.

Within that delayed-reclaim shape, three different early games are all viable, and the right one depends on what's threatened turn 1 and what the hand looks like. Prioritizing an early second card play trades a slower reclaim for a stable base — useful when the innate or a Dahan-gathering play needs fueling right away. Rushing toward a big hand and 3 card plays sooner sacrifices a turn-1 play but sets up tier 2 (or with good luck, tier 3) Darkness Swallows earlier and nets more power cards over the same stretch — don't overcommit to chasing tier 3 specifically if the early elements aren't cooperating, since the tier-3 upgrade is only about 3 extra damage. Holding at one card play for two turns while banking energy toward an early major power is the third option, worth taking when the opening board looks safe (no immediate threat to a sacred site or a turn-1 build) — Shadows of the Dahan itself or Instruments of Their Own Ruin are common early-major targets this way. Which of the three is correct is a read on the board, not a fixed script — stay ready to switch mid-opening if the drawn cards or threat level change the calculus.

Dahan-Support Play

When leaning into the support role, judge cards by effect over element. In priority order: powers (ideally fast) that push Dahan toward invaders, since they extend which lands are reachable and set up ravages; powers (ideally fast) that gather Dahan and invaders into the same land, which doesn't extend reach but improves the odds of that fight; defend effects or anything that tips a ravage's outcome toward the Dahan; and only then, Moon/Fire elements for the innate. Entrancing Apparitions, Lure of the Unknown, Rites of the Land's Rejection, and Veil the Night's Hunt are all strong picks under this lens.

Fast powers resolve this turn's ravage; slow powers set up next turn's. The goal is converting two or three ravages per turn into clean wins — the innate already handles stopping builds, so everything else in the support game is about winning fights that are already going to happen rather than preventing them. Don't try to pull double duty by pushing invaders away to stall instead of fight — that's a different spirit's job, and trying to do both waters down what Shadows is actually good at. Don't worry about minimizing blight either; some blight is an acceptable cost of winning fights, and trying to prevent all of it slows the whole plan down. Always keep at least one sacred site — losing the last one shuts off the range-ignore trick entirely.

Card Judgment Calls

  • Mantle of Dread — target a land about to build, not one about to ravage; it's slow, so aiming it at an imminent ravage is already too late. Works especially well for an ally whose presence often sits with invaders (Earth, Thunderspeaker, Bringer of Dreams), less so for allies who stay distant.
  • Favors Called Due — gathers Dahan for a counterattack, and combos hard with anything that consolidates or protects Dahan, like River's Bounty. It's slow: the gathered Dahan won't arrive until after invaders act that turn, so targeting a land that ravages the same turn nets fear but no counterattack, and targeting a build-turn land still needs separate protection the following turn.
  • Crops Wither and Fade — a slow, 0-range power, best spent downgrading a city to a town (town-to-explorer still leaves explorers that ravage and blight next turn unless paired with another removal effect the same turn). Bypass its 0-range restriction with the pay-1-energy trick when needed.
  • Concealing Shadows — protects Dahan from ravage damage, but doesn't stop the land from blighting, and being 0-range means that blight can destroy your own presence. Often better used for its Fear and elements than its stated effect, unless paired with the range-ignore trick and a big Dahan counterattack to make the trade worthwhile.

Selecting Major Powers

Because the range-ignore trick already grants near-total reach, don't weight major powers by range or sacred-site access — instead avoid ones that would destroy Dahan or add a non-Dahan targeting restriction, since either undercuts the whole support game plan. The Core Skill: Beast Placement, Not Presence Placement

Nearly every presence Sharp Fangs gains should become a Beast rather than sit on the island as a token — the island itself typically only needs 2-3 presence to avoid an instant loss (4 if still using the Tipping Point deck). Beasts are the actual currency of the kit: they let presence go down outside Jungles, power both Innates, reposition presence around the board, and fuel Prey on the Builders and Terrifying Chase simultaneously.

Getting a Beast into a land before Ranging Hunt needs it is a large damage multiplier. A land that already holds a Beast can gather two more (one from presence conversion, one from the Gather threshold) for 3+ damage; a land starting at zero Beasts caps out at 1 damage that turn. This makes pre-seeding — placing Beasts in lands expected to matter next turn, rather than reacting once they're needed — the single highest-value habit in the deck.

Balance spread against concentration: generally scatter Beasts as widely as possible for flexibility, but stack them in a smaller set of lands when the board or Adversary calls for one big hit rather than several small ones (against England specifically, small Ranging Hunts barely matter — better to mass Beasts and swing hard in fewer lands). Also worth keeping at least one Beast sitting in any blighted land — it keeps Prey on the Builders and fresh presence placement available there whenever the land needs attention again.

The Engine: Ranging Hunt

Ranging Hunt converts Plant+Animal elements into damage, but unlike most starting Innates it doesn't scale up with extra elements past the threshold — once enough Plant+Animal is online to hit full damage, additional copies of those elements are wasted. That caps how much the elemental side of the kit is worth chasing: the real requirement is just one Animal minor power (to activate the Innate from turn 2 onward) and, failing that, one Plant minor instead. Past that first element card, keep drafting for function over color — high card-play counts and reliable access to reclaim already patch most remaining element gaps, so a blight-clearing minor is worth taking over a marginally-better-elemented one almost every time.

Ranging Hunt's movement clause (repositioning a presence during the fast phase) matters as much as its damage — it's what lets both uniques land on a range-0 board when the Invaders haven't cooperated by exploring into a jungle yet. Terrifying Chase is slow, which buys a second chance for the Invaders to coincidentally walk into a covered terrain before it must be committed.

Energy Is the Real Bottleneck

Sharp Fangs' growth is unusually flexible — the choice between +energy, gaining a card, and reclaiming can be swapped freely turn to turn regardless of what an opening "calls for," and it's normal to adjust on the fly once the value of an extra Power outweighs banked Energy, or vice versa. What doesn't flex is the underlying scarcity: energy income is thin all game, the track's 2-energy-per-turn spot takes many turns to reach, and the 3-energy growth option isn't reliably affordable until well into the midgame. Actively hunt for zero-cost Power cards for this reason — they're worth more here than in most other decks.

This also makes early top-track presence a trap. It looks like a helpful long-term investment, but by the time a top-track push would pay off, Ranging Hunt already has enough Plant+Animal for the rest of the game (since it can't use more), while every point of bottom-track presence keeps paying off turn after turn as an extra card play or reclaim slot. Bottom track beats top track in essentially every case except the rare turn where a specific Innate threshold is being chased directly.

Choosing an Opening Shape

Four distinct opening shapes are all viable, and the right one depends on matchup, appetite for reclaiming, and whether Ranging Hunt's damage needs to land every single turn or can be skipped occasionally in exchange for something else.

Top-track hybrid (minor-focused) takes presence from the top track early while gaining two Minors in the first two turns, holding reclaim back until turn 3 or later. It activates Ranging Hunt's damage from turn 2 onward (turn 1 too, if a Plant+Animal minor is drawn early) while maximizing the number of Powers seen early — valuable for finding blight-control or otherwise strong cards sooner. The cost is staying at only 2 card plays until turn 4 and going without reclaim until turn 5 at the earliest, plus tight energy through the opening. This is the shape to reach for against fast or aggressive Invader boards (Base game, Sweden, France, Scotland, Russia) where seeing more cards and staying flexible matters more than raw output. A guaranteed-turn-1 variant exists too: lean on the two starting uniques (Teeth Gleam and Too Near the Jungle) to force the elements immediately, at the cost of even less energy.

Full bottom track gives up on activating Ranging Hunt's damage every single turn and instead front-loads card plays and an early reclaim-one, reaching 3 card plays by turn 2 and 5 by turn 6. That volume of plays lets Prey on the Builders and Too Near the Jungle punish a build-heavy board relentlessly even without the Innate landing every turn — this is the shape to favor against build-focused Adversaries like England, Sweden, or Habsburg Livestock. It runs very tight on energy since so many cards are in play each turn, so treat any 1-cost minor with suspicion unless it's clearly strong.

Mixed hybrid with a Major follows the top-track hybrid's early shape but detours into a Major power (picked up turn 3, played turn 4) instead of a third Minor. This exists to patch Sharp Fangs' one real gap: nothing in the starting kit touches blighted lands except Terrifying Chase, and only when no City is present. Reach for this shape specifically when Ranging Hunt's raw damage looks like it won't be enough on its own — heavy Blight already on the island, or an Adversary that raises Invader health — or when the matchup calls for blight removal that Minors haven't supplied. The Squeeze: Weak Energy, Expensive Powers

Wildfire needs both Energy and cards to function and is short on both. Card economy discipline is the main lever for delaying reclaim: don't play a card unless it's actually needed, even if that means playing zero cards in the first turn or two. If the board's genuinely under control, though, reclaiming even with cards still in hand can be worth it, since it frees up presence-placement turns later on. Wildfire can already cover Uniques and even low-to-mid-cost Majors from bottom-track Growth plus Flame's Fury alone.

Element Priorities: Plant > Fire

Minor draft preference runs Fire+Plant > Plant alone > Fire alone. Solo and Multiplayer Are Different Spirits

All four of Serpent's Gift powers can augment its own card plays in solo play, so a solo Serpent behaves like a normal spirit optimizing its own board — the presence cap barely matters, and every Gift is self-directed value.

With 2 or more spirits on the board, three of those four Gifts must instead target an ally. That guarantees at least one turn of thin value purely from whichever Gift comes up first, since only one of the four keeps its benefit at home. A multiplayer Serpent opening isn't planned in isolation — it's planned around reading which Gift got drawn and which ally is positioned to extract the most from it that turn. The first-turn Gift alone can swing a turn from excellent to nearly wasted.

Two Skills Replace "Optimize My Own Board"

  • Predictive management of the presence cap. Largely irrelevant solo, but a real constraint with 2+ allies — their card plays and lent elements can accelerate Serpent's own waking faster than expected.   • Reading allies' reclaim cycles. Serpent's Gifts don't just give value, they reshape when allies reclaim. The general allocation heuristic: slower-tempo allies get more value from an extra card play, since their bottleneck is plays-per-turn; faster or more flexible allies get more value from element access, since their bottleneck is hitting thresholds. Past that heuristic, there's no fixed answer — note what Growth choices allies already made and improvise from there, since the same Gift can be worth wildly different amounts depending on what a given ally is sitting on that turn.

The Intensify Aspect

Intensify plays differently enough from base Memory to be a distinct build rather than a straight upgrade. The innate also has a secondary trick worth knowing: it moves your own Presence and then resolves its further effects from that relocated Presence, effectively extending your reach every turn — useful to teach explicitly since it's not obvious from the card text. Optimizing which of the four effects matters most for the one land you can afford to target is genuinely complex; it's better learned through reps than memorized as a formula.

Unique Powers

It covers a full Ravage stack or an awkward pile of Explorers and Towns, and its best use isn't just blocking Blight — it's Defending a land where Dahan are already massed so they can counterattack. Reflexively Defending every Blight-adding Ravage is a trap: Blight is a resource, and letting a Dahan-less land Ravage while you spend Thickets elsewhere is often correct, unless the Blight would cascade or the land holds Presence you need to keep. Foul Vapors (0-cost, long range, Push Explorer, missing Moon) has two core jobs: pushing a lone Explorer out of a land that just Explored to stop a Build, or pushing Explorers out of a land right after the special rule turned its Build into Explorers, preventing the Blight that would otherwise land on Ravage. Because it's a Slow Power resolving after Invaders Explore, it's frequently played without a confirmed target — the trick is to scan which terrains can still Explore this stage, identify which lands would need saving, and commit accordingly rather than treating the blind timing as a reason to skip it. Exaltation (lets a Spirit gain a Power, missing Moon) is Mud's main support tool: it lets an ally skip a Reclaim-for-Growth turn, or bank a Growth pick that doesn't itself grant a Power on Mud's gift instead, and in true solo, targeting yourself is notably cheaper than gifting outward. It costs Energy per Power gained, so weigh it against other spends — external Energy gifts make it easier to justify, and a Growth option's Energy bonus can effectively be "traded" for an extra Exaltation draw.

Growth and Tracks

All three Growth options add Presence — Mud scales mainly through Presence and its tracks rather than through new Powers from Growth, with Exaltation serving as the Power-gain lever instead. The double-Presence option is the main scaling engine and, since Mud leans so heavily on Sacred Sites, usually the most important pick: both Presence can go adjacent to existing Presence to seed a brand-new Sacred Site in a single turn. The Reclaim+Presence option is weaker for raw Presence gain but not a trap — Mud is one of the few spirits that places Presence on Reclaim, so reclaiming early doesn't cost as much tempo as it does for spirits without that perk, and it's a real way to set up a Sacred Site while getting good cards back. The Powers/Energy option trades Presence growth for card advantage and Energy, which is strong for spamming toward a Minor- or Plays-heavy build, or for grabbing a Major plus the Energy to help pay for it. A strong opening line takes two of the double-Presence option and one of the Powers/Energy option, all before the first Reclaim — flexible, and swappable toward the Powers/Energy option as soon as the board stops needing both Presences purely for Build-prevention, since the earlier that swap happens the more time there is to plan around the new Minor Power gained. The Energy track carries a Water element, so a Major-focused build only needs one each of Moon/Water/Earth to hit Innate level 2; the Plays track carries Earth and Moon, letting a Minor-focused build cover Innate elements from the tracks alone and use its Unique Power plays (all Water) freely. One community framing treats the Powers/Energy Growth option as almost always the better overall pick — playing Mud like a Major-power tank — and reserves the double-Presence option specifically for stopping an imminent City/Town or grabbing an element under time pressure; absent reliably getting 3-4 card plays a turn, defaulting to a Major-driven build over a card-volume build is the safer read.

The Strife + Push Combo

The spirit's core special-rule interaction is Strife plus Push, usually used together: adding Strife to a town or explorer enables pushing it, and moving the Incarna into a land also adds Strife there, so the two effects reinforce each other. A top-rated combo is adding Strife on reclaim: this simultaneously avoids or downgrades a Build (by moving the explorer or town that would build) and lets that Strife'd invader be pushed into a land that's about to Ravage, with the Incarna then shifted away so the Dahan land the killing blow.

That shifting-away matters because of a caveat: Dahan don't participate in a Ravage in a land where Strife is present and the Incarna is adjacent. Wanting a Dahan counterattack means moving the Incarna somewhere else first. An empowered Incarna's land-isolation effect is a minor bonus in this plan — not very impactful in practice on its own.

Because the Incarna is highly mobile, most turns come down to several small positional decisions: push invaders together into one land, then hit that land with whichever innate is available. Keeping the Incarna roughly central preserves flexibility, since pushing it far away closes off options for stopping other builds. This spirit is best kept focused on a small area of the island, Strife-ing invaders there repeatedly, rather than roaming to help distant boards — building a couple of extra sacred sites nearby gives more flexible targeting without needing to travel.

Growth and Opening

The bottom growth track is a trap if played carelessly: pure bottom-track growth, or unveiling the 2- or 3-card-play thresholds early without the cards to fill them, strands the spirit in a repeated reclaim loop — worsened by its low initial energy income. Growth-order debate exists on how hard to lean into Air: one line repeatedly takes the G3 bottom growth to hit 3 Air per turn as fast as possible for a stronger positional game, though playtests of it were mixed. The Healing Track Isn't an Element Engine

Healing Markers, gained through Seeking a Path Towards Healing, are easy to mistake for Element Markers, but they aren't interchangeable. Healing Markers don't grant Elements and can't be used or prepared by effects that key off Element Markers, such as Shifting Memory of Ages' element-preparation ability — so don't plan a threshold or a gift around Healing Markers standing in for real elements.

Seeking a Path Towards Healing itself isn't gated on playing a card. It compares the spirit's current Animal and Water elements and awards a Healing Marker for whichever is higher, and a 0/0 tie still resolves to a marker. That means even a turn spent only placing Presence via Reclaim, with zero Energy spent and zero cards played, still advances the healing track. In practice this means the healing path progresses passively on its own schedule — a slow board-development start doesn't stall it, so there's no need to force early card plays purely to keep healing moving.

Reading the Growth Track

The innate powers threshold at 5 of the same element, which looks unreachable early since the board only ever offers 2 elements' worth of card plays at a time. The growth branch that looks like it should solve this by adding Energy actually adds card plays instead, up to 4 per turn. Planning around "I need more Energy to hit threshold" is the wrong read of that branch — it's card plays, and more plays is what actually scales element generation up to where the 5-element innate thresholds become reachable.

That third play is the single most-repeated priority across experienced lines — reaching 3 plays a turn sooner rather than later outweighs squeezing a marginally better Growth order.

G3's minor/major power gain also comes with a Reclaim, which forces a forget-from-discard choice. Because G3 leaves the energy bank thin for the next several turns, don't reflexively keep every expensive card you draw — a cheap 1-cost minor is usually worth keeping over a pricier one once G3 has passed, and a genuinely expensive major (something on the order of Talons of Lightning) is worth seriously considering forgetting in favor of a 2-cost card instead, even though giving up a whole major feels like a bigger loss.

Two Ways to Sequence the Opening

The Growth building blocks above can be arranged two main ways, and neither is strictly better — they trade differently. One line (mixed-powers) runs G3 into G2 into G4 into G2-bottom, gaining a minor turn 1 and building toward a balanced hand of Uniques before ever taking a major; it reaches Innate level 2 by turn 2-3 through card-element coverage rather than a big power spike. The other line (early-major) opens G2-top then jumps straight to G3-bottom on turn 2 to grab a major immediately, forgetting from discard right away — then forks depending on whether that major happens to carry the Fire element. If it does, ride it straight into Empowering the Incarna next turn. If it doesn't, hold it unplayed a turn and instead spend that turn's plays finishing off Innate level 2 with the cards already in hand, then catch the major up later alongside a second one. The non-Fire fork ends up very slightly more card-efficient by turn 6 despite a thinner hand in the interim, and it reaches Innate level 2 a turn sooner — the Fire fork trades that for a bigger single spike once Empowered.

Which of the two to run isn't really a fixed choice — treat both as conditional branches off the same trunk. If turn 1 offers a great Lahar target and a Wilds is on the table, the mixed-powers approach that banks Grasping Roots and Lahar together tends to flow naturally. If the priority is locking down a major as fast as possible regardless of what's drafted, the early-major line is the more direct route — and once you're in it, the Fire-element check on your gained major is the real fork, not something to plan around in advance.

Adapting to the Board

Against an aggressive early explorer/builder — a board racing to drop two towns fast — the standard Lahar-first opening can lose to the clock. The fix is to swap in Blazing Intimidation turn 1 to physically move builders out of a threatened land, even though that sacrifices the turn-1 innate trigger. An alternative when facing that pressure: open G3-bottom instead, adding Grasping Roots to power the innate off a Wilds pull, then take G2 on turn 2 pulling from the top track so Terrifying Rampage can stop the second city (with Surging Lahar backing it up if a Fire card has come in by then). Reading the board's build pace before locking in a fixed sequence is the actual skill here, more than memorizing either line verbatim.

Common Mistakes

  • Treating either opening sequence as a script rather than a set of conditional branches — the right line depends on what Lahar targets, Wilds, and majors are actually available, and on how aggressively the board's builders are moving.
  • Hoarding every expensive card drawn off a G3 major-gain instead of forgetting down to what the post-G3 energy drought can actually support.
  • Playing Terrifying Rampage turn 1, when it has nothing to answer yet and a Fire+Earth pair is more valuable spent elsewhere.
  • Skipping a Wilds pull to avoid Grasping Roots — it's close to always worth taking.

The mixed-powers and early-major openings are two ends of the same spectrum rather than competing scripts — read the board (Lahar targets, Wilds availability, builder aggression) to pick a starting lean, then let the Fire-element check on your first major and the pace of enemy building steer the rest.

The Repeat Mechanic

Casting a power from a land holding 3 of your own Presence lets you repeat that same card, paying an escalating cost each additional cast — roughly 0, then 1, then 3, then 6 energy, each repeat costing one more than the last. This turns every card "modular": a card with a tiny base effect can scale into a serious threat once repeated two or three times, often for less energy than a dedicated major would cost for the same effect. A 0-cost card that solves one Explorer or Town normally can, repeated twice more, wipe an entire land's invaders for 3 energy total — cheaper than most purpose-built Ravage-skip cards.

Fear effects benefit unusually well from repeating, because most other effects (damage, defense, pushes) have diminishing returns once a land is already handled — the extra output goes to waste. Fear has no such ceiling: every point generated counts toward Terror Level 3 regardless of which land it came from. Repeatable fear-generating minors are correspondingly stronger on this spirit than almost anywhere else.

Sanity-check any repeat chain against a simple rule of thumb: if spending more than about 3 energy repeating a single non-major card, ask whether you'd be excited to pay that for a 3-cost major with the same effect — if not, stop repeating and look for another play. One-cost cards are worth repeating less aggressively than 0-cost ones, since the jump to a second cast is proportionally steeper. Repeating matters least when running a pure-major or pure-bottom-track build (majors often solve a land outright, and bottom-track leans on extra card plays instead) and matters most in a mixed-track, minor-heavy build, where it's the central engine.

Presence-Destruction Resilience

Cards that destroy this spirit's own Presence — normally a real drawback — are far less punishing here, and the growth option that returns 3 destroyed Presence removes the downside almost entirely. Roughly half of the negative Blighted-Island events and board effects specifically destroy or force destruction of Presence; this spirit shrugs those off where most others spiral, since the Presence just goes back down next turn. It's sometimes even a net positive, functioning as free repositioning around the board.

The right innate reinforces this further: when active, it re-adds destroyed Presence instead of letting a power add Blight — but needs a specific element combo that none of this spirit's own cards natively supply, so finding it means fishing minor draws for the right elements. A card that would otherwise be feared for stripping your board presence (destroying it across every track) becomes a repeatable, near-free engine once this innate is active, since the loss reverses at no cost the following turn.

Early game is the exception to all this comfort: Presence is tightly constrained turns 1-2, since a triple-stack is needed to unlock repeats, every power needs Sacred-Site presence to originate targeting from, and Presence ideally sits in or near Badlands for repositioning. Overcommitting to one triple-stack turn 1 can leave no second Sacred Site available turn 2 — a real tension in the opening.

Left Innate: Fear vs. Blight

The left innate normally generates 2 fear or skips a Ravage; at 5+ of this spirit's elements (easy to reach), it generates 3 fear instead. That creates a recurring turn-by-turn choice — skip the Ravage, or eat the Blight and bank fear (worthwhile near a Terror threshold) — though the choice tends to resolve itself with experience, since favoring fear is so consistently correct late-game.

Using the innate without a Moon element adds a Blight every time; only 3 of this spirit's own elements plus a Moon avoids it. Without a Moon card, every turn becomes a forced choice between holding back the innate or accepting a Blight. This matters far more solo, where you're the only Blight sink on the board, than in 3-4 player games where an occasional Blight is more tolerable. Because of this, Moon-element minors — usually considered mediocre picks on most spirits — are disproportionately strong here, since they unlock the Blight-free innate and, combined with the presence-destruction resilience above, turn otherwise-clunky Blight-gated cards (cards that push and add fear but also add Blight) into repeatable, near-broken combos once the Blight-add is replaced by a Presence-destroy instead.

Concentrate-and-Clear: Building the Dump Land

The core gameplan is to push invaders into one or more Badlands-heavy "dump lands," then unleash burst damage once the stack is big enough to matter. A late-game staple: gather 7-8 Presence into a single land using a movement/repositioning power, then trigger the innate's per-Presence damage — turning a large stack into a one-shot "laser beam" that can clear an entire built-up land in a single action. The same repositioning tools double as damage, movement, or pure extended range — parking a triple-stack and using the innate purely to relocate it works too, and range-boosting support is unusually strong here since Presence already moves constantly, so every point of range compounds with the movement powers already in the kit.

Badlands-and-Disease combinations are a trap on this spirit specifically: the whole point of a dump land is letting invaders build up for repeated destroy/fear value, but Disease prevents that buildup from happening. Where a card offers both, relocate the Badlands out to a low-priority land and leave the Disease behind rather than stacking them together.

Three Broad Builds

Playstyle shifts significantly depending on which of three broad approaches gets leaned into, and the choice isn't locked in from turn 1 — it's frequently decided by what turns up in the cards. A pure top-track, major-fishing build pivots hard around whichever major shows up, since a strong one can reshape the whole plan. A pure bottom-track build grinds efficient small solves with base power cards, saving the left innate for late-game detonation of accumulated dump lands. A mixed-track build leaning on cheap Moon/fear minors turns off the left innate's Blight-adding, still blows up dump lands, and simultaneously builds huge Fear totals — base cards alone can generate roughly 16 fear per turn by late game solo (a full Terror level in a single turn), enough to let this spirit stall or turtle on fear generation alone and grind out a Terror Level 3 win even if board control goes sideways.

There's no single fixed opening — the viable turn-1/2 lines vary by board, adversary, and where the first Explorer lands more than for almost any other spirit. A safe default is building toward a 2-energy/2-card-play mix first (the order between the two doesn't matter much), then pushing to 3/3, by which point both innates are reliably affordable. Growth picks that draft new cards are worth prioritizing early rather than late, specifically because of the repeat mechanic: a card drafted turn 1 might get played 7-8 times over a full game, while the same card drafted turn 7 might only get used once — the value of any given draft decays as the game goes on. Early drafting also matters because finding a Moon card as soon as possible is what turns off the left innate's Blight problem. This competes directly with strong early aggressive lines (an extra-energy play into a wide-hitting push power), and balancing the two is a real source of the spirit's depth. Later in the game, its energy-growth option scales and improves more than most spirits' flatter energy tracks, which naturally pulls the build toward energy and reclaim options and away from further drafting as turns pass.

Common Mistakes

  • Tunnel-visioning on solving one land perfectly instead of learning to spread solves across multiple lands — one of the first real challenge points for new players of this spirit.
  • Committing to pure top-track play without a plan for a second effective action each turn — this leaves you able to solve only one land per turn, taking Blight everywhere else, and over-relying on the left innate for a "second land" compounds into a Blight spiral.
  • Missing the pivot point from "solve every big land" mode into "stop solving, dump into fear, rush Terror Level 3" mode — timing that transition is one of the harder skill tests with this spirit, since its fear ceiling is unusually high once the pivot is made.
  • Playing without a Moon-element card in hand for several turns, forcing a Blight every time the left innate gets used.
  • Repeating a card past the point of real value — extra damage or defense wasted on an already-solved land, or repeating a card whose effect (like adding several token types at once) rarely needs to be layered 3x when a land only needs solving once or twice.
  • Over-investing in a single land's repeat chain when two separate lands need attention — repeats only ever help the one targeted land; if two different lands have problems, more card plays solve that, not deeper repeats on one card.
  • Repeating one minor three times when playing three different minors once each would generate more distinct elements for similar cost — generally the more efficient use of energy, board state depending.

Ally & Adversary Notes

Range-boosting allies are unusually strong partners, since Presence already relocates constantly on this spirit — a flat range boost compounds with the movement tools already in the kit rather than sitting idle. Card-play support pushes a build harder into top-track majors, funding repeats with the extra energy a Growth 3 pick generates; energy support instead goes straight into drafting or into feeding the repeat-cost curve. The right innate can return an ally's destroyed Presence when active, and (situationally, when the right elements line up) donate spare energy back to a partner — a small but real way this spirit gives back rather than just consuming support.

Matchups vary sharply by adversary. Sweden is close to a non-contest: this spirit brings both damage and Dahan control to cover Sweden's few real pressure points, and can shrug off Dahan losses it doesn't care about. Brandenburg-Prussia plays to a real strength — where other spirits fear pocketing Prussia into a few coastal lands because of brutal chained Ravages, this spirit can reclaim-loop its Ravage-skip through repeated turns and then burst-clear the accumulated stack in one shot once its tracks are further along. Russia is a tougher, contested matchup: a bottom-track build can still grind it out by cleanly solving early, dumping everything into one beast-heavy land, and skipping that land's Ravage every turn even as it snowballs, eventually reclaiming enough card plays to seize control and detonate the dump land for the win — but a pure top-track build often lacks the action economy to handle Russia's overall pressure without that one-big-dump plan. The Habsburg Mining Encampment is the hardest matchup for some players: its early double-Explorer-into-immediate-Ravage line generates heavy Blight pressure with little buffer, and the fix is to break from this spirit's normal rhythm — focus fully on hitting Explorers the instant they land, and rush to shut down the double-Explorer engine itself, even deliberately adding a Blight to a land yourself if it helps cross that engine's Blight threshold sooner.

The Endless Dark as a Stockpile

Abduction isn't one-and-done removal — an invader pulled into the Endless Dark is a resource whose release timing you control. Delaying release lets you keep farming fear (and, for disease-carrying invaders specifically, holding them rather than releasing immediately can be worth the wait) rather than banking a single hit of value and moving on.

That stockpiling logic extends to reclaiming your own hand. Every reclaim is a detour away from maxed innate output, so the strongest lines treat delaying the first reclaim as a build order in itself: take cheap cards, underplay some turns, and grab the Reclaim-1 growth spot specifically so reclaiming stops being a tax. That spot — four growth purchases in — is repeatedly described as the point the spirit turns "unstoppable," since by then energy is piling up faster than it's needed and the rest of the game is just sustained fear output.

Tempo Above All

Breath is a tempo spirit before anything else: letting a turn-1 build go through costs roughly three times the effort to undo later compared to stopping it immediately. This cuts against any plan that skips a stop in order to save resources or set up a bigger play next turn — the math almost never favors the deferral, because the extra explores and blight that build enables compound.

The one recognized exception is boards where a land will see repeated new builds regardless of what you clear — dense multi-city lands, for instance. There, razing existing cities early can be worth it even at the cost of a turn's plays, because it denies coastal-adjacency builds from replacing what you just took out and lets you abduct a freshly-explored land without a new build immediately backfilling it.

Bottom Track: Rushing the Innate

The bottom track (plays and the left innate, Leave a Trail of Deathly Silence) aims to rush innate level 2-3 fast, banking a strong early board position and a high volume of card plays. It pairs naturally with the Incarna's own mobility, since the track's extra presence-move option compounds with it. The cost is heavier reliance on the top track's growth-3 energy spot to keep affording plays, which means more frequent reclaims and a later timeline to actually reach four plays a turn — some lines never unlock a fourth play at all even against tough adversaries, since three plays plus an extra moon element is often already enough to blast through invaders.

Aggressive versions of this build skip turn-1 card plays entirely in order to threshold the left innate a turn earlier, using the extra growth to clear a major land (even a double-city land) on turn 2 with a good chance at a pocket. This is a real tradeoff, not a free lunch: the counter-argument is that stopping a fresh build directly is almost always possible and preserves momentum better than playing nothing, since removing presence from a land that isn't yet doing anything is a worse trade than denying a build in progress. In practice the choice comes down to whether the drafted hand is strong enough to threshold turn 2 anyway without sacrificing turn-1 plays — if so, the extra growth turn is close to free; if the hand is weak, skipping plays to rush the threshold is harder to justify.

Even a bottom-track build that mostly avoids taking cities directly still has answers for them: the left innate's third tier can outright destroy a city when needed (useful for hitting Terror Level 3), and the thresholded right innate can abduct an invader out of a land that isn't empty, so "keep lands free of cities" isn't a hard requirement of the strategy.

Top Track: Patching the Gap

The top track (energy and majors) matters most against adversaries that build tall or fortify heavily, since the abduction powers need a land to be otherwise empty, and majors clear dense stacks that the innates can't touch alone. A run of unlucky draws that stacks several stages of building into one land before it can be reached is exactly the scenario a timely major resolves quickly, where the innate-only plan would grind.

The skeptical view is that the top track struggles to justify itself at all: card-play energy already goes a long way, majors add less than they cost in opportunity, and even the empowered Incarna isn't strictly necessary when three plays plus a moon element already sustains a fear-rushing plan. On this view, reaching the Empower slot costs several turns of maxed innate output that would have gone further spent on reaching four plays or the Reclaim-1 spot instead. The counter-view is that G2 (rather than G3) on the top track is efficient specifically because it doesn't cost the reposition option most builds rarely use, and that a couple of good cheap minors can reach the Empower slot before the first reclaim — meaning the top track adds power without the reclaim tax the bottom track incurs, as long as you're selective about which growth options you take.

The empowered Incarna's kill effect is a real but situational upgrade over the base kit: it can kill an invader in a land that isn't empty (something like an extra tier of the left innate, but working under different conditions), which matters specifically against non-lone-invader targets the abduction powers can't touch — it's not simply a strict upgrade over the Incarna's default damage/push option.

Choosing and Blending a Track

Because both tracks are viable, the choice is read off the adversary rather than fixed in advance. Adversaries that go tall or fortify hard call for leaning top track; adversaries that spread wide and build fast call for the bottom-track rush, which reaches the early innate tiers fastest and locks in board position before things get out of hand. Adversaries that don't fortify heavily and don't come out swinging early are the target for a hybrid: open on the top track for a turn or two, then commit to the bottom track. A common simplified default is "top track until the first moon element, then bottom track" rather than committing to either extreme from turn one.

The hybrid path has a card-flexibility advantage worth calling out: since the pure tracks already put two moon elements within reach, a hybrid opening can prioritize air and animal elements instead when drafting, since those are scarcer on the tracks and add more flexibility to the hand. Done well, a hybrid can hit the left innate's max tier about as fast as a pure-bottom rush, compensating for the slower start with the extra cards it picks up along the way — at the cost of being slower out of the gate than pure bottom, and weaker than pure top against a heavily fortified board.

A distinct hybrid variant targets delaying the first reclaim past the norm: grow the top track for two turns first (for energy and a moon element), then take the bottom track twice to reach three plays and unlock the left innate's third tier plus the full right innate — all before ever reclaiming. The idea is to stack invaders, including cities, into the Endless Dark across one or two key lands while fear accumulates faster than the invaders still on the board can threaten it, holding majors in reserve to finish off the fear deck or clean up a built-up land later if needed. This is explicitly framed as a safer alternative to a pure-bottom rush's risk of the board getting away from you before the fear plan pays off.

Its elements suit Innate level 2+ rather than level 1, so using it may mean missing a level-1-solvable problem elsewhere — plan turn order around that, for instance saving Sweltering Exhaustion for the turn after Herders if Herders can't cover a Build that needs stopping. Like Sandstorm it's mainly a small-problem fixer and can't touch Cities directly; hitting a City with it needs real setup, such as moving two-plus Dahan (or one Dahan plus a Sacred Site) into a land with a City about to Ravage, backed by enough Defend.

Big turns come from chaining several cards together: Gift of Searing Heat, a Minor, Sandstorm, and Herders in sequence can kill multiple Towns via reduced-Health Sacred Site Damage, re-establish a Sacred Site by gathering into it, and push the Innate to a high tier in the same turn — enough to jump a full Terror Level in one action. Drafting a Major forces dropping a Power in exchange, and Sweltering Exhaustion is a natural cut once a plan commits to letting Ravages through for Fear rather than blocking them.

A generally useful Spirit Island principle this spirit's playthroughs reinforce: it's often correct to let a land Blight, or lose a Dahan, during a natural Ravage rather than manually clearing it yourself, if a surviving Dahan will clear an Invader for free as a result — useful whenever full manual clearing isn't affordable, and Fear cards can tilt such trades further in your favor.

Rising Heat rewards funneling problems toward Sacred Sites and using Sandstorm/Herders to keep repositioning Presence and invaders into those consolidated kill zones, where Blistering Heat turns marginal Damage into clean kills. The Energy/Majors vs. Plays/Minors fork is genuinely even and forgiving, since Innate level 1 stays cheap and reliable either way — the real skill is sequencing Sweltering Exhaustion, Sandstorm, and Herders around what a land needs, and knowing when letting a Ravage through is worth more than blocking it.

Identity: a split-personality spirit — half disease-spreading, half revenge-and-damage — whose three special rules only pay off if read together. Rather than rushing to destroy invaders, the core plan is to deliberately let invaders build and Ravage for a while, seeding Disease and Blight as fuel, before pivoting hard into damage abilities that scale off the mess left behind. Below roughly 3 Energy / 3 Card Plays expect a rough, fragile early game; above that power spike, the rest of the game is comparatively easy — energy sustains every turn and Tier 2 innate thresholds unlock, so a confident board sweep becomes realistic.

Why the Special Rules Work Together

Terror of a Slowly Unfolding Plague lets a Build happen instead of being stopped by Disease, generating 1 Fear. It's tempting to just block builds outright, but letting them through is usually correct, especially early: two Unique Powers plus one innate can only target lands that already have Disease on them, so stripping Disease by stopping builds removes your own targets. Fear also compounds — allowing one build a turn adds up to roughly a full Fear card's worth of Terror by game's end.

Lingering Pestilence adds a Disease token whenever your own presence is destroyed by anything other than your own Power. This isn't just a consolation prize for bad luck — deliberately feeding presence into lands you expect to lose (especially turns 2-3) seeds Disease on purpose and sets up the rest of the game. Don't overdo it, but purposeful early losses pay off later, particularly stacked with the next rule.

Wreak Vengeance for the Land's Corruption treats Blight as Badlands for this spirit's damage purposes. Since the whole plan already involves letting invaders build and cause Blight, this converts that Blight directly into extra damage output — and both innate powers plus one Unique Power are damage-dealing, so the bonus compounds across the kit. It also matters vs. Disease: stacking Badlands and Disease in the same land is usually a trap, since Disease prevents the buildup a dump land needs — when a card offers both, send the Badlands to a low-priority land and leave Disease where you want to keep targeting.

One presence anywhere on the board (not necessarily in the Disease land) is enough to use the Build-through-Disease rule. Events that say invaders "ignore Disease this build phase" still generate Fear if you choose to let the build through — despite pre-errata phrasing suggesting simultaneity, the ruling is that you choose the order. And Disease from Lingering Pestilence triggers off ANY non-Power destruction of your presence — Events, blighted-island effects, Ravages, all of it, not just combat.

Growth: Presence First, Then Balance the Tracks

All three growth options are usable. Reclaiming cards plus energy, double-growth at Range 2 into buildings/Blight, and adding Presence/Disease plus energy. Because you want a lot of presence on the board — including presence you expect to lose — leaning into that third option early is natural, but over-indexing on double-growth burns through cards fast and forces a reclaim loop. Alternating between double and single growth avoids stalling.

Both innates gate behind element thresholds unlocked via the presence tracks (Fire, Animal), so opening moves are constrained by which lands your powers can actually reach and by available energy — only 1 Energy is uncovered at the very start. "Tempo" language matters here: acting on a land right as it Builds (via the Slow Power) or the turn Invaders Ravage (via the Fast Power) counts as being On Tempo; pre-empting a Build/Explore before it can ever Ravage counts as being Above Invader's Tempo. Sometimes lands still have to be sacrificed to Blight to stay on tempo elsewhere — that's expected, not a failure.

The overarching principle: scaling board presence and reaching the power spike matters more than card-efficiency in the moment. Playing Strike Low with nothing to skip just to hit a low innate tier, playing a weak minor when passing would bank it for a Tier 2 threshold next turn, or reclaiming purely because you can't otherwise act while holding Strike Low — all of these create repeated lingering weak turns (usually via lost cards to discard) instead of the one big unavoidable weak turn every game has anyway. Take the one bad turn; don't manufacture several.

Disease Placement Priorities Shift Over the Game

Early game, place Disease to control a Build or to force a Strike Low skip in edge cases (double Ravages), then follow up with growth to regain tempo — this is typically a turns 3-5 concern. Mid game, once past the 2/3 or 3/3 mark, Disease instead increases Epidemic-style damage, enables Plaguebearers manipulation, or blocks unwanted builds in lightly-Diseased lands, letting you control builds at leisure or stop a city build by relocating the explorer/towns out while keeping the Disease behind. Late game, Disease placement is about nuking or pre-empting Cities outright.

Innate and Unique Power Roles

The damage innate (fast, hits per Disease token, also hits Dahan though that's not a priority) needs max Disease on the board, fed by the Fear-and-Disease minor, by presence destruction, and by event cards — a relocation power can pile Disease into one land first. Its first tier is reachable by roughly turn 2 via card plays plus the Animal-track unlock; its second tier costs more energy than a typical turn provides for a while, so it takes patience to come online, but hits hard once it does; its highest tier is a late-game target requiring a maxed bottom track plus a second power carrying the right elements.

The mirror-image innate (needs Fire instead of Animal) is weak at its lowest tier in practice: it only triggers in a land with buildings and your own presence during the slow phase, but if invaders Ravaged that land this turn your presence is probably already gone (feeding Lingering Pestilence instead), and if you stopped the Ravage no Blight was added yet either — so the window where it actually fires is narrow early on. It gets much stronger once invaders return to already-Blighted lands in later invader stages, and hits hard at its second tier (capable of taking down a city). Its top tier needs a cleared bottom track and specific elements — a stretch goal, not a plan. Because both innates want different elements (Fire vs. Animal), running both online simultaneously usually needs lucky power draws that supply both.

The Disease-seeding minor is important less for its own damage and more because three other powers require an existing Disease token as a valid target — it also feeds Fear through the build-through rule. It combos well with the Disease-damage innate, poorly with the Fire-based innate. Its cost is on the high side, so some cheaper 1-energy minors that also add Disease are worth prioritizing as substitutes.

The Ravage-skip power (Fear + skip, Disease-lands only) runs directly against the "let them Ravage" plan, which is why using it turn 2 to stop the very first Ravage is usually premature — better to bank at least one turn of Blight-as-Badlands damage first. It becomes more valuable as the game goes on: presence and Blight can't be sacrificed forever, and skipping a Ravage keeps presence alive for the Fire-based innate to use. Events that state invaders don't Ravage in Diseased lands waste this power's Fear-generation angle when they overlap.

The self-targeting damage/Blight-manipulation power is usable on other spirits but mostly serves yourself, since you're the one deliberately sacrificing presence — it must target a land where your presence currently sits, so if that presence was just destroyed, growth needs to re-add it before the power can hit there again. It combos strongly with any power that moves or adds Blight, and is the cheapest power in the kit, so it sees frequent play once card plays scale up. It pairs well with the Fire-based innate, poorly with the Disease-damage innate.

The move power is a standout: it generates Fear, and moves invaders/Dahan/Disease itself by Disease count (moving Disease requires Disease already present in the land). The signature combo is seeding Disease, then using this power to gather all invaders into a different land, then hitting that consolidated pile with the Disease-damage innate next turn.

Card to Forget

Usually the Disease-seeding minor, once the kit is specialized around a drafted major plus the sacrifice/Disease engine — unless synergy cards for it were drafted (water-and-blight synergies, movement/range majors), in which case forget the self-targeting Blight power instead, even if it went unused. Against adversaries that give invaders extra health, it can also be right to forget the self-targeting power if no combo pieces for it showed up — otherwise keep it for cards that use the Sun element, generate Badlands, or need a finishing hit.

Board Choice

One board stands out as clearly best: it starts in an already-Blighted land with 2 Dahan, and every land is Range 1 except one coastal land — an easy turn-1 presence placement into the neighboring Sands land with any build, which is worth reaching eventually anyway. The 2 starting Dahan add extra Event/energy value and sit in one of the easier lands to protect via the self-targeting or Fire-innate tools. Its central location gives Range 2 to the whole board, which is ideal for the move power and several strong majors, and its geography lets a handful of minors cover nearly every land of a given terrain type with minimal Sacred Site investment. This meaningfully eases the game compared to boards with worse range or Dahan placement — board choice matters more for this spirit than it might seem.

If that board isn't available, look for a board with similarly strong central range to a Jungle-type land, or one with good central placement generally — a board that splits into two disconnected regional halves is a real downside since it fights the "consolidate into one land" plan directly.

Favorite Draft Picks

Strong major pickups include: a gather-and-range major that stacks Blight and threshold easily off the Disease-seeding minor; a big energy-battery major; a plant-synergy major that pairs unusually well with this spirit's abundant damage tools; a beast-typed major (this spirit itself counts as a Beast Spirit) with strong element and Blight/Badlands damage synergy; a Presence-destruction major that's normally awkward but becomes strong here, especially into adversaries that punish presence loss; a Skip-and-damage major whose 2-card-play threshold is easy to hit; a defense-then-board-wipe major that needs the 3/3 spike to threshold but hits hard once it does; a Badlands/Disease-stacking major that enables absurd token counts; a high-damage major off a 3-play threshold; a mobile-Blight-fortress major dealing heavy repeated damage once the Fire-based power is also in play; and a water/animal-synergy major that's fun but not always optimal, needing supporting 0-cost minors drafted alongside it.

Preferred minor powers carry Fire and/or Animal elements (either alone is fine; Water/Air also useful), including several rated excellent: a Fear-generating minor, a life-and-death minor, a Dahan-strife minor, an earth-Blight-synergy minor, and a beast-control minor all stand out; a cheaper alternate Disease-seeder with better elements (at the cost of not reaching every land) is a near-perfect fit. A Dahan-manipulation minor is risky unless Dahan are already quarantined to a single land, since it's easy to accidentally kill them. A late-game invader-self-destruction minor is handy once you've stopped caring about board damage yourself, though its threshold is rarely reached in practice.

Rarely worth drafting: several niche minors with narrow elements or effects that don't fit the plan, plus a small situational tier (some good only for their elements as Event-forgets, some good specifically in solo play, one good mainly against a Russia-style adversary or with France's extra towns). Majors almost never worth taking include several complicated or narrowly-focused cards, plus a small "only if desperate" tier that at least outranks the worst options in an emergency; one Blight-concentration major suits a different spirit's kit better than this one's, since concentrating Blight into a single land isn't this spirit's natural mode.

Common Mistakes

  • Blocking every Build reflexively — denies your own kit the Disease tokens it needs to target with its damage powers, and forfeits the Fear the Build-through rule would have generated.
  • Playing the Ravage-skip power the very first turn a land threatens to Ravage, before any Blight-as-Badlands damage has had a chance to accumulate there.
  • Reclaiming just because no better play exists this turn, while still holding the Ravage-skip power in hand for later — better to grow and wait if the 3/3 spike hasn't been reached yet, since scaling outweighs early reclaiming.
  • Over-committing to double-growth for presence, burning through the hand too fast and forcing a reclaim loop — alternate with single growth instead.
  • Stacking Badlands and Disease together in the same land, which blocks the very buildup a dump land needs — split them across lands instead.

Vengeance funds a mid-game power spike by deliberately letting invaders build, sacrificing presence for Disease, and banking Blight as bonus damage — the goal is 3 Energy / 3 Card Plays, not a clean early board. Its three special rules only make sense as one interlocking engine: Build-through for Fear and Disease targets, presence loss for free Disease, Blight-as-Badlands for damage. Choose growth to keep presence flowing without emptying the hand, place Disease with the game phase in mind, and don't burn Strike Low or a weak card just to avoid a quiet turn — the spike is worth the wait.

Identity: a pure logistics spirit — its special rule, Open the Ways, lets it push pieces between almost any two of its own lands rather than only adjacent ones, extending every range-0 power across the whole board. It has little direct removal of its own, so the game plan is to herd invaders into one land and then clear that land in a single burst, either with its own major or by handing the pile off to a partner who hits hard.

Table Size Changes Everything

In multiplayer, gift the concentrated pile to a spirit with strong direct damage (Volcano, Thunderspeaker, Stone, Ocean) rather than relying on Finder's own kill tools — Finder's real job is repositioning invaders into reach of that partner and shielding the rest of the board while it waits. In true solo there's no one to hand the pile to: you must both group the invaders yourself and carry a big land-clearing major to finish them, which makes solo noticeably harder than multiplayer for this spirit.

Finder can destroy invaders directly, but it costs an extra energy plus a presence — worth paying in true solo, where board presence isn't needed for much else anyway. Destruction can also come indirectly, via a set-up counterattack or a threshold effect on certain cards (the non-thresholded version of some cards, like Instruments, only moves or weakens — it doesn't destroy). Presence still isn't infinite even solo; a bad event card can run it low, so don't treat direct-destroy as a free unlimited tap.

Growth: Balance Energy and Plays, Don't Rush Either Extreme

A pure energy-first opening (heavy top track, using the +1 card-play growth option to reach 4 energy/turn quickly) gets to early Majors fast but tends to miss Air and Moon elements badly and leaves the spirit short on card plays to handle small fires along the way. A pure plays-first opening (beelining 3 card plays) gives board-control flexibility early but forces heavy reliance on a single growth slot for enough energy to keep up.

The stronger general approach is pursuing both moderately rather than either extreme — a slower initial ramp in exchange for stronger continued growth once both energy and plays are online. Reaching Growth 3 (the card-pickup growth option) is worth prioritizing specifically because it reduces how often you're forced to reclaim, letting the spirit grow more continuously through the midgame; a single Defend card picked up this way can open up many downstream options. Delaying the bottom-track card-play growth spot for too long forces the other non-presence growth slot into boosting plays instead of anything else, so that bump is worth taking reasonably early rather than last.

Once past the opening and sitting around 4 energy and 2-3 card plays, branch freely into whichever growth spot the board calls for: a third card play, extra range, or an any-element slot to plug a missing threshold. The unlimited-range movement from Open the Ways matters most in larger (3+ player) games; in 1-2 player games the +1 card-play growth option is usually enough on its own, so range isn't worth chasing as hard solo.

Getting allies to hand over energy or power cards helps this spirit reach its energy break points before being forced into an early reclaim. During growth and drafting, prioritize minors that defend, trigger the innates, or add more pushes — these compound with the spirit's core logistics identity more than raw damage minors do.

Key Powers and a Trap to Avoid

Use the Isolate innate heavily to limit new Explores — isolating the single land holding towns/cities stops further inland Explores entirely, keeping the number of lands that need handling each turn down to roughly one. Open the Ways lets you trigger the first innate and other range-0 powers from anywhere, not just adjacent to the target.

When hunting a finishing major, prioritize potent land-clearing effects (Jungle Hungers, Cast Down, Volcanic Eruption, Briny Deep, Draw Towards a Void) over chipping away with lesser powers — minor or weak powers aren't efficient against a big built-up land and waste the turns spent piling it up in the first place. Watch Cast Down specifically: its non-thresholded bottom effect wipes out your own presence along with the invaders, which can be a genuine "win-loss" mistake in true solo — either avoid triggering that version, or group invaders somewhere your own presence isn't sitting.

Two Ways to Race the Board

The default plan is to pile invaders into one land (often coastal) and hold that pile down until a wipe-effect major is ready, then clear it outright and repeat until nothing's left. A different framing that works at medium difficulty: don't try to prevent the pile's Blight at all — pile everything into one space, heal Blight whenever that land's Ravage comes up rather than trying to stop the Ravage, and push into an early reclaim loop to rush Fear once good Fear powers are drawn. Read as racing the invaders to a win condition rather than racing to stop them.

Ally Synergy: Thunderspeaker

Finder pairs exceptionally well with Thunderspeaker, in a way comparable to how Ocean changes other spirits' planning: normally spirits pre-plan where to act, but with Finder in play that need erodes, since Finder can trivially reposition invaders and Dahan across the board. Concretely, Finder keeps most of the board safe by continuously moving invaders out of threatened lands and into lands other spirits can easily reach with damage or defend actions, and can also feed Dahan into a land to turn a Defend action into a successful counterattack. Leftover invaders get pushed into one dump land; Thunderspeaker amasses a huge Dahan army there and periodically wipes it, and so long as other spirits keep the small scattered fires under control, the pair chews through the bulk of the invasion through that single land.

The cost of running this combo is that Finder effectively becomes "one less spirit" actively fighting invaders, since its actions go to repositioning rather than damage, and it meaningfully increases table planning and discussion time each turn (not necessarily bad, but slower). It also requires meticulous planning: you're voluntarily not handling fires as they appear, deferring them, so you need enough spare buffer that a bad event card can't blindside the group, and Thunderspeaker needs to reliably be able to clear the grouped lands whenever called on. In a reported 3-player game, this combo landed roughly 30 damage in one hit once running, effectively turning a third support spirit's role into pure support — though town/city counts still capped how much blast damage a single land could safely absorb (7 towns/cities against England, in that game).

Common Mistakes

  • Believing the spirit "can't do any damage" and just shuffling invaders around while they mass up and eventually overrun the board with Blight — this frustration reported by newer players traces back to not realizing or using the direct-destroy option (extra energy plus presence) rather than a real weakness in the spirit.
  • Rushing straight to 4 energy/turn for an early Major before board control is stable — one reported solo loss came from exactly this, getting blighted out before the major was ready, compounded by relying on the +1 card-play growth option instead of dedicated plays for board control.
  • Avoiding England and France as early matchups — both are considered harder adversaries to learn this spirit against.
  • Combining Badlands-heavy dump-land thinking with a scattered, unclumped board — new players especially can end up using only a fraction of the spirit's potential, with half-handled lands and early Blight despite trying, because invaders never get properly consolidated into the single land the plan depends on. Playing a solo game with no adversary is a suggested way to relearn how the cards, presence track, and innates interlock before returning to a real game.

Recap

Finder wins through concentration, not attrition: use Open the Ways and Isolate to herd invaders into one land while keeping the rest of the board quiet, then clear that pile with a land-wiping major (solo) or hand it to a hard-hitting partner like Thunderspeaker (multiplayer). Balance energy and card-play growth rather than rushing either track, prioritize card-drafting growth to avoid getting stuck reclaiming, and remember the direct-destroy option exists for the turns before a finisher is ready.

How the engine ticks

England breaks the assumption that shutting down Explorers stops a build. Its buildings go up regardless of whether you've touched the Explorer that triggered them, so prevention-based play — the instinct to nip a problem before it lands — loses most of its value here. Plan to act after a build resolves in a land, not before; cards that only stop Explorers lose value since a build happens next turn anyway.

Its buildings also carry bonus health, which quietly punishes any plan built around small or capped damage. Repeat-build lands are the sharper danger: the same land can build multiple times before a Ravage finally clears it — as many as four builds in a row in the worst case — so the loss-condition math needs constant watching, especially on coastal lands, which are unusually prone to this.

There are really only two ways to actually win against England: pile on enough destroy effects (not incremental damage) to get around the bonus health outright, or run a Fear-rush that sidesteps the board state entirely and never has to out-build England at all. The Fear route only works if your generation is reliable and heavy, not just occasional spikes — you need to clear the thresholds before blight catches up. In 2-hand solo play, a useful simplification is fully clearing one board early rather than trying to contain both at once.

Early vs. late danger

England starts slow — use that early slack to grow and set up combos before the escalation kicks in. The bonus health and build-without-invaders effects really bite from level 5 on, and by level 6 you need consistent Fear generation on top of board control, which narrows viable picks sharply. Any verdict on a spirit "beating England" should be read as level-specific — build-prevention/control spirits have a real edge at levels 1-4 that mostly evaporates at 5+. England has a reputation as one of the most difficulty-transformative adversaries in the game: which spirit you bring matters more here than almost anywhere else, and near-necessitates counter-picking at high levels.

What counters it, and why

  • Thunderspeaker: repeatedly cited as a top pick because her kit favors destroying invaders over merely damaging them.
  • Ocean's Hungry Grasp: Fear generation plus destroy effects; credited in multiple from-scratch wins at England level 5-6, including alongside River Surges in Sunlight (which supplies energy for majors) and Bringer of Dreams and Nightmares.
  • Lure of the Deep Wilderness: underrated here — Lure converts buildings straight into Explorers, so their bonus health is irrelevant, and the resulting badlands make follow-up kills easy. England tends to burn its escalation on the land Lure is already dismantling.
  • Many Minds Move as One: keeps the board free of blight with its Dahan/defend innate while pushing hard on Fear, converting cleanly into a terror-level victory.
  • Starlight Seeks Its Form: its whole toolkit — destroy effects and Fear — is exactly what beats England.
  • Serpent Slumbering Beneath the Island: England is one of the few adversaries slow enough for Serpent to actually awaken in time; defense and Major Powers bridge the early gap, then a huge stockpile of built-up lands becomes an unloading target.
  • Stone's Unyielding Defiance: played almost exclusively on the bottom track, its "let them break themselves" effect ignores blight and directly punishes buildings; strong through roughly level 5.
  • Sharp Fangs Behind the Leaves: disputed — no-cap damage variants plus terrifying-chase and build-prevention give real control, but the basic/capped-damage version struggles against extra-health buildings unless paired with Isolate from Jagged Earth.
  • Grinning Trickster Stirs Up Trouble and Finder of Paths Unseen with Isolate (Jagged Earth): moving invaders out of a land and then isolating it prevents builds entirely, functionally removing England's core power — Finder in particular can't solo England but shines on a team built around this.

  • Relying on repeated small/capped damage to clear buildings — bonus health means you fall behind the build rate.
  • Trying to contain the whole board evenly in 2-hand solo, rather than fully clearing one board early to cut total exposure.

How the engine ticks

Brandenburg-Prussia is the "vanilla" adversary — no bonus invader health, no extra build/migration options, no extra damage. Its entire identity is tempo: an unusually early Stage III escalation card front-loads Ravage pressure before most spirits have developed, and levels 5-6 turn that pressure on from turn one. Crush it early and there's almost no recovery for the invaders; fall behind early and there's rarely a mid-game runway back.

Its development is mechanically simple — lots of towns spring up early, but if explore draws don't hit those specific lands, invaders just make ordinary explorations. Destroying towns or routing invaders away from their lands turns the remaining threat into a "tickle" even though it's spread across many lands; Dahan usage early is close to essential, since Defend 2 plus one or two Dahan clears most lands outright. Because there's no extra health or build-cheat mechanic, "containment" — taking or moving inland structures fast enough that Explores simply fail — works here in a way it doesn't against adversaries built to defeat it (compare Russia, similarly weak to containment; Sweden, only weak to it late).

BP is also unusually exposed to event-card randomness: a beast landing in the right spot, a surprise "+1 explorer" or "towns to cities" card, or an energy-cost event can each swing a game with no real counterplay, since there's rarely energy to spare. This makes it feel trivial some games and hopeless from turn two in others, especially around the Stage II-to-III transition. Because the ruleset is so plain, it's a common pick both for teaching new spirits (levels 2-5, "what B-P actually does" tier) and as a difficulty-adder alongside another adversary.

Early vs. late danger

Almost entirely front-loaded — the endgame outcome is largely baked in by how the early game went. Falling behind early usually means no recovery path, since majors and other clearing strategies come online too late to matter. Level 6 ("handle 4 lands turn one") is a sharp, well-known spike; experienced players sometimes deliberately avoid known-weak spirit picks at that level specifically. Below that, level 2-5 is a fair, low-punishment testing ground for new spirits.

What counters it, and why

  • Heart of the Wildfire, Thunderspeaker, Lure of the Deep Wilderness: called out repeatedly as standout picks — front-loaded, explosive kits that don't need early setup to hit hard, which is exactly what a game this short rewards.
  • Thunderspeaker specifically: built around wiping invaders during the Ravage phase, and strong at clearing inland areas pre-emptively before overlapping invader-card pileups compound.
  • A Spread of Rampant Green and Sharp Fangs Behind the Leaves: build-prevention and fast explorer/town clearing denies BP's fuel outright rather than reacting to it.
  • Grinning Trickster Stirs Up Trouble: surprisingly effective at clearing explorers/towns fast, plus power recycling for free energy.
  • Bringer of Dreams and Nightmares and Many Minds Move as One: BP needs comparatively little total Fear to reach a terror win, so any easy Fear generation goes a long way — a Bringer/Many Minds pairing reached a Terror Level 4 win at level 6 in one report, with fear-card math making progress unusually trackable given the tight timescale.
  • River Surges in Sunlight: strong in smaller teams, since it profits directly off BP's fast, frequent explores for energy, on top of its usual movement utility.
  • Serpent Slumbering Beneath the Island: contested but trending positive — its slumbering-stage defense and utility (protecting big lands before awakening) counters BP specifically, since BP has one of the few consistent answers to the early Stage III card and doesn't need to fully awaken to win. Several players cite Serpent wins at BP level 6, including one player's first-ever level-6 win overall. The dissenting view: BP games often end before Serpent gets to awaken, and awakening is "the fun part," so some avoid the matchup on principle even when it works.
  • Vengeance as a Burning Plague: can actually enjoy BP's output here — BP's early overlap of multiple invader actions in the same land lets Vengeance get blight into targeted lands fast, then farm them, and its "wallow in blight" playstyle fits BP's blight output well.

Weaker matchups: carries and late bloomers (Serpent in some views, Keeper of the Forbidden Wilds, Shifting Memory of Ages, any Major/combo-dependent spirit) generally don't get time to bloom before the game ends. Setup-heavy spirits (Shroud of Silent Mist, Vengeance in one dissenting view) can also struggle if their long-term plan can't compress into BP's short game — though this is contested; Vengeance in particular is defended above. Base Vital Strength of the Earth is viable despite its slowness specifically because it can dump accumulated energy into early majors without caring whether the elements match.

Common mistakes

  • Playing a slow-scaling combo/engine spirit as if there's time to pay off a long-term plan — BP's game length usually doesn't extend far enough.
  • Prioritizing blight-resilience or healing ravage damage — the game is typically won or lost before blight accumulation matters, and preventing a build is usually better value than healing after the fact.
  • Chasing long-term elemental synergy on power cards instead of picking for immediate effect (pushing an explorer, gathering, a point or two of damage) — speed beats combo-building here.

How the engine ticks

Sweden doesn't add a separate loss condition — it just pushes the normal Blight condition harder. Nearly every card effect makes Blight more likely and faster: at damage thresholds, a Ravage adds an extra Blight on top of the usual one, and later levels raise town/city damage until that threshold gets crossed almost automatically. It's effectively "double Blight," sometimes only a 50% increase but consistently more than normal. Unlike England (building-focused) or Brandenburg-Prussia (exploring-focused), Sweden's whole identity is being very good at Ravaging.

It's also front-loaded rather than sustained: Sweden comes out swinging hard turn 2-3 and tapers off from there, lacking the staying power most other adversaries have late. By the time its later escalations land, the game is usually already decided. The one level that gives it late-game teeth is level 5 (Mining Rush), which spawns a new town wherever a Ravage adds Blight to an adjacent townless/cityless land — swingy and situational, but "very spooky" if it triggers two turns running.

The stage 2 escalation replaces a Dahan with a town in any land where invaders now outnumber Dahan — bad specifically because it destroys a Dahan rather than just adding a town, which punishes Dahan-reliant spirits (Thunderspeaker, Eyes Watch from the Trees, River Surges in Sunlight's travel aspect) hard. It's also the one place on Sweden's card that references Dahan at all, and is largely avoidable by bunching Dahan into already-fully-explored lands once you know the drawn terrain type.

Because so much of Sweden's design is "hit harder, Blight more," defend-based play is specifically punished — the town/city damage bump (3/5 instead of 2/3) means almost no minor power fully blocks a stacked Ravage. Partial defense still matters, though: dropping total damage below the extra-Blight threshold, even without fully zeroing it, still prevents the second Blight.

Early vs. late danger

Danger peaks early — turn 2-3 onward — and fades once you're past the opening, inverting the usual adversary shape of ramping up over time. Skip effects are excellent here: Sweden has no loss condition besides Blight, so a spirit that can skip every Ravage is only constrained by running out of invader cards, which takes a long time. Push/control effects that move explorers into lands you intend to skip or ignore work the same way — deal with the easy lands, ignore the one bad land, let it get cleaned up later. Fear-rushing also works well since Sweden's fear cards are front-loaded, making it feasible to race a Fear win alongside partial control. In multi-adversary games, Sweden is rated one of the strongest possible secondary adversaries specifically because its lack of sustain gets shored up by whatever else is on the table — and stacking its damage boost onto another adversary's tougher invaders compounds badly (e.g. paired with Habsburg's added toughness).

What counters it, and why

  • Heart of the Wildfire: one of the best matchups in the game against Sweden, possibly the best — Sweden has none of the "resist 1-damage control" tech that other adversaries use to blunt Wildfire's single-damage clearing (compare France's double-explorer or England's extra health). Wildfire can clean explorers, self-manage its own Blight via ignite, and eventually clear even a heavily built-up land — but it requires real know-how, since a misplayed Wildfire can trigger its own Blight explosion and lose immediately.
  • Relentless Gaze of the Sun: favorable for similar reasons — exploits the same lack of control-resistance, plus has a skip effect in its base kit.
  • Finder of Paths Unseen: "the one control spirit to rule them all" against Sweden. Sweden has no way to resist control effects once invaders are down, and no extra Build/Explore mechanisms beyond its base rate, so Finder can let invaders stockpile, push them off the board, then land one big nuke or Ravage-denial hit — often clearing Sweden's board faster than against almost any other adversary, even at high difficulty. Sweden's bonus Ravage damage backfires here: a stacked land only pays off if Sweden actually gets to Ravage it, and Finder isn't defending that land anyway.
  • River Surges in Sunlight and other push/control spirits: rated better than average vs. Sweden — can push towns/explorers before they build up, and only need a couple of well-timed skips since an occasional double-Blight isn't fatal if managed elsewhere.
  • Grinning Trickster Stirs Up Trouble: its Strife-cancel and skip-adjacent tools let it dodge Ravages outright, denying Sweden's damage math entirely.
  • Vengeance as a Burning Plague: converts the adversary's own escalation into fuel, since its playstyle wants blight rather than fearing it.

Weaker matchups: Ocean's Hungry Grasp struggles because its usual strategy of writing off inland lands (accepting one Blight) breaks down when Sweden's inland lands threaten two Blight plus a new town. Shifting Memory of Ages struggles too — a slow-starting spirit with a near-useless early innate doesn't get the ramp time it needs before Sweden's fast pressure lands; the same weakness applies broadly to any beast-late-game/weak-early spirit (Serpent Slumbering Beneath the Island, Wounded Waters Bleeding). Towering Roots of the Jungle and other vitality-generating spirits are notably weak here, since Sweden hits hard and often enough to strip vitality tokens as fast as they're added while Blight lands regardless. Blight-removal minor powers are also weaker than they look — they work well against adversaries with one reliable Ravage source (Russia's stacked explorers, where removing Blight every turn functions like a skip), but against Sweden the same land keeps re-adding Blight every Ravage, so removal is purely reactive.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming fixed "Defend N" values still hold — Sweden's boosted town/city damage (3/5 at level 3+) means old breakpoints frequently fail to fully stop a Ravage; mark the new numbers physically so they aren't forgotten mid-game.
  • Leaning on blight-removal minor powers as your main answer — you never get ahead of the rate against a land that keeps re-adding Blight every Ravage.
  • Forgetting that only Ravage-sourced Blight triggers level 5's Mining Rush — Blight from events does not, which matters for spirits like Heart of the Wildfire that self-inflict Blight.
  • Investing in Defend-boost cards generically — the new damage baseline means most standard "defend X" cards no longer fully cover a stack, especially if terrain-restricted.

How the engine ticks

France's difficulty runs backwards from most adversaries — its higher-level effects (4, 5, 6) aren't too punishing on their own, but the low-to-mid levels are what actually trip players up. There's no single definitive counter-strategy; what matters is whether a spirit can handle Towns directly, prevent or answer multiple Explorers landing in the same land, and mobilize Dahan ahead of the Slave Rebellion special event, which can wipe multiple Towns in one stroke if Dahan are already in position. A recurring pattern: a spirit that clears France's level 3 generally goes on to have a comfortable time afterward — the early-mid game is the real gate, not the late game.

Early vs. late danger

Front-loaded — unanswered Explorers become extra Towns, and multiple Explorers per land compounds quickly if not addressed. Turns 1-3 are the highest-leverage window. Despite the reputation, it's an overall fun and trackable challenge rather than an excruciating one, even at higher difficulty.

What counters it, and why

  • A Spread of Rampant Green: won at level 5 (would likely have taken level 6 barring one poorly-timed event). Its core strength is stopping Towns from being built in the first place; higher levels of Creepers Tear Into Mortar are strong, especially paired with Stem the Flow of Fresh Water, and Fields Choked with Growth gives it decent Dahan mobility for the Slave Rebellion window.
  • Sharp Fangs Behind the Leaves: also won at level 5, fell short at level 6. Core plan is converting presence to beasts and hitting Ranging Hunt every turn — effective against both France's multiple Explorers and its Towns generally; Prey on the Builders helps, Too Near the Jungle is useful when you can afford to pick off an Explorer to stop a second Town forming next turn, and Terrifying Chase helps move Dahan into position.
  • Thunderspeaker: won at level 6. Dahan mobility combined with Manifestation of Power and Glory to wreck lands is the core engine; drawing fast Dahan-movement cards (Call to Isolation, Enticing Splendor) amplifies this further. Sudden Ambush and even normally-overcosted Lightning's Raging Storm both get notably better value here since France stacks multiple Explorers per land, letting a single effect clear more than one invader at once.

Weaker matchups: Bringer of Dreams and Nightmares and Heart of the Wildfire both fell at level 5 in reported play. Ocean's Hungry Grasp and Keeper of the Forbidden Wilds both lost as low as level 3 — surprising given their usual strength elsewhere, and flagged as spirits that "should" do better here but didn't. Finder of Paths Unseen is speculated to struggle, since its style tends to leave invaders on the board rather than clearing them, which is a poor fit against France's building pressure.

Common mistakes

  • Playing a spirit whose kit scatters or relocates Invaders rather than destroying them — delay is specifically punished by compounding Town-count.
  • Treating France as a slow-burn adversary and stalling — its real pressure is front-loaded, not backloaded.
  • Underestimating the low-level escalation because the high-level effects sound scarier on paper — the early-mid game is the actual test.

How the engine ticks

Russia's core threat is explorer volume and damage rather than city or town density — its explorers individually hit harder than normal and swarm the board. Defend is comparatively weak here, since the explorer swarm's increased damage makes blocking less efficient than actually removing or stranding explorers. Control matters more than usual — minimizing the risk of additional Ravages and protecting beast tokens from being wiped — and combos well with any "damage to all invaders" ability, since explorers cluster densely. Mobile, far-reaching spirits also matter more than usual, since Russia's explorers move around the board aggressively rather than sitting still.

Early vs. late danger

Russia's escalation triggers land on alternating turns at high levels, which can paradoxically make top-difficulty Russia more manageable for some decks than mid-level Russia. Its difficulty has a real variance/gimmick component distinct from England's or Sweden's: certain events and fear cards at high difficulty can chain into effectively instant-loss states, so part of "beating Russia" is just avoiding those swingy interactions rather than out-planning a predictable engine.

What counters it, and why

  • Lure of the Deep Wilderness: one of the most direct hard-counters to any adversary in the game applied to Russia specifically. Russia's strategy of adding more high-damage explorers is neutralized outright because Lure's innate abilities are built around killing explorers specifically — the more and higher-damage explorers Russia adds, the more fodder it hands Lure.
  • Many Minds Move as One: pairs especially well with Lure here — its beast tokens protect Lure's own beasts from Russia's explorers (which don't damage beasts), and the added beasts further feed Lure's explorer-killing engine.
  • Shroud of Silent Mist: "damage to all" abilities rated highly useful against Russia's explorer swarm, even at the cost of somewhat lower energy income.
  • Fear-generation spirits generally: many fear cards manipulate or remove explorers, and Russia's buildings usually aren't the core threat, so a fast Terror Level 3 win is sometimes reachable — helped along by the explorers themselves contributing fear as they're dealt with. Caveat: at level 5+, fear generation can backfire, since drawing an extra invader card at a bad moment (from hitting a fear threshold) can be catastrophic against Russia's escalation.
  • Isolate effects: a good release valve when overwhelmed by explorers, buying a breather without needing to clear the whole swarm at once.

Mixed matchup: Stone's Unyielding Defiance benefits from Russia's extra Ravages and has no trouble destroying invader builds, since the explorer bonus damage feeds "Let Them Break Themselves" (which reflects/scales off damage taken). But Stone is weak against Russia's specific loss-condition mechanic — pulling Blight from the box (Russia's Blight-add mechanic) still kills any Beasts sitting in the Ravaging land regardless of Stone's defenses, which is enough that some experienced players don't rate Stone as a top Russia pick despite the surface-level synergy.

Common mistakes

  • Leaning on defend as a primary plan — boosted per-explorer damage makes blocking inefficient compared to removing or stranding explorers outright.
  • Rushing fear indiscriminately at high difficulty — pulling an extra Invader card at the wrong moment can be catastrophic when explorer pressure is already elevated.
  • Assuming Stone's damage-reflection synergy makes it a safe pick — it doesn't protect Beast tokens from Russia's Blight-from-the-box mechanic.

How the engine ticks

Scotland is often described as "mini England" or "England-Light" — a coastal-focused adversary whose difficulty is fairly direct rather than gimmicky: control the coast and you largely control the game. It's widely regarded as one of the best-balanced adversaries, rewarding a clear, telegraphed strategy without being especially swingy, notably staying fair even against Ocean's Hungry Grasp, a spirit that should counter it on paper.

The level-dependence of a given counter-strategy matters more for Scotland than for most other adversaries. At level 5+, its extra-Blight-to-ocean rule partly undercuts strategies (like Stone's) that plan to let invaders build up before knocking them down on the Ravage — the ocean still takes Blight from the card regardless of how well the land itself is defended, which specifically punishes the "stall and burst" approach.

Early vs. late danger

Most of Scotland's higher-level effects only matter at the Ravage step, so a fast spirit with strong board-presence-per-turn can shut Scotland down before its escalation ever comes into play — preventing Ravages from happening at all sidesteps the design entirely. One solo Trickster game won by the Fast phase of turn 3, driven by an early "Let's See What Happens" event draw and a first Invader card that happened to match land 2, letting enough Dahan get shuttled in to wipe the land via Impersonate Authority plus Mob. Seeing the first Invader card match land 2 is generally a good sign for a spirit that can take out Cities early — it turns Scotland's usual land-2 threat into a turn-1 opportunity.

What counters it, and why

  • Downpour Drenches the World: can shut Scotland down hard — demonstrated in a full solo playthrough against Scotland at level 6.
  • Grinning Trickster Stirs Up Trouble: fast tempo lets it end the game before Scotland's Ravage-dependent escalation matters; one solo win at level 6 came by turn 3 via early Dahan mobilization into a matching land 2.
  • Stone's Unyielding Defiance: strong below level 5, where its usual let-invaders-build-then-knock-them-down plan works cleanly — but weaker at level 5+, since the ocean still takes Blight from the escalation card regardless of how well the land is defended.

Ocean's Hungry Grasp, despite being the obvious coastal-control pick, doesn't dominate this matchup as cleanly as its reputation suggests — Scotland is specifically noted as staying balanced even against Ocean, which is called out as impressive design rather than a weakness of Ocean.

Common mistakes

  • Playing reactively at the Ravage step instead of preventing Ravages from happening at all — most of Scotland's danger is specifically gated behind that step.
  • Treating a land-2 match on the first Invader card as bad news by default — for a spirit that can hit Cities early, it's an opportunity to wipe the land before it compounds.

How the engine ticks

Habsburg's loss condition only cares about blight taken off the adversary card itself — not blight on the board in general. At high levels it forces you to blight lands yourself just to manage escalation pace and keep towns from becoming too durable, which is a serious problem for any spirit that needs clean, blight-free land to function. Its signature move is gathering and consolidating towns into clusters, which is normally hard for a spirit to defend against.

Because the whole design revolves around blight-as-liability, the sharpest counters aren't just generically strong spirits — they're spirits that are indifferent to blight or actively rewarded by it, since Habsburg's own pressure becomes fuel rather than a cost for them.

Early vs. late danger

Habsburg is widely regarded as one of the best-designed adversaries in the game — it forces genuine tradeoffs on when and what to sacrifice, in ways that reinforce good play patterns rather than feeling arbitrary. Difficulty swings heavily on the player's own blight-management skill rather than on raw numbers. Level 4 is a hard mechanical gate: destroying towns on non-blighted land requires double attack power there. Some players find the level 6 loss condition the hardest point overall, since it becomes comparatively easy to trigger by accident.

What counters it, and why

  • Stone's Unyielding Defiance: the standout counter, called the most one-sided matchup encountered by one experienced player. Stone can force blight to come out of the box entirely, bypassing the card and nullifying Habsburg's core loss-condition mechanic outright. Because Stone doesn't care about blight at all, it can weaken Habsburg's towns freely without giving anything up, and Habsburg's signature gather-towns move actually helps Stone, since consolidated towns let it destroy more of them in one counterattack than it otherwise could. Reported as feeling "too easy" after repeated plays — a strong recommended pick if the goal is to decisively beat Habsburg.
  • Heart of the Wildfire and Vengeance as a Burning Plague: also do well here, though not to Stone's degree — both are blight-synergy spirits whose power curves benefit from the same blight pressure that cripples most other picks.

Common mistakes

  • Picking a spirit that needs clean, blight-free land without checking Habsburg's blight-liability mechanic first.

🔥Mechanics Deep-Dives

These are the mechanics that reward a deeper read than their card text suggests — keywords and systems that cut across every spirit rather than belonging to any one of them. Understanding when they're strong, and when they're a trap, separates a mechanically-fluent table from one that's just moving pieces.

Isolate — cuts logistics, not presence▸

Isolate tells Invaders that a land isn't adjacent to its neighbors for the purposes of their own logic — it doesn't remove anything from the board, it just severs the link Invaders use to Explore, and to move or coordinate off adjacency-dependent effects. That distinction matters: a land already holding Invaders keeps acting normally even while isolated, since Build doesn't care about adjacency — Isolate only stops new Invaders from arriving, not what's already there from doing its thing.

Where you put it matters more than how often. Isolating the land Invaders would land in creates a single dead end, but isolating the source — a City-containing land, or an Ocean acting as an Explore source — cuts off every land that depends on that source at once, protecting several destinations for the cost of one action. Isolating an Ocean specifically denies coastal Explore whenever that Ocean is the only source feeding it, which is a clean way to seal off a stretch of coastline in one move.

Used sparingly — once or twice a turn — Isolate reads as underwhelming, closer to a minor bonus effect than a real answer to a problem; that's the experience most players have with it in isolation. It becomes a genuine engine only at volume: spirits built to reapply it repeatedly, or a deliberate strategy of stacking Isolate-granting powers across a turn, compound into locking down Invader coordination across large parts of the board rather than one land at a time. Pairing heavy Isolate use with other tools that keep Invaders from ever reaching, Building in, or Ravaging an isolated land multiplies the effect further, since the isolation buys time for those other answers to land.

Isolate is also a precision counter to Adversary rules built on adjacency — rules that treat certain lands as connected regardless of distance, or that let Invaders move or act differently right after a Ravage, both rely on adjacency logic Isolate can simply turn off. It doesn't touch Dahan, who move freely into or out of isolated lands regardless of what Invaders are doing, doesn't restrict your own Powers targeting that land, and doesn't slow Blight — a Ravage inside an isolated land still cascades Blight to its neighbors exactly as if the land weren't isolated at all.

In short: think of Isolate as a targeting decision (source over destination) and a volume decision (occasional use is minor, sustained or stacked use is strong), not a blanket protection for a land — it only ever cuts Invader logistics, never Dahan, Spirit powers, or Blight.

Blight as a resource, not just a countdown▸

Presence, Dahan, energy, power cards, and Blight are all resources whose relative value shifts turn to turn — the skill isn't following a fixed priority list, it's deciding what's worth spending attention on right now. A single Blight token, absent something more pressing riding on it, usually isn't worth diverting a whole turn to prevent; over-protecting every point of Blight costs more tempo than the Blight itself would have cost you.

Certain situations do earn active intervention. Presence matters when losing it would break a specific plan — if that land's presence or sacred site is required to use a power you're relying on, that's a real cost, not just cosmetic loss. A land that's about to take multiple Invader actions in sequence (a Ravage now, then a Build, then another Ravage) is worth stopping early, since letting all of that resolve compounds into 3+ Blight from one chain — one or two actions now heads off a much bigger problem later. A land holding Dahan is worth defending too, since Dahan retaliation is one of the cheapest ways to remove Invaders going forward — unless the group is large enough that plenty will survive the Ravage anyway, in which case retaliation happens regardless and intervention is wasted effort.

Geography matters as much as the count: letting Blight concentrate into a corner of the map is worse than the same Blight spread thin, because a blighted region becomes a magnet for repeated future cascades — Blight begets Blight locally. And naturally, anything that risks the game outright, or pushes the Blight total toward flipping the Blight Card, always gets full attention.

The underlying cascade mechanic is what makes all of this matter: Blight added to a land spreads to an adjacent land, and if that neighbor is already Blighted, it cascades again from there — a single trigger can stack 3-4 Blight tokens through a chain reaction. Push this far enough and a board can reach a state where every land already carries Blight; at that point, adding Blight anywhere triggers an unstoppable chain that ends the game. By the time a double or triple cascade is forced, the presence in that region has usually already been wiped out too, so the same event that deals the Blight damage also strips away your ability to prevent the next round of Builds and Ravages there — the damage compounds on itself.

Different spirits value these resources differently, which is worth knowing when weighing tradeoffs: a spirit whose kit gets better presence placement specifically from reclaiming (rather than worse) can afford to reclaim more often than the norm, treating "presence on the board" and "progress on presence tracks" as two separate resources to juggle rather than one. A spirit whose main way of clearing Invaders runs through Dahan retaliation should weight Dahan survival well above what a typical spirit would. Recap: don't chase a clean board — spend Blight-adjacent resources deliberately, watch for compounding cascades and blighted clusters, and weigh presence/Dahan/energy/Blight against each other based on what your specific kit actually needs.

The Blight Card / Event Deck coupling▸

The Blight Card holds every Blight piece not currently on the island — Blight added to or removed from the island always comes from or returns to this shared pool, which is what makes the card function as a hidden pacing dial rather than a fixed number. It starts Healthy-side-up; once that side's pool empties, it flips to its Blighted side and refills to that side's own printed amount — if that pool also empties, the game is lost. Some cards (mostly from Jagged Earth) have a "Still Healthy" back instead of a Blighted one: the island stays counted as Healthy even after flipping, and emptying that pool draws a fresh random Blight Card and goes straight to its back rather than ending the game — so a single game can chew through more than one Blight Card in sequence. The flip is one-way: once a card has turned to its back side, it never turns back to Healthy even if Blight is later cleared from the island — the card represents crossing a point of no return, not a live health bar.

Running the Event Deck makes using a Blight Card mandatory, not optional, since a meaningful number of Events reference the island's Blight state directly to resolve. If you're playing without Events, the recommendation is to actively avoid thin Blight Cards — ones whose Blighted side carries only a small pool (around 2 Blight/player) or whose Blighted-side effect is a one-shot bonus rather than an ongoing one. Those cards were balanced assuming Events occasionally punish a Blighted island; strip Events out and that same card becomes a low-risk freebie that undercuts the tension the flip is supposed to create. Pull those specific cards from the pool before shuffling, or swap one out if drawn.

Solo play sharpens all of this: a Blight Card with only a 2-Blight-per-player Blighted pool is already considered especially punishing solo, since there's no group of players to split the response burden across a small buffer — if you draw one solo, swapping it for a different random Blight Card is a reasonable adjustment rather than playing it out as printed. Recap: the Blight Card's back side is the real difficulty knob, Events make it mandatory and add the punishment that offsets thin/beneficial Blighted sides, and solo play should treat a thin Blighted pool as a signal to swap rather than a challenge to embrace.

Digital client quirks (Steam app — not tabletop rules)▸

A handful of interactions play out differently in the Handelabra digital client than they do at a physical table, and they're easy to miss if you're used to the tabletop version. Isolate: the app doesn't apply it as a single fixed toggle — every nearby action that could care about adjacency prompts you individually for whether the isolated land still counts as connected for that specific action, so the same isolated land can be treated differently action to action within the same turn.

Tokens that can represent more than one thing at once — for example a spirit token standing in for both an animal and a badlands, which comes up with a couple of specific spirits and powers — work the same way: each action, you choose per-token whether it counts as that other category right then. This matters directly against Events or Adversary effects that target or remove a category like "animals" — simply don't let a token count as an animal for that check if doing so would expose it to removal.

Sacrifice-victory situations (winning and losing at the same moment) rely on the app's action resolution order: normally a full action resolves before win/loss conditions are checked at all, so an action that simultaneously pushes Fear to a winning threshold and also triggers a loss condition (Blight running out, an Adversary's extra loss trigger) resolves as a win, since both conditions are being evaluated off the same already-completed action.

Worth flagging as a general caution rather than a mechanic: the app's Undo button doesn't always cleanly reverse state that depends on hidden information — undoing after certain reveal-based effects can permanently desync a token's tracked state from what's shown on screen, so treat Undo as safe for simple misclicks but be wary of using it to back out of anything involving a hidden/revealed token. These are implementation-specific clarifications, not tabletop house rules — worth knowing if you play both formats so you don't misattribute an app quirk to a rules misunderstanding.

⚲Team Building

A strong multiplayer lineup isn't just several strong spirits at the same table — the best pairings work because one spirit's kit directly absorbs another's cost, or one spirit's growth engine directly feeds another's ceiling. Look for that kind of structural fit rather than picking spirits purely on individual strength.

Pairing Principles

Let One Spirit Soak the Blight Another Spirit Generates

Some spirits accrue Blight quickly as an unavoidable cost of doing damage; others have kits built specifically around generating or absorbing Blight faster than it accumulates. Pairing the two means the table can keep Blight away from the card-draw threshold for the whole game, which lets the Blight-heavy spirit swing freely instead of playing around its own downside.

Pair Reach with a Spirit That Needs Reach

A spirit whose kit extends another spirit's range or unlocks targets it couldn't normally hit turns a narrow, board-edge-only tool into something that threatens the whole map. Look for this shape whenever one spirit's specialty is "removal" or "control" but its native range is limited — a range-extending partner solves that limitation directly rather than asking the spirit to out-grow it alone.

Feed a Slow Spirit's Growth Engine Early

A spirit with a naturally slow wake-up or ramp can have that timeline compressed significantly if a teammate's kit is built around boosting Sacred Site or Presence growth. Committing a support-oriented spirit's early turns to accelerating a slow, high-ceiling teammate gets that teammate online well ahead of its solo schedule, which matters most against Adversaries or difficulties where a late power spike otherwise arrives too late to matter.

Notable Combos

Heart of the Wildfire + Stone's Unyielding Defiance

Both spirits interact with Blight from opposite ends, and together they can stack up enough Blight-absorption and generation to keep the island healthy — avoiding Blight card triggers — for a full game. The combo works because the pair generates and absorbs Blight faster than it can build toward the threshold, neutralizing the resource that would otherwise cap how aggressively either spirit can play.

Finder of Paths Unseen + Ocean's Hungry Grasp

Ocean's Hungry Grasp drowns everything on the coast but has no native reach inland. Finder of Paths Unseen extends that reach to targets Ocean normally can't touch, letting the pair drown problem cities sitting deep in the board's interior. It's a clean example of a range-extender turning a coast-only removal tool into a board-wide one.

A Spread of Rampant Green + Vital Strength of the Earth

Vital Strength of the Earth boosts Sacred Site and Presence growth, and A Spread of Rampant Green compounds that growth further — together they speed up Serpent Slumbering Beneath the Island's notoriously slow wake-up, pushing it past its otherwise-lengthy threshold well ahead of schedule. The pairing turns two support-leaning spirits into a growth engine that gets a slow, high-ceiling kit online far earlier than it would manage solo.

✦Tips & Tricks

Smaller-grain lessons that don't rise to core strategy but repeatedly trip up tables — sequencing tricks, easy-to-miss rules interactions, and a handful of digital-client-specific gotchas worth knowing before they cost you a game.

Build Around Your Innate, Not Just Your Card Text

An innate power is usually the strongest thing in a spirit's kit, but it only fires once you've accumulated the right mix of elements from cards played that turn — it isn't available turn one and needs real presence development first. Treat the innate's elemental threshold as a design target from early game onward, not a bonus that happens to trigger sometimes.

In the early game, prioritize three things in order: contain damage rather than trying to eliminate every threat, get presence off the card-plays track to uncover your reclaim ability and refill your hand, and pick up power cards that supply the elements your innate needs. Flashy card effects matter less than whether the card's element symbols close a gap in your threshold.

Pick Cards for Elements, Not Just Effects

Roughly half of a well-played hand's picks should be chosen for the element symbols on the left side of the card rather than the printed effect. Missing a single required element can force an entire extra card play just to close the gap — an expensive tax in a turn where card plays are already scarce.

When two attractive options are on offer and only one supplies an element you're short on, take the element even if the other card's effect reads stronger. Early game, with more slack before thresholds matter, effect quality can win out as normal.

Let Blight Happen — Strategic Abandonment

Not every land is worth defending. A land with no Dahan and no Blight tokens yet is cheap to sacrifice: building it up to survive a Ravage costs more in cards and presence than the land is worth. At lower difficulties, an abandoned land typically won't Ravage again for several turns, buying time to focus your actions elsewhere.

Learn to read a land's cost before committing to it — Dahan present, Blight already clustering, or a sacred site at stake all raise the price of losing it. Letting something go should be a deliberate calculation, not an accident.

Let Dahan Guard the Isle

Dahan defense is a threshold effect, not a linear one, and the difference between two and three Dahan on a land is stark. One Explorer against two Dahan on the same land plays out as: the Explorer builds a town, the Ravage kills one Dahan, and the surviving Dahan destroys the town — net result is one Explorer, one Dahan, and one Blight left standing.

The same Explorer against three Dahan resolves very differently: the Ravage still kills one Dahan, but the two Dahan who survive destroy both the town and the remaining Invaders — net result is zero Invaders, two Dahan, and only one Blight. Three (or more) Dahan stacked on a land is effectively self-sufficient defense against Explorers; if you're short on cards or time and Blight there isn't urgent, let the Dahan handle it and spend your actions elsewhere.

Reclaim Feeds Your Engine

Clearing your card-plays track doesn't just refill your hand — it unlocks reclaiming cards you've already played, which is often how you get your best elemental sources (energy- or Dahan-generating cards especially) back into rotation. Treat getting to reclaim as a mid-game priority in its own right, not an incidental side effect of running out of plays.

Never Forget Your Starting Power Cards

Starting powers are deliberately chosen for good elemental spread across a spirit's kit, and that spread is usually exactly what an innate's threshold depends on. When you acquire a major power and need to forget something to make room, forget a minor power you picked up mid-game instead — never one of your originals.

If you want to keep your active hand fully intact, you can also forget powers sitting in your discard pile rather than ones you're currently holding. Save major-power upgrades for later in the game when you actually need a big effect, rather than rushing one early at the cost of your elemental base.

Three Ways to Close Out a Win

Island-control victories aren't the only path. Repeatedly reclaiming cards just to keep firing your innate power can carry an entire endgame on its own for spirits with cheap, fast reclaim access — though how efficient this is varies by spirit and by how steep the innate's reclaim cost is.

Running out the Fear deck is a separate, viable win condition even when island control is slipping: focus on destroying towns and cities to generate Fear, and keep the Blight pool from running dry in the meantime. This path has its own clock, though — running out the Invader deck first is a loss, so don't stall on it indefinitely.

Match Spirit Complexity to the Table

Spirits vary enormously in how much they demand of a player — some are straightforward offense-first kits good for a first game, others require heavy spatial planning, careful tempo/timing judgment, or building your own strategy from scratch as the game unfolds. Picking a spirit that matches your (or a new player's) current comfort level matters more for a good first experience than picking the "best" spirit in the abstract.

When teaching someone new, favor a low-complexity spirit with a clear, singular gameplan over one whose strength depends on juggling several interacting systems at once — the latter is more rewarding once the rules are second nature, not before.

Digital Client: Per-Action Choices Aren't Persistent Settings

Digital (Steam app) only. Several interactions ask you to decide fresh every time rather than setting a fixed state once. Whether an Isolated land still counts as connected to a neighbor is decided per action, not fixed for the rest of the game. Whether a token that can count as more than one thing (for example, a spirit token that can count as an animal, or as both animal and badlands) counts as one type or another is also chosen per use — this matters against effects that remove or target a specific token type, so don't let a token count as something exploitable if you don't have to.

Digital Client: A Win and a Loss Can Land Simultaneously

Digital (Steam app) only. Normally an action fully resolves before the game checks win/loss conditions, but a single action can still trigger both at once — for example, an action that pushes Fear to a winning threshold at the same moment it also runs the Blight pool out or trips an adversary's extra loss condition. If your game ends in an apparent draw-like result, this simultaneous-trigger case is usually why, not a bug.

Digital Client: Played, Thresholded, and Used Are Three Different Things

Digital (Steam app) only, though the underlying rule is universal. "Played" means you paid the card's cost. "Threshold met" means you had the right elements at end of the Spirit phase, checked independently of what happens afterward. "Used" means the card's effect actually happened in the Fast or Slow phase. You can play a card purely for its elements and never use it, and you can meet a threshold without ever using the card — for instance if the game ends before a Slow-phase power gets its turn. Seeing "threshold met" without "used" in your card history is normal, not a bug.

Digital Client: Watch the Undo Button Around Found Tokens

Digital (Steam app) only. In scenarios that hide face-down tokens on the board, using Undo after a token gets found has been known to permanently lose that token's found status — the icon stays on the land, but the game no longer counts it as found. Avoid undoing past a token discovery if you can resolve the mistake another way instead.
