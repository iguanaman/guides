Islands

Exploration boards (bought via the Sailing/Explore Island actions, matched to the ship symbol you own) are the game's main income multiplier — but "which island is best" is the wrong question. Every board is really an investment decision with a Viking/tile cost up front and a repeating income + one-time VP payout after, and the same board can be excellent taken in round 2 and mediocre taken in round 5. Three variables matter more than the printed numbers: tileability (can your actual tile production fill this board's shapes without waste — a board that looks strong on paper but demands tiles you can't generate efficiently underperforms its ceiling every time), timing/compounding (income earned in round 2 gets 5+ rounds to compound into more actions and tiles; the identical board taken in round 6 only pays once or twice — a weaker board available now regularly beats a stronger board available later), and bonus-tile access (does this board's bonus squares feed an engine you're already running, or hand you resources you have no route to use). A board's headline bonus is also often gated behind covering blank/negative squares first — net value after that hidden cost is regularly much lower than the printed maximum suggests.

Don't evaluate an island in isolation from the alternative use of the same Vikings: filling an island costs far more than it looks like on the box (the acquisition action, plus the board's own -1 penalty squares, easily nets -20 to -50 points before a single income tile pays for itself), so a board is only worth taking if you can actually feed it tiles quickly — a half-filled island is a bad investment regardless of face value. Two islands is the modal target in a strong game (one or three both also work; four is rare because there aren't enough good boards or spare Vikings), and the alternative isn't nothing — longhouses and a strong home-board fill compete for the same Vikings and tiles, so weigh an island against building a third longhouse or just finishing your home board, not just against other islands. Norwegians' pigs/animal sheds/cheaper knarrs make a deliberate zero-island build viable (not just a fallback) by replacing islands' unique role as red-tile and bonus-resource suppliers — a legitimate archetype, not just a beginner's missed opportunity.

Two-Viking Islands

A 2V board's real minimum cost isn't 2 Vikings — it's 5 (1V to gather stone for the mountain strip, 2V to build a Knarr, 2V to explore), unless you pay the Knarr's 5-hacksilver anytime-buy price instead. That reframes the choice: it's not "1V island vs. 2V island," it's "commit 5 Vikings now for a 2V island" vs. "wait for spare hacksilver, pay 2 Vikings later, and take a 1V island instead." Since 1V islands are also richer in placeable bonus tiles, the default lean is toward 1V unless a specific 2V board's payoff clearly justifies the premium. The counterweight: 2V boards generally have steeper income lines (often one filled step per income increase, vs. two on the home board), so an early 5-Viking buy can out-earn a late, cheap 1V pick purely through extra rounds of compounding — the verdict varies board by board, not as a blanket rule.

Three-Viking Islands

The six 3V boards split sharply into two tiers by impact. Tierra Del Fuego and Baffin Island (available earlier) are solid, moderate-value picks; Cork, Labrador, Waterford, and Newfoundland (available only from round 5&ndash;6 on) are "monster" boards worth 60&ndash;80+ points each once their unlock conditions are trivially easy to hit &mdash; which they usually are by round 5&ndash;6, because a diligent player already has the sheds/ships/longhouse/bonus-resources banked from earlier exploration that the late boards' bonuses key off. Solo, you structurally can't get more than two of the four monsters and only one will actually bonus, since taking one in round 5 blocks your own 3V action in round 6 &mdash; so priority is skip Cork, take Waterford; or pivot to Labrador + a round-7 Newfoundland.

Artisan Sheds

Every player starts with one randomly-assigned, double-sided shed board — you only ever build out one side of it. The obvious-looking side is wrong more often than you'd expect, because "which bonus looks flashiest" isn't the question that matters. Evaluate each pair on four things: (1) how cheap and early the side is to activate, given your opening resources and occupation cards; (2) points-per-square on its build track, i.e. how efficiently filling it converts into score; (3) how much action-saving its ongoing bonus generates per round, versus just adding to a pile you may not use; and (4) what you have to spend or sacrifice to turn it on, and whether that resource is one your engine is already drowning in or genuinely starved for. None of these are fixed verdicts — a normally-weak side can become correct when an exploration-board bonus, a starting occupation card, or an early animal windfall removes its usual cost problem. Read your drawn occupations and early tableau signals (what resources are you naturally producing? what path are you leaning toward — see Strategy Paths) before committing, and once you've sunk Vikings or resources into filling one side, don't chase the other; sunk-cost reversals mid-game rarely pay back the actions spent getting partway there. Occupation Cards

Score = community rubric (How Likely to be played 0-4, + How Strong when played 0-5; 0 = discard on sight, 9 = broken). Click a header to sort.

Norwegians adds a standing option, available any time you're allowed to play an occupation card (not just via the Tutor card): discard it instead for a flat VP token — 4-point tokens first (only 2 exist), then 3-point (6 exist), then unlimited 2-point once those run out. That scarcity means an early discard nets more than a late one, so the option quietly gets worse as the game goes on. It's best used as a release valve for a card that's genuinely dead in your build (no engine to plug into, no realistic window left to play it) rather than a default line — a marginal-but-synergistic card is usually still worth more played than cashed in, and reflexively discarding for guaranteed points is a commonly-cited overcorrection. The rubric scores below implicitly price this option in: a card scoring nothing purely by playability is a live discard candidate the moment it stops fitting your plan.

Reading the scores: a few patterns explain most of why a card lands where it does. Cards that need an early play to earn a slow trickle payoff (a recurring per-round bonus you only benefit from for a few rounds if drawn late) tend to score lower, since the timing risk is real — the rare exceptions are cards whose one-time effect is strong enough on its own that the trickle is a bonus, not the point. Cards that reward hoarding a tile in supply rather than banking it toward your build immediately score worse, since unplaced tiles don't score at all. And cards that key off green/blue tiles generally outscore ones keyed to red/yellow/raw-resource tiles, simply because green/blue are the tiles you're already trying to accumulate for their own sake — a red/yellow-keyed card can still be strong, but usually only when you're already resource-starved in that specific category. When judging a card the table doesn't make an easy call on, those three questions (does it pay off immediately or need trickle; does it want you hoarding a tile; does it reward tiles you already want) get you most of the way to a verdict without needing the full rubric.

General & Beginner Advice

▾

The board looks overwhelming — 60-plus action spaces, dozens of tile types, a home board full of negative squares — but that density is actually what keeps the game readable. Because the spaces are so differentiated rather than a handful of generic "get resource X" slots, you can rule out most of the board at a glance once you know your plan, instead of weighing all of it every turn. The flip side: don't assume a space will still be open just because it usually is — the ghost Viking from last round can be sitting on exactly the spot you wanted.

Every Viking action is really being judged against a running average, even if you never write the number down. Across a full solo game you get roughly 57-63 total Viking-actions, and a 100-point game needs those actions averaging around 3 points each; push toward 200+ and the bar rises to something like 4-4.5 points per action. Early actions naturally fall short of that — a round-1 action netting +2 isn't a mistake, engines haven't compounded yet — but you need to claw the average back up with +5 to +8 actions by the mid-late game once bonus tiles and upgraded engines come online. Use this as a pacing check, not a per-action rule: if you're several rounds in and still mostly landing +2-3 actions, that's the signal to pivot toward whatever's underdeveloped, not a reason to keep grinding the same line.

  • Don't worry about food in rounds 1-2. The harvest phase alone covers the first couple of rounds. Spending early actions on food-conversion spaces is opportunity cost you don't need to pay yet — the scarce resource early is action-space time, not food.
  • Don't stockpile resources without a specific plan for them. Wood, stone, and silver sitting unused earned no return on the action spent gathering them. The one exception is a targeted buffer — e.g. holding 1-2 wood in reserve when you're about to hunt or whale with two matching weapon cards, since that specific pairing makes success likely enough to be worth pre-paying for. Otherwise, spending down a pile to secure a strong bonus now beats holding it for a hypothetical better moment — a stockpile doesn't score by itself, and a bonus claimed a round earlier is usually worth more than the flexibility of sitting on resources.
  • Push for an exploration board in the first half of the game. Islands generally return more per action than continuing to build out the home board — a full ideal home-board push (covering every negative and chasing the income curve turn by turn) nets only around 9 points more than largely ignoring it, and that thin margin evaporates the moment your tiling execution isn't perfect. Once you take an island, commit — get full value from it before splitting attention back to the home board, rather than half-developing both at once. You don't strictly need multiple islands to score well, either: a build that instead leans hard on houses, animals, or a crafting engine can rival a multi-island score, provided your occupation draws actually support it — treat "how many islands" as a question your cards and turn order answer, not a fixed target.
  • Buy ships with idle silver instead of building them. If you have the silver and don't need it for anything else right now, buying converts otherwise-dead cash straight into a tile. Building the same ship instead spends actions and resources that could go toward something else. This is really "buying an action" — especially true of knarrs and longships, where the per-use payoff is high. The exception is round 1: building your first ship with actions rather than silver gets an engine (whaling, pillaging, exploring) running a full round earlier than buying it later would, which is often worth the up-front action cost if you're committing hard to that engine.
  • Spend income the round you get it. Silver gained at the start of a round can often combine with tiles already in hand to unlock more bonuses that same round, rather than sitting banked for later. That said, don't undercount silver you're deliberately not spending yet — leftover hacksilver is easy to overlook on a tableau since it doesn't sit on the board like a tile, but it converts almost 1:1 to points, and the gap between a good score and a great one is often just how much silver quietly went unspent.
  • Rough income pacing for a long game: end of round 1 around 2-3, round 2 around 4-5, round 3 around 7-9 (or comparable bonus value). Treat these as a sanity check on ramp speed, not a formula — the point is judging early rounds by how fast your engine is accelerating, not by how full your board looks.
  • Let your weapon and occupation draws steer your strategy, not the other way around. You can't run every engine in one game — hunting without bows is a bad bet, for instance. Read what you're dealt each round and commit to the lines it supports rather than forcing a predetermined script. This extends to occupation cards specifically: judge a card by what engine it plugs into and how cheaply you can already feed its inputs, not by its printed point value in isolation — a conversion card that looks mediocre against manually-gathered resources can be excellent once an island or animal engine is feeding it for free. A card whose benefit only pays off later (a discount you won't use until round 5, say) is still often worth playing round 1 if the action cost is low then, since Vikings only get more valuable as the game goes on — banking the card beats risking never finding a good moment to play it.
  • Don't chase every dice-roll space's downside. Pillaging, hunting, trapping, and whaling all come with a die roll, but their lower-cost tiers pay out well on expectation, and weapon cards only improve the odds — they're never a hard requirement to attempt the action. Pillaging in particular is safe to gamble on even with no supporting resources; hunting, whaling, and snaring without any backup gear are worse bets and should generally wait until you have support for them. Whaling's failure result is worth calling out on its own: even a total whiff still returns a spear, wood, and an occupation card for a single Viking, which makes it one of the best "floor" spaces in the game and a cheap way to draw a card before a big action, independent of whether the whale-meat jackpot ever lands.
  • Uptrade the non-pregnant half of a breeding pair. The pregnant animal will give birth regardless, so upgrading its partner to a green tile loses nothing and usually adds more board value than leaving it in the stable.
  • Favor big actions (3V/4V) more than intuition suggests. The conventional read is that 1V/2V actions are simply more efficient, but 3-Viking actions draw an occupation card and 4-Viking actions let you play one — and many occupation cards effectively duplicate a cheap action's effect for free. Taken together, a big action often nets you everything a small action would have anyway, plus its own reward and a card, for a comparable or better Viking-to-point ratio. Look for a repeatable pairing of one 3V and one 4V space you can alternate round after round (whaling/mountain crafting, longhouse/ship-building, weekly market variants) rather than defaulting to the smallest available action every time.
  • Run at least two active engines, not just one. An active engine (pillaging, whaling, crafting, weekly market) needs a Viking action every time you use it, unlike a passive engine (income, bonus squares) that pays out for free once unlocked. Solo self-blocking makes a single active engine fragile — you can lock yourself out of your own best space for a round just by having used it already, with nothing to fall back on. A second active engine running in parallel means a blocked round still has a productive alternative, and stacking as many passive engines on top as you can find is what actually separates a good score from a great one.

Commonly Missed Rules

▾

Most rules mistakes below don't just cost a few points — they systematically make certain spaces look worse or better than they are, which distorts strategy evaluation for the rest of the game.

  • "From X get Y" is not a trade — you keep X. Action text that reads "from" means you retain the source tile and also gain the listed reward; only a silver arrow icon means you give up the source tile. A gold arrow (occupation cards only) means the trade can be repeated. Misreading a "from" space as a trade quietly cripples its value — the Norwegians' Weekly Market spaces are a common example, where you keep the sheep (and pig) while also banking the market benefit.
  • Anytime actions really mean anytime — including mid-resolution of another action. Buying ships for silver and playing blue occupation cards don't cost a Viking action at all, and can be interleaved into the middle of a different action's resolution (e.g. using a cheap occupation upgrade to convert a resource you just received into what a bonus tile needs, before that action even finishes). With only 57-63 total Viking-actions in a game, rushing a ship early by spending actions on it is expensive next to just buying it later with anytime silver — unless the early access itself unlocks something time-sensitive.
  • Tile placement is also an anytime action, separate from choosing your action. The polyomino packing puzzle for where a tile goes doesn't have to happen during your turn — solve it whenever. Treating "pick my action" and "solve my tile puzzle" as one blocking step is the single biggest cause of this game's reputation for slow turns; keep them separate and turns move much faster.
  • Adjacency rules differ by color and by board type. Same-color tiles (red-red, orange-orange, etc.) can never be orthogonally adjacent anywhere in your tableau — spend a silver as a placeholder spacer if you're stuck without one. Green tiles are the one exception, but only inside houses; on the home board or an exploration board, green tiles still need spacing like everything else. That asymmetry makes houses the efficient place to dump bulky green tiles (e.g. filling a long house with oil), while green placement elsewhere still needs the same discipline as other colors. Ore additionally can never go in a house — it always needs open board space, so budget for that if you're running an ore-heavy plan.
  • The upgrade chain goes further than it looks. Animals are red-tier goods and can be upgraded into green tiles just like any other red good — just not your active breeding pair, since an upgraded animal stops breeding. Herbs (orange tier) can upgrade into pigs (red tier), which is easy to forget since most animals don't have an obvious orange precursor. Practical consequence: before spending actions to buy an upgraded-tier good directly, check whether the cheaper precursor tier is available first and upgrading it is more action-efficient — it often nets bonus goods along the way too.
  • Multi-slot resource rows are taken strictly left to right. You can't snipe a specific resource (say, the coin slot) out of a mountain strip — you take from the left end inward. Visiting a high-cost mountain-strip space early mostly nets abundant, low-value lumber and leaves the better back-loaded resources for whoever visits next. Treat the mountain strip as a one-time early lumber grab, not a repeat destination — bonus tiles and board income are better resource sources afterward.
  • Emigration costs silver on top of the Viking cost, non-negotiably. The silver cost equals the current round number and can't be partially paid — no silver, no emigration. Forgetting this overstates emigration's point efficiency (a "13-point net" play is often closer to 8 once the round's silver cost and the ship's forgone value are counted), so only lean on emigration when you have a genuine ship surplus.
  • Final income is scored once — cashed in-round or recorded in the final box, never both. Double-counting it silently inflates income-heavy strategies by however much your final income was, and makes engine comparisons across games unreliable.
  • Aim to play at least a handful of occupation cards across a game. Forgetting to draw them mid-puzzle is an easy slip, and a low play count is usually a sign of under-using a resource that's meant to shape your strategy, not just add points.

Tile Adjacency (Blue & Green Tiles)

▾

Board coverage is the scoring system in this game, not a side effect of it — so how you place tiles matters as much as which tiles you get. Blue tiles can never be orthogonally adjacent to other blue tiles anywhere in your tableau, on the home board or an exploration board alike. Green tiles are the one color exempt from same-color adjacency — but only inside houses; on the home board or an exploration board, green still needs the same spacing discipline as blue, red, or orange. Forgetting this and letting two greens touch is reportedly the single easiest placement error to make and the easiest to spot in someone else's tableau — worth double-checking rather than trusting intuition, especially mid-puzzle when you're focused on fitting a shape rather than checking its neighbors.

  • Green is worth more than it looks, precisely because of that adjacency exemption. Blue and green tiles of the same footprint aren't equally easy to place: a blue tile needs green (or a coin, or a grey/ore tile) touching every one of its edges to avoid a wasted, awkward gap, while a large green tile can happily butt up against other greens with no penalty. That means a big green tile is systematically easier to slot into a tight board than a same-sized blue one — a source of green being undervalued by players who default to "blue is always better." A useful way to picture it: imagine the "shadow" of non-same-color space each tile realistically needs around it to pack cleanly — long, thin blue tiles need less shadow per space than square ones of the same size (fewer edges to neutralize for the same area), while for green tiles the relationship reverses — square green tiles are cheaper to neutralize than long thin ones. In practice this means don't reflexively reach for blue when a comparably-sized green tile is available; the green one may genuinely be the easier piece to place well.
  • Look for blue/green checkerboarding, not same-color clustering. The most efficient tilings alternate blue and green blocks against each other rather than grouping same-color tiles together and hoping the spacer rule works itself out. Two blue tiles ending up adjacent because you clustered them is a visible, avoidable waste — it means a green tile could have gone there instead and didn't. When you have a choice of which side of a green tile to butt a new blue tile against, pick the alignment that leaves a clean checkerboard rather than one that forces an awkward same-color corner.
  • Keep a mixed reserve of blue and green tiles, not just one color. Alternating blue and green as you tile your board is less a hard rule than a coverage-efficiency habit — relying on a single color pool risks getting stuck when the shapes on offer don't fit your remaining gaps. Holding a couple of small tiles in reserve alongside your large ones lets you plug awkward leftover spaces instead of being forced into a bad-fit large tile.
  • Tile size priority should flip over the course of the game. Early on, favor smaller, oddly-shaped tiles (the grey U- and E-shapes are called out specifically) because the goal is precise coverage of specific bonus-good icon squares — overshoot with a large tile and you waste the overhang. Late game, flip to favoring the largest tiles available, because by then the marginal value of a tile is in blanketing as many -1 penalty squares as possible in a single placement — coverage-per-tile matters more than precision once most of the easy bonus icons are already claimed.
  • Watch out for highly symmetric tiles. A tile like the Chest looks like an easy same-size match for something like Wool, but its symmetry cuts down the number of genuinely distinct ways you can orient and place it, and its own shape can "eat into" the adjacency space a less symmetric tile of the same size would leave usable. When two tiles of equal size are available and one is markedly more symmetric, expect the asymmetric one to be the easier, more flexible placement.
  • Resisting a green-to-blue upgrade is usually correct unless you have a concrete reason. Most illegal-placement problems with green tiles (i.e. you'd have two greens touching outside a house) can be solved by raiding or pillaging for a spacer tile instead of burning an upgrade action. Save the upgrade for when you already know what you need it for.
  • Houses are the efficient dumping ground for bulky green tiles. Since green-green adjacency is only legal inside houses, a long house or stone house is the natural place to unload oil, pelts, or other large green production rather than fighting for space on the home board — plan house capacity with this in mind if you're running a green-heavy engine (whaling, animal breeding, crafting).

Food Economy & Feeding Your Vikings

▾

Food problems in this game are usually a symptom, not the disease. New players see food-conversion spaces rated poorly and assume they need to patch a structural gap — flax to linen and beans to milk in the same game, orange-to-red conversions on top of that — when experienced players report barely thinking about food at all. The difference isn't a trick, it's about which sources you lean on, in what order, and whether you're producing food ahead of need or scrambling to gather it reactively round to round. A village that plans its feast a round or two ahead, rather than solving it turn-by-turn, is what separates a smooth mid-game from one where every round's plan gets derailed by a food shortfall.

  • Exploration islands are the real food answer, not the food-track spaces. Almost every early island (Shetland, Faroe, Iceland, Greenland) has at least one bonus tile that produces food (fish, milk) and is cheap to claim early, while the board is still empty. That single action nets food, victory points, and income or goods simultaneously — strictly better than spending actions purely on food conversion. In practice: explore early and food follows as a byproduct rather than a dedicated goal. Shetland is a partial exception — it produces no good red food of its own, so pair it with a follow-up island (Iceland or Greenland) for the actual fish/milk source, and lean on its beans and cabbage for orange-tier feeding instead of trying to eat its game meat.
  • Converting orange goods to red is usually a trap unless you have a specific opener built around it. The small early orange-to-red conversion spaces have poor tile-size-to-yield ratios. Get red goods instead from hunting, whaling, or island bonus tiles, where they come in worthwhile sizes — don't manufacture red food from orange when you can harvest it from a bigger source. The same logic caps how much early conversion you should do at all: flax-to-linen and beans-to-milk are each fine individually, but doing both in the same early game usually overextends the food budget you're trying to protect. Pick one, if your margin allows even one, and only after you've secured an island. If you do upgrade flax to linen, only do it when you don't need that flax for an upcoming feast — otherwise the "efficient" conversion just creates a food gap you have to scramble to patch a round later.
  • Lock in one reliable red-food source and stop worrying about food. Home-board mead, a fish or milk bonus tile, or a growing cattle herd are the common choices — any one of them, stacked with small oranges from harvest, covers feast requirements without ever needing to eat hunted game meat (which scores better used elsewhere, e.g. placed in a long house). Buying 2 fish for 1 coin is a fine short-notice top-up when you're briefly short, but treat it as a safety valve, not a plan.
  • Don't treat village growth as pure upside. Every Viking on your board is a recurring food tax, not a one-time cost — evaluate population growth against your actual ability to keep producing food round over round. Emigration is the primary release valve: it shrinks your village (fewer mouths at the feast) while converting a comparatively low-value ship into a much higher-value emigration score, at the cost of silver. That combination — points up, upkeep down, for a silver price — is worth prioritizing once you have ships to spare, but it competes with other silver sinks so it isn't free. It's rarely too early to take a first emigration once a ship has already delivered its main value (an island, a pillage run) — even round 2-3 is fine — but it isn't an auto-include on the last round either; weigh it against whatever else that action slot could do.
  • Build food capacity ahead of the population that will need it, not after. Reactively patching a feast shortfall round by round costs far more attention and far more inefficient action-spending than locking in a passive food engine (an island bonus, a long house food slot, a breeding herd) a round or two before you actually need the extra output. If you notice your village growing faster than your food sources, that's the moment to redirect an action toward feeding capacity — not the round you actually come up short.
  • Format changes your margin for error. All the above assumes the long (7-round) game. The short game has one fewer harvest phase, so the same food slack is tighter — expect real food pressure and less room for "inefficient" double conversions than in a long game.

Negative-Point Coverage (Home Board Tiling)

▾

Every player board starts printed with a field of -1 penalty spaces — a design device (from the same designer as Agricola) that turns tile-placement efficiency into the entire scoring system, not a punishing handicap bolted on top of it. On the home board alone, roughly 86 of its 135 spaces start as pure negative-point squares that must be covered just to break even, before any of the remaining income or bonus-good spaces can pay out. The whole early-to-mid game is really digging out of that hole: you cover penalty squares with buildings, animals, resources, and goods to cancel them one by one, and positive score only starts accruing once coverage outpaces what's still exposed. Framing it this way changes how you should read a slow-looking early board — it isn't behind, it's still paying down principal.

  • Income spaces are gated by everything southeast of them. Your income each round is set by the lowest-numbered income space still uncovered on your board, but to legally cover an income space you must first cover every space southeast of it. That forces a rough sequencing on your tiling — you can't just cover the -1s you personally find annoying, board geography dictates what becomes coverable next. Plan placement with this dependency in mind: filling low-value filler squares is sometimes a prerequisite for unlocking a much better income space, not a wasted placement. A rough pacing target for a strong long game: 2-3 income by the end of round 1, climbing to somewhere around 9 by round 4-5, and pushing toward the mid-teens by round 6-7 — falling well behind that curve for more than a round or two is the clearest sign your tiling sequencing needs attention, not your luck.
  • Don't over-invest in fully solving the home board in isolation. Chasing the theoretical ideal income curve and covering every last home-board negative square nets only a slim edge — something on the order of 9 points — over a much lower-effort approach that fills just enough to stay functional and otherwise prioritizes exploration boards and houses. That margin is also fragile: falling even a little behind the ideal curve for a round or two erodes most of the benefit, while tiles placed early on the home board are stuck there for good, so an awkward early placement can cost you extra actions later patching around it. This doesn't mean ignore the home board — it means don't treat "fully tiling it early" as inherently correct; weigh each placement there against what the same tile could do unlocking an exploration board or a house instead.
  • The swing from a first game to an experienced one is almost entirely about coverage and sequencing, not luck. First-game scores in the low 20s versus experienced scores around 100+ is a large jump, and it's attributable to more efficient board coverage and income sequencing — not different cards or dice. If your score feels stuck, the lever to pull is tiling discipline, not hoping for better draws.
  • Every new exploration board reintroduces the same penalty pattern. Island and expansion boards carry their own negative-point squares to pay down — budget actions for covering a new board's penalties the same way you did for your home board, rather than treating exploration as pure upside once you've claimed it. This is also why committing to an island rather than half-developing several at once matters: a board with its penalties only partly paid down is dead weight, while one you've pushed through to net-positive is pure profit for the rest of the game.
  • Uncovered negative squares compete directly with everything else you could place. It's easy to over-focus on a specific engine (animals, crafting, a favorite island) and leave stray -1 squares exposed on the home board that a small tile could have quietly cancelled. Coverage should be weighed against other placements every time you're deciding where a tile goes, not treated as cleanup for whenever you get around to it. A board that looks visually "ugly" — heavily blue, tiles clearly forced into odd corners — is usually a sign of exactly this: good tiles spent on upgrades or specific-shape needs rather than the best-fit coverage placement available at the time.
  • Sweep for unplaced resources before the game ends. Any tile-eligible resource still sitting unused after your final action is pure lost value — ore in particular can almost always still be dropped onto some open board space for free points right up to the last placement. Losing even a single point this way at the very end is a common, easily-avoided leak after a long game of careful coverage.

Solo Play

▾

Solo introduces one mechanical wrinkle that shapes strategy more than anything else: self-blocking. A ghost Viking occupies whatever space you used last round, so you can't simply repeat your single best action every round the way you might wish to — you're competing against your own recent turns for board space, not just planning in a vacuum. That single fact is why solo strategy leans so heavily on running multiple engines side by side rather than perfecting one.

  • Run at least two active engines specifically because of self-blocking. In multiplayer you might get away with one strong active engine (pillaging, whaling, a crafting loop) if opponents don't contest it; solo, you contest it against yourself every single round. Without a second engine to fall back on, a round where your main space is self-blocked becomes a wasted or badly compromised turn. Add a second active engine as soon as you can support it, and treat "what do I do when my main space is blocked" as a question worth answering before it happens, not after.
  • Self-check your actions against a rough silver-equivalent value target. A 200+ point solo game needs an average of roughly 4-4.5 points of value per Viking-action across the whole game (with ~57-63 total actions available); a solid 100+ game can get by on something closer to 3. Early actions naturally undershoot that — a round-1 action netting +2 isn't a mistake, it's normal, since engines haven't compounded yet — but you need to compensate with +5 to +8 actions by the mid-late game once bonus tiles and upgraded engines are online. Use this as a running sanity check on pace rather than a per-action rule: if you're several rounds in and still mostly landing +2-3 actions, that's the signal to pivot toward whatever's underdeveloped, not a sign you should keep grinding the same line.
  • Risk-free engines can compete with pillaging and whaling. A build leaning almost entirely on exploration boards plus houses (crafting, animal breeding, building) can reach elite scores while barely touching risk actions — one documented 192-point solo run took only two risk actions the entire game, and by the second one its downside was already mitigated by an unlocked weapon-card bonus. A zero-island build is viable too, particularly with the Norwegians expansion: pigs give a reliable red-food source without ever exploring, animal-market spaces are strong enough on their own to feed a full engine, and cheaper emigration plus a wider grey-tile pool (since you're not pulling tiles from island bonuses) round out the approach — one such run reached 182 without a single exploration board. Risk actions and islands both are one route among several, not mandatory ingredients — read your occupation and weapon draws and let them tell you which route is actually open to you this game, rather than defaulting to the same shape every time.
  • Prioritize action-free (passive) engines over one-shot resource grabs. A Viking action that unlocks a recurring bonus square — a mead, pig, cow, or weapon-card bonus on an island or long house — pays for itself every remaining round, which compounds over a multi-round game far more than an equivalent action spent on a single resource grab. This is why a strong game spends much of the mid-game unlocking bonus squares rather than just filling board space for immediate income. Chained commodity engines compound especially hard solo: an early island's bonus goods can unlock a second island's bonuses, which unlock a third's, until a single exploration action nets a large basket of goods the same round you claim the board — look for these chains rather than evaluating each island bonus in isolation.
  • Evaluate occupation cards by what engine they plug into and when they'll scale hardest, not by their point value in isolation. A card that trades a cow for a jewelry tile is only good if you're already breeding cows; a card that returns a Viking from a large action space is worth banking until you can chain several big actions in the same round, rather than playing it the first round it's available. A conversion card (wool+pelt+linen into a jacket, say) is worth evaluating by how cheaply you can already feed its inputs — mediocre if you'd have to manually gather them, excellent once an island or animal chain supplies them as a free byproduct. Where two similar starter cards compete (a flat resource-per-visit card vs. one that grants a different resource pair, vs. one that lets you upgrade a tile for a coin), rank them against what your specific engine actually needs rather than assuming the more common pick is the better one. Playing cards purely for their printed points, without checking whether they feed your existing production loop, is a commonly self-reported mistake — though a played card is essentially never wasted, since even an off-plan card usually still banks some points or a usable minor effect.
  • Front-load a cheap occupation play even if you won't use its effect for several rounds. A card whose benefit only matters later (e.g. a house-building discount you won't use until round 5) is often still worth playing round 1 if the action cost is low then — Vikings get more valuable as the game goes on, so committing one early to bank the card beats risking never finding a good moment to play it at all.
  • Don't hoard resources waiting for a better moment. Spending down wood, stone, or weapons to secure a strong bonus now is usually correct — a stockpile doesn't score by itself, and a bonus claimed a round earlier is typically worth more than the flexibility of holding resources in reserve.
  • Weigh strong-looking openers against their two-turns-later cost. Some of the highest-ceiling solo openers hinge on a specific early die roll or resource sequence that only pays off if a later roll also cooperates — treat these as having a clean abort point: if the qualifying roll misses, fall back to the next-best tile or line immediately rather than sinking further actions chasing the original premise. The same applies to any strong-looking single-island commitment: it's only worth pursuing if your tiling capacity can actually leverage what the island offers by the time you take it, not just because the island's bonuses look good on paper.
  • Plan the shape of your game, but expect to improvise the specific path. A rough plan beats no plan — production without a target consistently scores worse than production aimed at a specific engine — but rigid scripting fights the game's openness. Stay flexible about exactly which occupation cards and bonuses you lean on inside that plan; the goal is direction, not a fixed sequence of moves.
  • In the final round, sweep for loose unplaced resources — especially ore. Ore in particular can still often be dropped onto an exploration board for pure free points right up until scoring; leaving it in hand after your last action is a commonly self-reported way to leave a point or two on the table.

Ten scripted solo games, ordered easiest to hardest. Every pool follows the same combo logic (see the Occupation Cards tab): a bonus for an action you'll repeat anyway, recurring income keyed to that same trigger, and something that makes the trigger cheaper or more repeatable. 1. Wool for Winter — Sheep

▾

The flock came to you the way most fortunes do in the north: someone died. Your aunt's eleven ewes are now your eleven ewes, scattered across a wind-scoured hillside above the fjord, and the neighbors are already wagering how many you'll lose before the snow. 10. The Edge of the World — Exploration & Emigration

▾

The charts end in a scribe's shrug — a sea serpent, a guess at a coastline, a prayer in bad Latin. Good. You've buried two brothers in soil other men owned, and you're done enlarging other men's maps. Somewhere past the ice-line there are green shores no jarl has taxed, and you mean to spend your last strength getting your people onto them: every hull you own crammed with seed and stock, runestones in the ballast to mark the way for those who follow. They'll call the new land by your name or they'll call it nothing. Cast off.

Starter

Undertaker (b) — a silver for every runestone placed on an exploration board.

Occupations
  • Harbor Guard (B) — an immediate emigration, discounted per large ship; the fleet's retirement plan.
  • Steersman (A) — knarrs count as longships for far exploration and more.
  • Ship Builder (A) — 2 silver turns a whaling boat into a longship.
  • Longship Builder (B) — the discounted longship, down to 1 silver with three knarrs.
  • Boat Builder (B) — two free whaling boats at four large ships.
  • Ore Boatman (A) — an ore per longship, every round, straight onto the island boards.
  • Ship Owner (C) — whale meat for holding full boat sets.
  • Whaling Equipper (A) — oil per knarr, wood per whaling boat.
  • Sail Patcher (C) — wool as the third large ship launches.
Islands (A–D)
  • A — Lofoten — a fast-filling whaling-boat toehold.
  • B — Isle of Skye — the runestone-rich board.
  • C — Outer Hebrides — free stone and houses on the board.
  • D — Greenland — fast whale-meat and fish income.
Artisan shed

Icehouse — provisions for the crossing. See the Artisan Sheds tab.

Why these picks

Engine: the fleet is built, milked, and then spent — in that order. Ship Builder and Longship Builder assemble the big hulls cheap; Steersman lets every knarr sail where only longships go, putting far islands in reach of a merchant fleet with no dedicated longship needed; Boat Builder refunds two whaling boats the moment the fourth large ship launches. While it sails, the fleet pays rent: Whaling Equipper, Ship Owner, and Sail Patcher all read the harbor, and Ore Boatman drops an ore per longship onto your island boards every round — the islands fill themselves. Undertaker turns every runestone you plant out there into silver, and this lineup is deliberately rich in them. Then the last conversion: Harbor Guard emigrates with a discount per large ship — the Emigration path's rule applies here, a ship whose work is done converts straight to points. Four islands, one fleet, no way home.

Islands: Lofoten is the seed of the fleet this challenge is built around — cheap early income and a first whaling boat. Isle of Skye's runestones are silver in Undertaker's hand, and its wool/flax feed the crossing's stores. Outer Hebrides gives room to bank the fleet's excess resources without spending an action to build storage. Greenland jump-starts the fleet's provisions immediately, so ships aren't idle waiting on their own crew's food.

Shed: a fleet this size makes Icehouse's whale-meat activation trivial, and its green tile lands directly on the islands you're filling.

How This Campaign Was Built

▾

Reusable recipe for generating another set of 10 — rules first, then the deviations this set actually hit.

  • Challenge = 1 story paragraph + 1 starter (lowercase deck (a)/(b)/(c)) + 9 normal occupations (uppercase A/B/C; 3×3 table grid) + 4 islands (one per slot A/B/C/D) + 1 artisan shed side.
  • Occupation pool = fixed draw-replacement list — every draw picks from the 9; not a free swap across the whole deck.
  • Pool must be thematic AND synergistic — same-trigger chain: flat action bonus + recurring income per unit + cost discount/enabler, all keyed to one recurring action or resource; theme-only lookalikes fail.
  • Before drafting a pool, re-read that theme's own writeup on the Strategy Paths tab first — it names the actual action space the engine runs through (not just "breed the animal"), any side-hustle byproduct the strategy organically produces, and the strategy's own named weakness. Draft cards against all three, not just the breed/convert chain that's obvious from the theme alone.
  • A pool that only covers acquisition + upgrade-conversion is incomplete if the source strategy names a specific recurring action space (e.g. Sheep's Weekly Market: 3V wool-by-flock-size, 4V herb→pig) — include cards that pay out on that named space specifically, not just on owning/upgrading the animal.
  • Include the strategy's own named weakness as a design constraint, not an afterthought — if the writeup flags a structural problem (e.g. Sheep's green-tile flood: green can't sit adjacent to green, so pure sheep outpaces most boards' absorption), the pool needs a card or two that counteracts it (a blue/special-tile outlet), not just cards that make the flood worse.
  • Chain depth ramps: 2-deep chains challenges 1–3 → 3+-deep by 6–10; deck mix A-heavy early → B/C-heavy late (B/C hold the edge-case synergy pieces).
  • Starters: all 10 distinct, no repeats.
  • Normal cards: max 2 challenges per card; ~74 usable normals for 90 slots → some repeats forced, cap at 2 and spread them.
  • A lowercase-deck (a)/(b)/(c) card is still fair game as a normal-pool pick (not just as a starter) if it's the best thematic/synergy fit — show it with its actual lowercase deck letter in the pool list, don't relabel it uppercase to match its neighbors.
  • Island slots are physical, not tiers: the exploration boards come in four groups (A/B/C/D), each group holding several double-sided boards; a challenge lists exactly one board per group, side 1 (its starting face) — never a tier label like "cheapest" or "monster," and never a side-2 board (only reachable by flipping mid-game, not choosable at setup).
  • Source of truth for which boards belong to which group and what side 2 is: .campaign/islands.csv — check it before assigning, don't infer groupings from the Islands tab's own 1V/2V/3V framing, which is a different, unrelated grouping.
  • Pick islands by bonus squares first: boards whose surround-bonuses feed the challenge's engine (both sheep-bonus boards → sheep challenge; ore boards → forge; runestone boards → Undertaker), then by income type, only then by flavor.
  • Breeding-bonus boards (Faroe's sheep) go to the challenge whose pool can rush the unlock — their value decays a generation per round of delay. Boards only reachable via a flip (Orkney's cow, Wexford's horses) can't be used at all under the side-1-only rule.
  • Island variety: with only 11 usable (side-1) boards across 40 slots, most boards repeat 3–6× — that's expected, not a deviation; don't force artificial variety past what the pool supports.
  • Rare/unusual boards (non-flipping, harder to execute, e.g. Tierra Del Fuego) get used sparingly — 2 uses across 10 challenges, not defaulted into an easy/early challenge just to fill a slot, and spaced apart rather than clustered in adjacent challenges.
  • Sheds: 12 sides, 10 challenges, no side repeated; drop the 2 worst fits — here Hermit's Hut + Whaling Hut (guide rates both priciest/narrowest).
  • Difficulty ramps simple→complex in this priority order: combo depth, island execution difficulty, shed activation cost.
  • Deviation hit: several famous combo pieces (Cattle Breeder, Storeman, Clerk, Ironsmith, Refugee Helper, Patron) missing from the filtered card list — chains rebuilt from surviving cards, not copied from community lists.
  • Deviation hit: an earlier draft assigned islands using the Islands tab's 1V/2V/3V cost tiers as if they were the A/B/C/D groups — wrong; A/B/C/D are physical board groups per .campaign/islands.csv, unrelated to Viking cost. Re-derive from the CSV every time, don't reuse a prior campaign's groupings from memory.
  • Deviation hit: an earlier draft for challenges 2–10 covered only the breed/upgrade chain obvious from each theme's name, without re-reading that theme's Strategy Paths writeup — missed Sheep's Weekly Market engine and green-tile weakness (see above), Hunting & Whaling's guidance that it's a supplement/card-fishing archetype not a primary fleet-build, Cattle's named Foreign Trader/Clerk upgrade-chain card, Horse's explicit cattle bolt-on via market grain, Longhouses' food-engine payoff (not just cheap stone), and Raiding & Pillaging's "pillaging beats raiding, ore is pillage-fuel" rule. Re-read the source writeup and rebuilt each pool against it once this was caught.
  • "Overseas Trade" (challenge 7) has no dedicated Strategy Paths writeup in this guide — it was synthesized from the Occupation Cards research on Clerk/Foreign Trader-style pre-upgrade chains instead. If replacing it in a future set, prefer one of the guide's 9 actual named paths (Cattle, Sheep, Horse, Pig, Hunting & Whaling, Raiding & Pillaging, Ore Crafting, Longhouses, Emigration) so every challenge has a real writeup to draft against.

House Rules

Optional variants, not official rules — everything here changes the base game in some way. Click a section to expand it.

Face-Up Occupation Market

▾

Replaces blind drawing from the occupation deck with a visible, choosable market — aimed at cutting the game's card-draw randomness without removing the deck entirely.

  • Setup: keep the occupation deck face-up instead of face-down, and lay the top 3 cards out next to it, so 4 total are visible at any time (the 3 laid out, plus the new top card of the deck itself).
  • Drawing: when you'd normally draw an occupation card, take any one of the 4 visible cards instead of the top of the deck blind. Immediately refill the market back up to 3 laid-out cards from the top of the deck.
  • Discarding: at the same time you draw, you may also discard any of the 3 laid-out cards (not the deck's own top card, and not a replacement card that just refilled the market). Discarded cards are out of the game, same as a normal discard.
  • Why: a large share of this game's variance comes from which occupation cards you're dealt and when — see Tips → General & Beginner Advice on reading your draws rather than forcing a script. This doesn't remove that skill test, it just lets you choose which problem to solve instead of being handed one at random, and gives a way to actively cull cards that don't fit your board instead of being stuck holding or blind-discarding them.

Fixed Die Results

▾

Replaces all four dice-roll actions with fixed results equal to their expected value over up to 3 rolls (no take-backs), removing dice variance entirely while keeping each action's original risk profile.

  • Hunting Game (d8, want low): always resolves as a 3.
  • Whaling (d12, want low): always resolves as a 4.
  • Raiding (d8, want high): always resolves as a 4.
  • Pillaging (d12, want high): always resolves as a 5.
  • Why 4/5 and not 5/7: the unmodified expected value for the want-high dice is closer to 5 (raiding) and 7 (pillaging), but this assumes the swing-value weapon draws you'd get from the base game's random weapon deck. Paired with the Free Weapon Choice house rule below (which guarantees your best sword each round), those baseline numbers overshoot — the 4/5 values rebalance raiding and pillaging back down to a comparable difficulty once the weapon-luck component is also removed.
  • Why fix at all: raiding and pillaging are otherwise the game's biggest swings of pure luck — see Tips → General & Beginner Advice on dice-roll spaces being safe to gamble on regardless. Fixing the result turns them into a known, plannable quantity instead: you can now size a raid, pillage, hunt, or whale exactly against a guaranteed outcome, the same way you'd plan around any other action.

Free Weapon Choice

▾

Replaces the blind draw from the face-down weapon deck (New Weapon phase, and any other point you'd draw a weapon card) with a free pick of any weapon type.

  • Whenever you'd draw a weapon card at random, instead take whichever weapon type you want (bow and arrow, snare, spear, or long sword).
  • Why: which weapons you draw and when is a large source of variance independent of the dice themselves — a long sword drawn early is worth far more than one drawn in the last round. Choosing removes that timing luck the same way the Face-Up Occupation Market removes card-draw luck. Pair with Fixed Die Results above, since guaranteed swords is exactly why raiding/pillaging get rebalanced down to 4/5 instead of the base game's 5/7.

Fixed Setup Order

▾

Locks a few setup elements that are otherwise randomized once, so repeated solo plays are directly comparable to each other.

  • Artisan shed: instead of dealing yourself a random Norwegians artisan shed tile, choose which one you start with directly.
  • Mountain strips and weapon deck: fix the shuffled order of both ahead of time (or simply pick a known order) rather than reshuffling each play, so two attempts at the same seed face the same sequence.
  • Promo island slot (Tierra Del Fuego): when using a fixed A/B/C/D 3V island lineup, slot the Tierra Del Fuego promo tile into the C position — it's conditional/harder-execution value (strong only with a longship/raiding build already running), not a clean top pick, matching the tier's other C-caliber boards rather than the A-tier "monster" boards or a clear D-tier skip.
  • Why: the goal of all these house rules together is a fully deterministic "static" version of the game — same starting conditions and same decision points every time — so a higher score on a later attempt reflects better play, not a friendlier shuffle.

Setup

A step-by-step setup sequence for a solo game using the Norwegians expansion plus the Harvest expansion together — a combination none of the official rulebooks lay out as a single procedure, since Norwegians and Harvest are each written as deltas against the base game independently. This reconciles both sets of deltas into one ordered walkthrough. Click a section to expand it.

1. Action Board & Supply

▾
  • Use the three Norwegians action boards, not the single large base-game board. Set out the 1-2 player side face up. The base game's own board and its two 4-player extension tiles go back in the box; they're superseded by the Norwegians boards.
  • Special tiles: place the Norwegians special-tile supply board (sword value 8 or lower, plus a few base-game tiles like the crucifix) instead of the base oval board. Flip the base-game special-tile board over — the higher-value base tiles go on its reverse side, used alongside the Norwegians board.
  • Ships: use the new Norwegians ship supply board in place of the base one, and place the new "small emigration" tiles in their marked space on it.
  • Mountain strips: shuffle the new Norwegians mountain strip into the base game's eight, then follow the normal count for your player number (two face up for 1-3 players). Solo tip: the rules themselves suggest laying out more mountain strips than required and tracking with pencil how many you can actually use each round — this saves a bookkeeping step every round without changing the odds, since you're the only one drawing from them anyway.
  • VP tokens: place the Norwegians victory-point tokens (2× "4VP", 6× "3VP", 8× "2VP") near the action boards — these are new, not a base-game component.
  • Exploration boards: lay out the 4 new Norwegians exploration boards front-side up. Return the base-game exploration boards to the box — but for a solo game (which uses the "up to 3 players" rules), also add the revised base-game boards: one from the A/B pair and one from the C/D pair, face up alongside the new ones. (You can add every base and Norwegians exploration board regardless of player count if you want more variety — nothing about it is illegal, it just adds board-selection choices you won't always take in a solo game.)
  • Harvest exploration board: add the Isle of Mull board to the exploration board layout, Isle of Mull side face up.
  • Goods boxes: lay out both base goods boxes per the standard sorting (rows by color, columns by shape — see the box art), then place the Harvest expansion's new goods tiles next to the boxes, or inside them if there's room.
  • Artisan sheds: shuffle the Norwegians artisan shed tiles and deal yourself one at random (return the rest to the box). It starts unbuilt — you'll need to build it during play, choosing either printed side, before you can use it.

2. Home Board & Vikings

▾
  • Take a home board and pick a side — long game (7 rounds) or short game (6 rounds). Either works solo; long is the default recommendation, short is the higher-difficulty variant once you know the game.
  • Choose a color and take your 12 Vikings, plus a second set of 12 Vikings in a different color — the solo game needs two colors for you alone (see the Solo Game Setup section below for why).
  • Place a Viking on each numbered space of your Banquet Table track; the rest go on the Thing Square as your starting pool. Short game gives you one extra starting Viking versus long game.
  • Sort wood, stone, ore, and silver tokens by type and keep them within reach.
  • Place the "shed", "stone house", and "long house" building tiles in separate stacks on the table. (Your dealt Norwegians artisan shed sits separately, unbuilt, until you build it in-game.)

3. Occupations & Weapons

▾
  • Sort occupation cards into light brown and dark brown backs. Playing with Harvest changes the light-brown draw: instead of drawing 1 starting card, draw 2 and pick one, discarding the other before round 1. (The base solo rules also let you skip the draw entirely and simply choose your starting card if you want to explore a specific strategy — this applies whether or not you're using Harvest's draw-2 step.)
  • Harvest ban list: before shuffling the dark brown deck, remove cards 52a, 107B, 115b, 125c, 150c, 163a, 170a, 189B from the game entirely — these are banned outright when playing with Harvest, not just recommended cuts.
  • Harvest recommended cuts: optionally also remove 1A, 8a, 21b, 28B, 41a, 42b, 45C, 46B, 56A, 96A, 97B, 99A, 101B, 104b, 106B, 124B, 133B, 134A, 135A, 138c, 139C, 157C, 158A, 161B, 162C, 174a, 175B, 177B, 180C, 190B — Harvest's own rulebook flags these as optional; leave them in if you want a larger card pool.
  • Norwegians errata: if you're using cards 26, 31, 35, 52, 60, 66, 88, 99, 102, 108, 109, 110, 117, 123, 125, 148, 150, 157, 163, 170, 176, 179, apply the Norwegians rulebook's clarified wording for them — the printed text on these predates the expansion's interactions.
  • Shuffle the remaining dark brown occupation cards and remaining weapon cards into their own face-down stacks. Take your starting weapons: 1 bow and arrow, 1 snare, 1 spear, plus 1 mead goods tile.
  • Harvest tokens: shuffle the 4 harvest tokens and reveal one at random onto the phase-2 space of the Round Overview tile — this is your first round's harvest. Shuffle the remaining 3 harvest tokens together with the 4 no-harvest tokens and set that stack face down nearby for later rounds.

4. Harvest: Long/Short Game Extras

▾

Harvest adds an extra setup step depending which side of your home board you chose in Step 2. In solo play there's no turn order to go "around," so just resolve your own pick.

  • Long game (7 rounds): pick 1 resource token and place it by your home board, orange side up.
  • Short game (6 rounds): take 3 silver and 1 additional random weapon card. Then pick 1 resource token (orange side up) — and because the short game gets an extra pick versus the long game, repeat the resource-token pick a second time.
  • Because you're playing with Norwegians (either game length): skip the "draw 2 dark brown occupation cards" alternative Harvest offers to non-Norwegians groups. Instead, since you're already using a Norwegians artisan shed, that shed simply starts the game already built by your home board — you don't deal it unbuilt and construct it later like a normal Norwegians game.

5. Solo-Specific Setup

▾

This is the step that makes it a solo game rather than a 1-player-shaped multiplayer game: you place a second color of Vikings to simulate a "ghost" opponent that blocks action spaces, so the board fills up and forces trade-offs the way it would with real opponents.

  • Place 1 Viking of your second color on round space 1 of the Banquet Table (2 Vikings if playing the short game), and 2 more of that color on each of spaces 3, 5, and 7 (space 7 only exists in the long game).
  • Place 2 Vikings of your second color on each of round spaces 2, 4, and 6.
  • Place 5 Vikings of your first (real) color on the Thing Square, and 5 Vikings of the second color (6 in the short game) on the home board's solo waiting space (the small space at the bottom left, unused in multiplayer).
  • Remove whatever single Viking of the second color is left over from the game (in the short game, remove 1 Viking of your first color instead).
  • Play exactly like a multiplayer game, taking one turn at a time — but since there's no opponent to leave Vikings behind, you leave last round's Vikings sitting on the action board to block spaces, which is why two colors exist: one tracks your current actions, the other tracks the "ghost" occupying spaces from the previous round.

6. Running the First Rounds

▾

Setup is only half the picture — the first two rounds play out differently from the rest of the game while the "ghost" Viking system spins up.

  • Round 1: play it exactly like a multiplayer round, but at phase 12 (end of round), don't remove any Vikings from the action board. Instead, move the 5 second-color Vikings from the solo waiting space onto the Thing Square.
  • Round 2: you receive 2 additional second-color Vikings. At the end of round 2, leave the Vikings you placed that round on the action board (they become the "ghost" blockers for round 3), and return your round-1 Vikings to your home board to place again in round 3. Continue this one-round-lagged pattern for the rest of the game.
  • You always go first — always skip phase 6 (turn order) entirely; there's no one to contest it with.
  • Once you're comfortable with the flow, you can skip phase 3 (checking which exploration boards are newly available) each round and just reference the appendix's exploration-board-by-round table instead of re-deriving it live.
  • At phase 12, cross-check the Banquet Table to see which Vikings must come off the action board that round.
  • A final score of 100 is considered a strong solo result — the blocking Vikings tend to depress solo scores below what the same board state would earn in a multiplayer game, so don't benchmark solo scores against multiplayer ones.
