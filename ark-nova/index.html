Strategy Guide

Ark Nova

Zoo layout, card engine, appeal/conservation balance, and action strategy — written for solo play.

Core Strategy

Solo Ark Nova is a 27-turn sprint against a fixed break schedule, not a marathon against opponents. Everything below assumes that context.

⚖️Action efficiency is the whole game

Every turn spends one action, and the number of actions you get is fixed — so the entire game reduces to points scored per action, not points scored total. By the final count, almost everything except actions runs to a surplus: leftover money, cards never played, a worker that sat idle a turn too long. Anything left over at game end was potential that never got converted. X-tokens are the one deliberate exception — they exist purely to multiply a future action's strength, so banking them idle is closer to a loss than a saving. Spend them down.

Use a fixed exchange rate to judge any trade that "feels" valuable: roughly 1 Conservation Point ≈ 3 Appeal at final scoring, looser (closer to 2:1) right at the start of the conservation track and climbing toward 3:1 as it develops, with Reputation sitting in value between the two. Early Appeal and early Conservation Points are worth more than that ratio alone suggests, because they let your engine develop rather than sit flat — a point banked in round 2 buys you options a point banked in round 10 doesn't. Run any tempting trade through this math before committing: releasing a high-appeal animal purely to gain 1 conservation point is a bad trade by this ratio, even though "conservation point" sounds like the more prestigious currency.

There's no globally "best" action or fixed priority order to lean on every turn — every action is a genuine opportunity depending on the state you're in. The most common efficiency mistake is defaulting to the rightmost, highest-power slot out of habit. A weak power-2 or power-3 action that lands exactly the tag, animal, or project you need beats a nominally stronger action that doesn't fit your plan. This applies concretely to Animals and Build: you don't need the rightmost slot on either — only as far right as the enclosure size or animal you actually want to place this turn. Going further spends an action on capacity you don't need yet.

Where you spend X-tokens matters as much as whether you spend them. On Animals and Cards, the printed effects stop scaling at strength 5 — Animals caps at playing 2 animals, Cards caps at its normal draw — so tokens pushed past 5 on either buy nothing extra. On Build, Association, and Sponsors, strength keeps paying off well past 5, because those three read as "perform up to X" rather than a fixed per-level effect: a boosted Association action can fund two separate partnerships in the same activation if you have the workers to spend on both, and effective strength can reach roughly 10 on Build and Association, higher still on Sponsors. When you're deciding where to dump banked tokens, favor Build, Association, or Sponsors over Animals or Cards for exactly this reason.

Two under-used tactical tools worth calling out. The Cards action's Snap option trades quantity for certainty — instead of your normal reputation-range draw, you take any one specific card straight from the display. Use it only when you already know exactly which card you need; don't default to it, since it costs you the extra cards a normal draw would give. Cards sitting deep in the display also drift toward the front as you draw, play, and discard, so if you don't need a specific card immediately, it's often worth waiting for it to migrate into normal reach rather than spending a Snap (or extra reputation) to get it directly. The X-action (bank a token, move that card to slot 1) reads like a pass but isn't one — it's worth taking when a card is stuck in a low slot with nothing useful to do and no break coming soon to reset it, since you're not just skipping a turn, you're raising the value of your next few turns on that action and banking a token for later. Don't default to breaking a spent action for cash instead; X-ing is usually better if there's still real development left to do before the next break — take the cash only if you're simultaneously out of both money and free workers, since then neither track can move without it. Later in the game, playing an animal, sponsor, or project directly from the display — skipping the hand step entirely — trades money for action-efficiency, and that trade gets better as turns grow scarcer relative to cash.

The throughline: before taking any action, ask what it gets you and whether it fits where your engine actually is, not what it costs or how strong its printed number looks.

🏃Why solo plays differently than the rulebook assumes

Solo-specific

Solo doesn't use the break token the way multiplayer does. A cube slides one step on the dedicated solo tracking board after every turn, regardless of which action you took, and the interval between breaks shrinks as the cube approaches the marked break space — breaks come at a genuinely variable, gradually-accelerating cadence, not a fixed round count. Before the very first break specifically, the topmost cube places itself on the lowest-cost donation space on the Association board as an automatic step, then normal break actions resolve as usual; after any break the remaining cubes reset to the left column and the countdown starts over.

In multiplayer, spending an action specifically to trigger a break is a real, deliberate lever — reserved for punishing an opponent's oversized hand with a forced discard, breaking while you're cash-poor but an opponent still has spending money (denying them the extra turns that cash buys), cutting off an opponent about to neutralize your appeal lead, or breaking specifically to unlock the income you need for a key animal. Some animals even carry a "jumping" ability built to trigger breaks on demand while still advancing your own appeal, so the break-forcing isn't pure tempo loss. None of that machinery exists solo — you can't trigger a break, delay one, or read an opponent's incentive to avoid one, because the cube advances automatically no matter what you do. The "should I force this break" question the rulebook trains you to ask simply doesn't apply to you.

Multiplayer's subtler break-timing play — reading whether a leader is stalling to spend one more worker action, or staying quiet about your own break-timing intentions so you don't tip them off — has no solo equivalent either, since there's no opponent to read or hide information from. What carries over is just the underlying value: a worker-turn squeezed in right before a break is worth real points, so treat an idle worker or an unused strong action the same way a careful multiplayer player treats their own timing — don't let it sit unused when the cube ticks over.

Your total turn budget is fixed — roughly 27 turns, though the effective productive stretch runs a bit shorter once setup and the compressed final rounds are accounted for — and the game still tends to feel like it ends earlier than the raw number suggests. Final rounds arrive as short bursts, not full-length rounds. Planning as though you have a multiplayer-length runway, rather than budgeting explicitly for this compression, is the single most common way a new solo player ends up with unconverted potential still sitting on the board at the final count.

By the closing turns you'll typically be cash-rich and action-poor — money accumulates faster than you have turns left to spend it. That's the clearest solo-specific proof that turns, not money, are the binding constraint (see Action efficiency, above): don't hoard cash waiting for a better moment to spend it, because that moment may never arrive before turns run out.

Solo also removes a multiplayer constraint in your favor: 2-3 player games block off some conservation-project slots, but solo blocks none — every project stays available all game. That flexibility cuts both ways with efficiency: it's often correct to fund a project at its lowest tier purely to nudge the conservation track forward, rather than waiting for the resources to fund it in full.

X-tokens are scarcer solo despite mattering more. Two structural reasons: you can't gain an X-token from triggering a break the way some multiplayer situations allow, and multiplayer generates a steady stream of "filler" X-token turns — players early in a round with nothing better to do, and no desire to be the one who triggers the break, default to banking a token. Solo has no other players creating that pattern, so those free tokens don't show up for you. A handful of animal abilities that grant combat effects in multiplayer grant X-tokens instead in solo, but it only partially offsets the gap — don't assume you'll have tokens to spare the way multiplayer trains you to.

Any ability that slides a played action card straight back to slot 1 at full strength (worded as Clever or Boost on different cards) matters far more solo than the base rules telegraph. Your short endgame needs an action — usually Association, sometimes a second one — back at high strength fast and repeatedly in the closing rounds; an X-token only boosts one future action, where a Clever/Boost effect re-charges the action immediately, which is strictly better when what you need is tempo right now rather than power banked for later. Solo offers more of these than it looks: a Clever placement bonus exists on nearly every map, several abilities that read as pure combat effects in multiplayer become Clever triggers in solo instead, and at least one card grants a Clever trigger every time you play a matching-region icon. Weight these higher than you would in a multiplayer game when reading your opening hand and choosing placement bonuses.

A 3rd or 4th Association worker matters less solo than the rulebook's framing implies. Short rounds rarely call for spending two workers in the same Association activation — needing to is the exception, not the norm — so the 4th worker's unlock mostly just pays out its flat one-time bonus rather than opening real ongoing capacity you'll use. Treat reaching max workers as a nice-to-have, not a goal worth reshaping your plan around.

Card variance also matters more solo. With no other players cycling the deck, a weak opening hand isn't diluted or corrected by anyone else's draws — a bad initial hand stays bad. Stay flexible on which final-scoring card and which upgrade path you commit to rather than locking in from turn one: Ark Nova lets you track two final-scoring options simultaneously and only requires committing near the end, which is exactly the tool that lets you recover from a draw that didn't go your way. One recurring solo pattern worth weighing rather than assuming: the action cost of acquiring and housing large animals often struggles to pay back within a 27-turn budget compared to cheaper animals played more often — worth checking against your specific hand rather than chasing size by default.

None of this changes what wins the game — points per action, same as always — but it changes what "efficient" looks like when there's no opponent to read, no break to negotiate, and a hard clock instead of a soft one.

🌱Invest, then convert: why Cards and Association compound

Appeal and Conservation look like two versions of the same progress bar — both push toward the game ending, both feel like forward motion. They aren't shaped the same, and mixing them up is exactly how you finish with the best-looking zoo at the table and still lose: highest appeal, lowest conservation, board full of visible activity, score in last place.

Appeal is a transaction. Play an animal, gain appeal, move on — no system underneath that grows an animal's value over time (a few animals that trigger off other animals, and some Break thresholds that reward high appeal position, are minor exceptions that don't change the overall shape). Appeal-focused play looks productive because something visibly happens every turn. Conservation-focused play looks slow next to it — three quiet setup turns, then a donation that scores a chunk of points at once, repeat. The math usually rewards the second pattern even when the first one feels better turn to turn.

Cards and Association compound instead of transacting, because both are entry points into systems that keep paying out for the rest of the game rather than paying out once:

  • Workers, hired via Association, are reusable every cycle for as long as the game runs. A worker hired round 2 gets nine-plus rounds of reuse; the same worker hired round 7 barely gets a handful before the game ends.
  • Reputation gates which folder of the card market you're allowed to draw from. Push it early and you're drafting from the stronger, pricier folders for the entire rest of the game; neglect it and you're stuck on the cheap, weak leftmost folder throughout — reputation runs as its own sub-game alongside the main one, and falling behind on it costs card quality, not just quantity.
  • Conservation donations scale with the Association card's current strength, which is highest right after it cycles back and decays with use. Donating at strength 4 nets a meaningful chunk of conservation points in one action; scraping together the same total piecemeal from a strength-2 card several rounds later can cost three actions for the same yield. That's literal arithmetic, not a metaphor.

Cards compounds for the same reason and reinforces Association directly: a wide early draft keeps your options open, while a narrow one locks you into a single strategy within the first few rounds. The two systems also feed each other — without reputation you can't reach the stronger cards; without card depth you can't complete the projects that need specific icons or abilities. Fall behind on either one and it drags the other down.

None of this means ignore Appeal, or that Conservation is always the right push. Lean too hard into Conservation and you can trigger the game's end before your appeal engine has had time to mature, capping your final appeal the same way neglecting Conservation caps your CP — the two tracks are opposite ends of the same clock, and getting the crossing point right matters as much as picking a direction to run. Occasionally the hand in front of you has real appeal-granting synergy sitting in it (cards like Penguin Park or Meerkat Den turn appeal into its own mini-engine rather than a flat transaction), and pushing that is correct. The principle isn't "always chase conservation" — it's know which of your options actually compounds before spending an action on it.

The practical shape of an opening: run Cards while its action strength is at its highest, since that's the most cards you'll ever draw off one action, then Association for reputation and a worker, and let Build and Animals wait unless a specific card gives a strong reason not to. An empty-looking board in round 1 isn't a problem; an empty hand and no engine several rounds in is — and it's usually traceable to spending your strongest action card on Build or Animals early because leaving the table empty felt wrong. That instinct is worth resisting on its own, but the order underneath it isn't a memorized script either: reassess each turn which track is actually closest to unlocking its next project or upgrade, and let that — not a fixed sequence — pick the next action.

The reason any of this compounding matters is that every system in the game touches every other one. Wanting a specific conservation project pulls in the animal icons it needs, which pulls in specific animals, which pulls in specific enclosures, which competes with kiosk placement for the same board space, which affects income, which affects what you can afford next turn. Investing early in Cards and Association isn't just "more options later" in the abstract — it's what keeps that whole chain solvent instead of stalling out partway through.

🪤Common traps

Building to fill an empty board

Playing Build or Animals in round 1 because the zoo looks empty and leaving it that way feels wrong is the single most common early mistake. It spends your strongest action-card strength — the round it's freshest — on a system that pays out once instead of one that compounds (see Invest, then convert, above). The animal itself is usually fine; the action-card slot burned to get it out that early is the actual cost.

Letting reputation slide

Reputation determines which folder of the card display you're even allowed to draw from, so treat it as a sub-game running the whole time rather than background progress you'll get to later. Fall behind and strong cards sit visibly in the higher folders while you're stuck drafting the cheap leftmost one — the Cards upgrade only partially closes that gap. Aim to clear a real reputation milestone (a partner zoo, a university, or reputation level 5 itself) within the first break or two rather than letting it drift.

Locking into one script and refusing to adapt

Breaking a skill plateau isn't about running a better fixed plan — it's about running no fixed plan and building whatever the hand and market actually support. Committing hard to one archetype regardless of what's dealt (always rushing conservation projects, for instance, no matter the hand) routinely leaves value on the table, including appeal-engine synergies some hands hand you for free: cards like Penguin Park or Meerkat Den turn appeal into a real engine rather than a flat transaction, and a rigid gameplan walks straight past them.

Upgrading Sponsors for a card that doesn't earn it

The Sponsors upgrade is tempting the moment any level-2 sponsor shows up, but most level-2 sponsors aren't worth the upgrade action on their own — reflexively taking it is a quiet cap on your win rate. Reserve it for the sponsors that are genuinely strong by themselves (Explorer, Talented Communicator, or the sponsor that lets you pick a card at every break are the kind worth it) and spend the upgrade action on Animals, Build, Cards, or Association otherwise.

🔧Upgrade priority

Only four action-card upgrades exist in a single game — one per action type, with the fifth type left un-upgraded — plus a possible fifth slot via the Conservation-2 milestone (see below). Treat upgrades as a genuinely scarce resource to sequence deliberately, not a checklist to clear top-to-bottom.

  1. Cards, near-mandatory but timed. Unlocks drawing from your full reputation range instead of a blind draw, adds extra cards per draw, and gates reputation-track progress past its higher thresholds — likely the single most beneficial upgrade available, and one of the two upgrades (with Association) that's genuinely hard to do without eventually. Don't take it the moment you gain reputation, though — a low reputation range offers few real choices to draw into, so the upgrade is weak until you're a few reputation cards in. Note the common mix-up here: only the Cards upgrade lets you draw a card into hand from your reputation range. The Animals, Sponsors, and Association upgrades instead let you play straight from the display, skipping the hand step entirely — a different, narrower trick (see Action efficiency, above, for when that's worth it).
  2. Build, usually the first completed — but situational. Lets you build multiple structures in one action instead of one at a time, which is what actually breaks a cash-poor start: bundling a kiosk cluster alongside a pen in the same action, filling fiddly size-1 gaps, and using Large Bird Aviary/Reptile House tricks to save space and cost. It's also necessary to fill the whole map for goals that require it. Weigh this against the counter-case: the upgrade is dead weight if you're low on money for a stretch, kiosks rarely earn back their cost once you're several build-rounds in, and a free size-2 enclosure or cash from the Conservation-2 bonus (below) can outvalue it if you're leaning on releases, big animals, or a petting zoo instead. Building at strength 1 purely to place one kiosk or pavilion is a weak use of an action either way — a reason this upgrade earns its priority when you do need it. Map matters too: Icecream Parlor, Restaurant, and Silver Lake need fewer kiosks, so the upgrade can wait; Observation Tower demands it early for expansion room. Unmanaged building — including free size-2 enclosures — can also choke off late-game large-animal space, so preserve room if a big animal is still a live possibility.
  3. Association, situational but often worth it. Lets you take multiple Association actions in one activation, which mainly pays off two ways: racing the cheap early donation tiers before the board state moves past them, and unlocking a 3rd or 4th partner zoo if you need those continent icons. Don't upgrade purely to chase donation money immediately, though — an early upgrade nets only the 2-5 money tiers, at the cost of the cards or buildings you'd have gotten from other actions in that window, and by the 1CP≈3Appeal ratio (above), 10 money for a few appeal-equivalent points is a weak trade unless you're already flush. The stronger window is mid-late game, once you have a couple of workers and can chain X-tokens into a strength-7/8/9 Association action to fund a project and something else the same turn. Skip it if you don't need extra zoos and have already banked the cheap donations — in a short or two-handed game, the higher donation tiers and extra zoo/university actions often go unused anyway.
  4. Animals, usually later. Lets you place two animals in one action instead of one, and is worth a reputation point at strength 5 on top of that. Its value peaks once Build and Cards are already up, and it's most worth prioritizing early only if you're running an aggressive small-animal strategy or chasing specific big animals that require it. Where it earns a higher priority than it first looks: in the last round, Build, Sponsors, and Cards often go to waste with nothing left to spend them on, so being able to chain Association into a double-Animals play into Association again is one of the few sequences that still uses every action productively at the very end.
  5. Sponsors, rarely the right pick first. The most situational of the five — worth it mainly if you're holding multiple strong sponsor cards, since the main benefit is the flexibility of +1 power (timing plays more freely, spending fewer X-tokens) rather than raw output. Playing a sponsor directly from the display saves a Cards action but is rarely the deciding factor. Upgrade this only once the other four offer no better use of the slot — but don't write it off reflexively either; it's easy to underrate relative to how often a strong sponsor hand actually shows up.

Treat this order as a default to deviate from, not a script — your specific hand, map, and how the conservation track is developing all shift it in practice.

Solo-specific

Independent solo reports lean toward upgrading Association earlier than the multiplayer-general case above suggests — specifically to unlock donations as soon as possible, since they're a reliable, repeatable conservation-point source and the sprint format rewards compounding a track early rather than waiting for the "better" mid-game window. If you're leaning this way, feed it by hoarding X-tokens for Association activations specifically rather than spreading them evenly across actions.

The Conservation-2 choice: card upgrade vs. extra worker

At conservation level 2 — typically reachable within the first couple of rounds — you get a one-time bonus choice between a 4th card upgrade or an extra Association worker. Default to the upgrade. It's a genuinely scarce, permanent resource (only four exist across five action types, so missing one costs real long-run efficiency), while an extra worker is usually reachable another way later — a 2nd partner zoo, a 2nd university, or the conservation track's own worker rewards on most maps.

Take the worker instead only when several of these line up at once:

  • You have no other near-term route to a 2nd or 3rd worker, and the extra Association action is spendable productively the same turn — two conservation-project supports in a round, or funding a partnership plus something else in one activation.
  • You're already on track to reach your first 2CP through zoo/university plays rather than a conservation project itself — the worker then compounds progress you were making anyway, without actually delaying whichever upgrade you'd have taken.
  • The card upgrade would sit low-value in your current state regardless — you're short on cash and the upgrade you'd take needs money to matter, or the game is trending short and break-heavy, where a one-time bonus outperforms a per-use upgrade you won't get to use many times.
  • You already know which three cards you're upgrading, and none of the remaining candidates is worth much to you right now.

Skipping a card upgrade permanently forfeits whatever that action type would've unlocked — Association's 3rd/4th zoo access and ongoing donation income, Cards' reputation-range draw and top-track rewards, Build's ability to finish the map, Animals' faster and double placement, or Sponsors' money access and multi-sponsor plays. Weigh that against what the worker actually buys you this specific game, not what it buys in the abstract.

Solo-specific

Solo's sprint format (see Why solo plays differently, above) shifts this choice toward the worker more than multiplayer norms suggest. Players who consistently favor the action that helps right now over a permanent-but-slow-to-pay-off upgrade take the worker far more often than the roughly-one-in-twenty rate multiplayer averages converge on — and if you're hammering conservation points early through releases, breeding programs, and key animals, finishing the game with only 3 upgraded actions is entirely survivable as long as you compensate with bigger-ticket animals to hold your appeal pace. On maps where the conservation track's own rewards don't include a worker anywhere else, this bonus is also your only route to a 3rd or 4th worker at all — which raises its value further on those maps specifically.

Either way, decide once and move on — re-litigating this choice turn after turn costs more than either option is worth.

📋Reading your opening hand

Apply this checklist to any dealt hand rather than following a fixed script:

  1. Cut what you can't cast soon. Big late-game animals and any card with an unmet prerequisite — a required upgrade, a partner zoo or university you have no route to — are dead weight in an opening hand. Hand size shrinks fast at breaks, so a card sitting uncastable for many turns is occupying a slot something playable now could use. Cards that need an upgrade at game start specifically are close to an automatic cut regardless of raw power — you can't afford the upgrade before you'd need to play them anyway.
  2. Guarantee support for some project, not necessarily the best one. Scan the public conservation projects against your hand before anything else — the first two conservation points, plus the bonus for sitting left-most on the project track, are among the strongest returns available early. Treat "can support something by the first break" as the floor and "can support the best project" as a bonus if it happens to line up. Exception: if two diversity-style projects (Species and Habitat Diversity) are both realistically racing toward the same strength threshold, sometimes the better play is skipping a round-2 project entirely and pushing both races instead — two live opportunities reinforcing each other can beat locking in one smaller guaranteed one.
  3. Look for small synergies, not a full build. Ark Nova rewards pairing two or three cards sharing an icon or an ability chain, not committing to one archetype from turn one. Forcing a deep build around a single plan the hand doesn't actually support burns cards and actions a looser, more flexible line would spend better.
  4. Check that your keeps share a path. Two individually strong cards can still be a bad pair if they need different early actions to unlock — a card needing university science icons and a card needing a partner zoo you have no near-term route to are both fine alone and a trap together, since your opening actions can only chase one of those paths at a time.

Some cards are close to auto-keeps independent of context — a strong sponsor with no real downside is worth taking on sight in most hands, and the eventual cost of upgrading Sponsors to unlock it isn't a real deterrent, since that upgrade tends to be worth taking regardless of what it unlocks. But "usually a keep" isn't "always a keep": the same card can be a correct cut in a specific hand if your other candidates advance your actual game plan more directly. Evaluate every card against what's in front of you this game, not its generic power level.

Don't undervalue eagles and rhinos just because they have no immediate use — they're routinely undervalued as "late game only" and are strong enough to bank for later in most hands. The one time to actually cut one: if both Species Diversity and Habitat Diversity are already claimed as projects, which removes most of a rhino's usual payoff. A card's building footprint can also redeem a pick that looks weak on paper — a normally mediocre card can become a key combo piece if its shape happens to chain toward a second university or a placement bonus on your specific map, so check board geography before cutting on card-text power level alone. And weigh true scarcity on close calls: playable animals are scarcer than sponsor plays, so in an even tie between an animal and a third sponsor, lean toward keeping the animal. Be wary of any card whose payoff depends on collecting several of a kind — Petting Zoo animals need three to pay off and there are only ten in the entire deck, so starting with just one is a bad bet, not a foundation to build around.

Solo-specific

Multiplayer hand-reading has to price in contested resources — a regional partner zoo can get taken by an opponent before you reach it, real enough at two players that it's worth weighing roughly a one-in-three chance a specific regional zoo gets denied to you. Solo removes that risk entirely: nothing in your hand's plan can be taken out from under you, so a build-around plan a multiplayer hand-reader would hedge against is safe to commit to at full confidence. Read the board and your hand for the strongest plan, then take it — there's no opponent-denial calculus to weigh against it.

🎯Managing your goal pair

Your two final-scoring cards are dealt as a pair, and how they relate to each other matters more than either card's printed value on its own. Some pairs share enough underlying requirements that serving one mostly serves the other for free; some sit in genuinely separate lanes and need separate investment. Read the pair before committing a build around either one.

When a pair shares a resource, that overlap is worth more than the numbers suggest. A pair rewarding sponsor buildings alongside big animals, for instance, is served by keeping cards that each pull double duty — a sponsor building that also happens to fit a live project, next to a large animal that also fits a live regional project, advances both goals at once without either stealing actions from the other. Look specifically for cards that do double duty rather than cards that only serve one goal at a time; solo removes the contested-resource risk that would otherwise make committing to this kind of dual-purpose plan a gamble (see Reading your opening hand, above), so lean into it with confidence once you spot it.

When a pair doesn't share anything real, don't force an overlap that isn't there — run two separate lanes instead. Building an entire early combo around securing a second university to feed one goal's science requirement, while separately chasing an unrelated regional or diversity project for the other, is a normal and correct shape when the two goals genuinely don't touch. Hunting for synergy that isn't there wastes cards and actions a straightforward two-lane plan would spend better.

Your goal pair isn't the only scoring signal on the board, either — the live conservation projects available that game can matter more to your opening than the printed goal cards do, especially when one of them (a diversity race sitting at a matching strength threshold, for example) is unusually strong that specific game. Read your goal pair alongside the project state, not in isolation from it; a hand built purely around the goal cards while ignoring an exceptional project on the table is leaving value behind.

Card quality is relative to your specific pair, not a fixed ranking. A card that's a near-automatic keep in most games can be the correct cut in a hand where your pair and the board state point elsewhere, and a card that's mediocre in the abstract can jump in value because its footprint or icons happen to feed exactly what your pair needs. Recap: read the two goals together before building, chase overlap where it's real, run two lanes where it isn't, and weigh both the pair and whatever conservation projects happen to be strong that game.

Scoring & the Two Tracks

Appeal and Conservation look like two separate scores, but they resolve into one number — and that number is really a countdown toward a fixed finish line. Here's what the split is actually doing mechanically, how to read your progress against it, and how to time the endgame trigger and reputation bonuses in your favor.

🧮The scoring reduces to one number

Underneath the two markers crossing from opposite ends of a shared track, only two things ever determine your final score: your conservation total and your appeal total, converted to a common scale and netted against each other. It doesn't matter when or how the markers cross, which one moves faster, or what order you built them in — only the final tally of each matters.

The exchange rate is roughly 1 conservation point ≈ 3 appeal, but not from turn one: at the very start of the game conservation converts at a worse rate (closer to 2:1), so your early conservation gains are worth less than the 3:1 rate would suggest. The rate settles to 3:1 after that initial stretch and stays there. The track itself represents a fixed 114-point gap between the two markers' starting positions, and score reads as zero the moment they meet — which means every player effectively starts the game at –114 and climbs back toward positive as both totals grow. A negative number at game end is not a sign you played badly; it's just where the scale is zeroed. Read your score by how much of that gap you closed, not against zero.

One consequence worth internalizing: the game is fundamentally a race to close that gap fastest. Most games are won by whichever player (or, solo, whichever attempt) gets there first — but "first" isn't final, because a bonus round is still coming (see below), so a close race is never fully decided until that round resolves.

A second consequence: past that early undervalued stretch, conservation isn't intrinsically "worth more" than appeal — 3 appeal and 1 conservation point are close to interchangeable once you're comparing raw score. A build that leans entirely on high-appeal animals can score just as well as one that chases conservation, if the numbers work out the same. Conservation still tends to be the better lane in practice — its rate of return per action is usually strong and its track bonuses are large — but that's an efficiency argument, not a "conservation points count more" argument. Compare the actual point yield an action produces, not which track it feeds.

🤝Why two tracks instead of one summed number

It's a fair question why the game doesn't just add appeal + 3×conservation into a single running VP total instead of keeping two markers on a shared track. The split is doing several jobs at once, not one:

  • It's what makes the endgame trigger work. The two markers meeting is the end condition — an elegant, thematic one. Collapse them into a single pre-summed number and you'd need to invent an arbitrary substitute end condition instead, which is fiddlier, not simpler.
  • Appeal isn't just a score — it's live income. Your appeal level sets how much money you collect at each break, so it has to stay legible as its own number throughout the game, not get absorbed into a blended total. Conservation, by contrast, is close to pure end-value (plus its own early bonus thresholds) — the two stats do genuinely different jobs during play, not just at the final tally.
  • Keeping them separate cuts the mental math. Reading your income at a glance means appeal has to stay its own visible number; if it were pre-merged with conservation into one VP counter, you'd have to constantly back out how much of that total was appeal before you could tell what a break pays out.
  • Both tracks carry their own threshold effects. Conservation has early-game bonus thresholds of its own; appeal has thresholds at 5 and 25 that specific cards key off. A single blended number can't cleanly expose either kind of trigger.
  • It preserves build diversity. Because the tracks aren't pre-combined, you can legitimately go all-in on one axis and mostly ignore the other, or split effort between both — nothing structurally forces a particular ratio. "Balance" between them is a loose habit, not a rule: appeal generally costs money (playing animals) while conservation generally doesn't (supporting projects), and that asymmetry is what naturally nudges most builds toward touching both rather than any hard requirement to keep the markers close.
  • It's thematically honest. The two-track split maps onto the real tension a zoo runs — commercial popularity versus conservation mission — so the scoring keeps both dimensions in view instead of flattening them into one abstract VP chase.

Of that list, the build-diversity point is the one most worth acting on at the table: don't treat the two tracks as something you're obligated to keep in lockstep. Let your build lean wherever your draws and combos actually point, and judge trades by the ~3:1 exchange rate rather than by which track looks "behind."

Trade-off heuristic

Use the ~3 appeal : 1 conservation point ratio to sanity-check any action that trades one for the other. Releasing a 12-appeal animal to gain a single conservation point (one "shield") is a bad trade by this math — that appeal was worth roughly 4 shields, not 1. Don't release or sacrifice appeal for conservation (or vice versa) unless the exchange actually clears the ~3:1 bar; conservation's early bonuses and strong action economy are reasons to prioritize it on their own merits, not reasons to accept a below-rate trade just because it "feels thematic."

For context on why this design reads as fresh rather than gimmicky: most Euro games just run a fixed number of rounds and tally VP at the end, which works fine but isn't distinctive. A marker-meeting trigger belongs to a smaller family of non-standard-but-non-gimmicky end conditions (shared, for reference, with games like Rajas of the Ganges and Civilization: A New Dawn) — different from the norm without being different for its own sake. A useful comparison case is Zoo Tycoon, a near-neighbor design that runs three parallel tracks (attraction, conservation, and reputation/education) but counts only the lowest of the three as your final score — a harsher rule that forces genuinely equal investment across all three rather than letting you lean into whichever is working. Neither approach is objectively better; each fits the goals of its own game, and Ark Nova's choice to combine rather than floor its tracks is what enables the strategic flexibility described above.

🏁Playing the endgame trigger

Crossing the point where the two markers meet ends the game — but not instantly for everyone. The player who crosses it finishes out their own turn, and then every other player still gets exactly one further turn each before the game actually ends. It's not a full extra round and it isn't simultaneous — it's one last turn apiece, then done.

That makes the trigger a lever you pull deliberately, not just a finish line you stumble across. Unless you're sitting on a commanding lead, don't let the step that crosses the meeting point be the last thing you do on a turn. End your turn positioned right at the edge of it instead, then take the crossing step as the first action of your next turn rather than the last action of this one. That keeps you as far past the zero-score baseline as your position allows, and leaves opponents less room to close the gap with the one bonus turn they're about to get. Losing the game on the very last step you didn't need to take yet is the trap this avoids — treat "one more action banked before I cross" as the default, not the exception.

Before you're the one who triggers the end, weigh your total against everyone else's: if any opponent is within roughly one turn's worth of points of you, crossing hands them exactly the turn they need to close the gap or overtake you. Only trigger once your lead is bigger than what one strong opponent turn could plausibly produce. And if you're on the other side of that — not the trigger, with the game looking close — don't write the result off early: your guaranteed final turn is a real chance to overtake whoever crossed the line, so plan it as a scoring turn in its own right rather than a formality.

Solo-specific

There's no opponent to hand that bonus turn to, so the multiplayer caution about not triggering unless your lead beats one opponent turn's worth of points doesn't apply — there's nobody to close the gap on you, and nobody to overtake either. What does carry over unchanged is the sandbagging principle itself: bank one more action before you cross, because that's about squeezing full value out of your own turn budget, not about opponents. Solo actually makes this easier to plan than multiplayer does — your total turn count is fixed and known in advance, so you can work out explicitly which action should land right before the crossing step rather than reacting to it turn by turn.

🎖️Reputation track bonuses

One ruling worth knowing before it costs you value: your reputation token doesn't need to land exactly on a bonus space to get it.

Bonus spaces trigger on pass-through, not just landing

Any bonus space your reputation token reaches or moves through in a single advance triggers immediately — stopping on it isn't required. A reputation gain that jumps several spaces at once collects every bonus space along the way, not just the one it ends on, so multi-step reputation gains are quietly worth more than they look at first glance.

Default resolution order, when a move passes multiple bonus spaces, follows track order — resolve them in the order the token passes them. The one exception: when a single action triggers more than one bonus simultaneously (as opposed to one move stepping through several in sequence), you're free to choose the order yourself rather than being locked to track order — worth doing when resolving one bonus first changes what a later one needs or gives you.

Recap: your score is one number — conservation and appeal netted against each other on a fixed scale, so negative endgame totals and lopsided builds are both normal, not warning signs. The two tracks stay separate because they're doing different jobs (income, thresholds, the trigger itself, build flexibility), not because they need active balancing. Treat the meeting point as a lever: bank an extra action before you cross it, and in multiplayer, only trigger once your lead clears what one opponent turn could close. Reputation bonuses resolve on pass-through, not just on landing, so multi-step gains are worth more than the space they land on suggests.

Zoo Building & Enclosures

Cash tightness, what to build, and when specialized strategies (large animals, bird aviaries, filling the map) are actually worth pursuing.

💰Running low on cash is normal

Running out of cash between breaks isn't a sign you're misplaying — it's the expected texture of the game. Manage it in the moment rather than trying to design it away entirely. When you're stuck with no affordable action, force your own break instead of waiting for someone else to trigger it, ideally via a sponsor card's bottom-half action, since bottom halves typically push the break token forward — that turns a dead turn into the income tick you actually need.

Kiosks are worth prioritizing specifically when you're cash-strapped, since they generate cash directly rather than just appeal. On the conservation-project side, don't spend the $5 cash-cube reward on your first support of a project — hold it for the second support instead, since the second support pays out more than the first, so the free cube does more work sitting on the bigger reward.

Early on, favor animals that are cheap and easy to play over anything large or expensive: the opening rounds are about getting appeal moving, not landing a marquee animal. Appeal compounds straight into income, which is why sponsor cards that grant appeal outright deserve extra weight while cash is tight — that's income with no capital outlay.

Avoid front-loading large animals

Playing a large enclosure animal in the opening rounds is the most common way to stall out: the enclosure plus the animal itself typically burns through your starting cash before appeal is even moving. Building bigger enclosures and stacking more animals produces shrinking returns when it happens too early — that same pattern is the main engine behind the no-money loop below. Get appeal moving with cheap, easy animals first, and treat large animals as something to graduate into once income is already running, not an opening move.

🔁Preventing the no-money loop

The no-money loop — buy one animal, sit broke until the next income tick, repeat, with income never actually growing — is self-inflicted, not bad luck, and the fix is upstream sequencing rather than an escape maneuver once you're already in it. It comes from two distortions: overvaluing random animals played without a specific reason, and undervaluing kiosks, pavilions, sponsor buildings, and appeal-granting sponsors.

  • Push appeal to roughly 20 early via cheap sources, then be content sitting there a while rather than chasing it further with ever-bigger enclosures — bigger enclosures and more animals give shrinking returns, and doing that too early is exactly what produces the loop. Play only the animals you actually need for tag or project requirements; get the rest of your appeal from other sources.
  • Kiosks and pavilions are undervalued: roughly 2 money for 1 appeal beats almost any animal's points-per-money conversion. "It just takes up space" is a weak objection to building densely — the board rarely fills by game end anyway, and building early also claims placement bonuses. Place kiosks adjacent to more than one enclosure to maximize income per kiosk.
  • Upgrade Build before most other actions in most games — it lets you place a kiosk, a 1-size enclosure, and a pavilion in a single action (ideally near an enclosure you've already got animals in) for 3+ income per break, which is the single highest-leverage income upgrade available. One correction worth knowing: Build II alone does not let you place two of the same tile type in one action — you're still limited to one of each distinct building unless a card like Architect or Engineer removes that restriction.
  • Upgrading Sponsors doubles the money you collect per break-token movement (2 vs. 1), and using a sponsor's bottom-half ability to move the break token and collect money is the most direct tool for escaping a stuck cash state, since it also hastens your next income phase. Grab the board's 5-money bonus space as early as you can, and lean on Sunbathing-keyword animals for direct cash generation.
  • Upgrade Cards to draw more — a bigger hand makes it easier to find income pieces and useful sponsors. If nothing usable turns up, falling back to the base, unupgraded 2x-money sponsor action is fine, just not ideal.
  • Avoid overbuilding: empty enclosures with no animal plan are pure waste of money and actions. Sponsor and association tags hand out free points on their own, so games are often won on those rather than raw animal count.
  • Break more often than feels natural — many players under-break. Each turn is a tradeoff between playing a sponsor now and forcing the break to keep tempo; it's usually better to force the break and play the sponsor early next round if it survives, since playing a sponsor late in a round often leaves you with nothing affordable to spend the remaining actions on.
  • Associate less early: overvaluing workers before you can actually use them productively is a common beginner trap. Filling the partner zoo or university slot every single game is playing too slow and leads to prolonged no-money stretches at round ends.
  • Take the once-per-break animal snap before or after your first project rather than as your very first move, especially when you're the one triggering the break — that gets you first pick of the refreshed card display. It feels worse than grabbing a worker first, but it keeps tempo.
  • Upgrading Sponsors is on par with upgrading Association, and better when the card display skews toward level-2 sponsor cards — the doubled cash matters more the more often you're breaking. A reasonable default upgrade order is Build, then Animals, then Cards, then Association, with Sponsors slotted in situationally; Association is usually the one left unupgraded if Sponsors gets upgraded instead.
  • Donate sparingly: donations costing more than 5 money rarely beat an equivalent-cost animal once enclosure cost is factored in.

It's also worth reframing what "low on cash" even means: running out of money after a single animal play can be entirely fine, even optimal, since only Build and Animals need cash — Association, Cards, and Sponsors don't. Plan to hit the break right before your next build or animal need rather than timing it around cash refills, and watch for the trap of playing a second sponsor purely for the economy and then wasting the rest of the round on actions you can't afford to use. Because cash only gates two of the five action types, hand size (the only cap on the Cards action) and a second worker (which enables Association) are often worth prioritizing over raw cash. In multiplayer, forcing the break mid-round can also work against opponents — it puts them in a game of chicken where they either rush cards onto the board now or risk losing the chance, often stranding them holding cash they can't fully convert (money for four animal plays but the action only allows two, for instance), sometimes forcing discards. Solo removes the opponent side of that, but the underlying lesson — force the break at the moment that serves your own tempo, not whenever it happens to trigger on its own — still holds. None of this fully insulates against bad card luck (no usable income sponsors drawn), but it removes the self-inflicted version of the loop.

🦁Large animals: a pivot, not an opening plan

Ark Nova rewards adapting to what's actually achievable over locking into a plan, and large animals are the clearest place that shows. A starting hand with two or three large animals plus a big sponsor card looks synergistic, but it's a trap for a specific, repeatable reason: on top of the visible cost in cash and enclosure space, most large-animal payoff cards also stack a secondary gate — a reputation threshold, a science icon, a sponsorship icon — that has to clear before the play is even legal. Those gates are independent unlock conditions competing for the same early actions your baseline economy also needs, and losing a race for any one of them — another player taking the university or partner zoo you needed — can strand the whole hand.

Enclosure cost is the easiest piece to soften. Large birds go in the Aviary instead of a size-4/5 open enclosure, and herbivores that can legally share space cut the same cost; either is worth more when the animal also counts toward an Association project, and it's worth favoring animals you plan to release again later, so the enclosure gets reused rather than sitting built-and-empty.

Two structural problems stack on each other: clearing several conditions at once is hard, and generating enough income to afford even one large animal within the first few rounds is harder still. Land one or two small animals first to get an income base running before committing — a starting hand already stacked with three large animals makes that awkward, which is exactly why it's worth discarding one or two of them even though they're the flashiest cards in hand, rather than forcing the whole hand to work. Keep one or two for near-term use, maybe bank one for later, and let the rest go; more large animals surface later in the game, so there's no need to force the ones you happened to draft turn one.

Treat WAZA Special Assignment and similar big sponsor plays as a mid-game payoff, not an opener — playing one too early hinders more than it helps. If you do commit to a large-animal push, upgrade the Sponsors action alongside it, since affordability is the whole problem and the upgrade directly widens what you can afford. Secondary requirements are a learnable pattern, not bad luck: the large-animal endgame scoring card only needs 4 large animals to max out versus 10 for the small-animal equivalent, which is the game telling you large animals are deliberately harder to land, not that you drew badly. (One related rules point worth knowing: the Rhino's project-fetch ability only searches the base projects laid out at setup — 3 or 4 of them — not the full deck, a common misread that overstates how flexible the card is.)

Most animals big enough to need a 4-5 space enclosure don't earn that space back. Rhinos, the golden eagle, and occasionally elephants are the recurring exceptions worth building around; an oversized enclosure for anything else is usually the overvaluing-big-animals mistake that drains a zoo's cash. A reasonable readiness bar before committing to the large-animal route: a small income engine already running, roughly 6 reputation and a research icon banked, and a starting zoo that isn't already fighting you elsewhere.

A large-animal focus rarely wins on its own — it needs pairing with the Large Animal Zoo objective card or a Large Animals conservation project to become a primary scoring engine, and the odds of seeing either on a given board are middling. It's fine to run one specialization that isn't backed by a base project or objective, but keep working toward at least one project or objective in parallel rather than betting everything on the large-animal line. (In the Marine Worlds expansion, the Large Animals conservation project was buffed to need only 3 animals for 4 conservation points, down from a steeper threshold — with that project in play, the whole strategy pays off more easily.)

🐦Bird Aviary vs. Reptile House

Reptile House gets built far more often than Bird Aviary at most tables, but that's a card-pool artifact, not a power difference. Every one of the 25 reptile cards is Reptile-House-eligible; only 11 of the 25 bird cards are Aviary-eligible, so the Aviary simply comes up less often as a live option. Run the numbers per-card instead of by table frequency and the picture flips: Aviary-eligible birds average about 6.45 appeal for 15.45 cost against a standard-enclosure need of 3.91 spaces, versus reptiles averaging 5.04 appeal for 13.04 cost in 2.2 spaces. Housed in their special enclosure, birds jump from roughly 1.65 to about 5.08 appeal per space (saving ~2.67 spaces per animal) while reptiles go from 2.29 to about 4.67 appeal per space (saving ~1.12 spaces) — birds start from a worse baseline but the Aviary rescues more space per animal than the Reptile House does.

Treat the Aviary as a low-frequency, high-payoff play rather than a standalone strategy to chase from the outset: it costs more per bird and the eligible pool is thinner, but once you're already holding or playing several Aviary-eligible birds — especially the larger, space-hungrier ones — building it converts a real space problem into your most space-efficient enclosure in the game.

🗺️Filling the map

Filling the entire board is a real, achievable target, not just a flex — the two levers that make it feasible are special buildings from sponsor cards, which add structures to the map without eating a normal build action's usual cost and footprint, and an upgraded Build action, which lets each build action place more than one structure at a time so coverage compounds instead of crawling one tile per turn. Chase it when a scoring card you're already building toward rewards it; treat those two levers as the actual bottleneck to solve rather than just building outward opportunistically and hoping the board fills itself.

🌍Diversity conservation projects

When a Species Diversity project (distinct animal type icons) and a Habitat Diversity project (distinct continent icons) are both live, Species Diversity is the softer target even though both share identical thresholds and payouts. Seven animal types exist to draw from against only five continents, and several animals carry two type icons at once, stacking extra overlap on top of the already-wider pool. A zoo that's just naturally diversifying its animal types will clear Species Diversity's thresholds sooner than the equivalent continent spread clears Habitat Diversity's — weight it as the easier, higher-efficiency project to prioritize when you have the choice.

📜Rules traps worth knowing before you plan around them

"Base" conservation project is a strict term

Only the four project cards placed face-up under the board at setup count as "base" projects. A project fetched into hand later — e.g. via the Rhino cards' Assertion ability, which lets you add any unused base project to your hand — does not retain base status once played, even though the fetch text itself calls the source pool "base conservation projects." This matters because Breeding Cooperation/Program lets you discard a token as any one missing icon specifically when supporting a base project — a fetched-then-played project won't trigger that discount. One reported game hinged on exactly this: a player fetched a Habitat Diversity project via Rhino Assertion, held it for the final turn short one continent icon, expected a Breeding token to cover it, was told no because the fetched card wasn't one of the original four — they scored 9 instead of 15 and lost by 2 points. Verify a project is one of the original four before banking a final-turn play on a Breeding-token discount.

Unique-building sponsors need a legal spot

Sponsor cards that come with a unique building (per the rulebook glossary) must have that building actually placed to gain the card's benefit — you can't take the effect and skip the placement. If there's no legal spot for it on your board, you cannot play the card at all, not even for a partial benefit. Confirm your layout has room before committing to, or holding for, one of these.

Conservation projects usually beat Association's other options

When your Association action lets you either support an available conservation project or instead recruit a university/partner zoo, take the project almost every time — it stacks points, a board-clearing bonus (removing an animal marker, sometimes bumping income), and progress toward conservation-track thresholds that unlock extra workers or card upgrades, a combined value university/partner zoo rarely matches. The real exception is when a university or partner zoo is what sets up your engine or unlocks a project in the first place — that's worth sequencing first. A second Association worker is a strong follow-up specifically because it removes this choice entirely: with two workers you can support a project and take a university/partner zoo in the same cycle instead of picking one.

Association power-5 tension

Supporting a conservation project needs your Association card at power 5, but universities and partner zoos are usually taken at lower power and reset the card back to power 1. Every project support therefore costs a full 1→5 cycle — this is the mechanical reason one worker can only push through a handful of project supports per game, and why the "take the project first" default above still has to be balanced against how much runway is left to rebuild the card's power afterward.

Cards & Animals

A card's power is almost entirely contextual — tier lists of "strong cards" are near-useless on their own, because a 2-appeal slow worm can be exactly the right play if it completes the icon set you need. What follows is the evaluative lens, plus the standout cards where the reasoning itself is instructive.

🔍How to evaluate any card

Most cards are situational — some are worth a hand slot early despite being dead weight lategame, others only click once a specific engine piece is already running. Run any card through these lenses before judging it by raw printed stats:

  • Does it convert an action you'd take anyway? The strongest project/sponsor cards ride on top of your natural game plan rather than requiring a dedicated detour — the clearest example is a card that turns "get a partner-zoo animal into play" (something you were likely doing regardless) into bonus conservation points and reputation for free.
  • Does it compound over remaining turns, or pay out once? Cards are built around three different payout timings: an immediate one-time effect (value fixed the moment it triggers), a final-scoring effect (only matters if the condition still holds at game end), or a per-break income effect (compounds with however many breaks are left). A sponsor that supercharges a repeated action multiplies with turns remaining, so land it early; a final-scoring card is a bet on your own board state holding up, not a guaranteed payout.
  • Does it buy flexibility or choice? Cards that let you pick your best-fit project or animal on demand (rather than take whatever you'd otherwise draw) remove mismatch risk and are worth more than their printed stats suggest.
  • Is its value swingy? Some animal abilities draw a random scoring card as payout, worth wildly different amounts depending on the draw. Value these at their average payout across the full range of outcomes — a card that's a blowout one draw in four and a whiff the rest averages out closer to "1-2 points," not the best case you're picturing.
  • Does a repeatable trigger key off your plays only, or anyone's? A partner-zoo-gated bonus only fires on your own plays; a university-gated bonus of the same shape fires off any play at the table (though that only matters in multiplayer) — check which one a card actually reads before assuming they're interchangeable in value.
  • Is it gated behind a threshold you might outgrow? A handful of cards are only playable while your own zoo's appeal stays at or below 25 — treat those as early/mid-game tools with a soft expiration, not cards to save for later.

🃏Standout cards worth knowing

White/Indian Rhinoceros — the game's strongest single ability

The Assertion ability draws and immediately lets you play a conservation project from the deck, functionally letting you choose whichever base project best fits your zoo instead of taking what's on offer — typically worth 4-5 conservation points, sometimes more, for only a size-4 enclosure (most other powerful animals demand size-5). White Rhino edges out Indian Rhino by needing no card-flip requirement to play, giving more lead time to plan around whatever project you end up drawing.

Eagles & King Cobra — Determination

Play for appeal, then immediately take another action — usually a conservation project — for a big tempo swing, or a decisive closing move if you want to end the game on your terms. Both eagles cost only a single Aviary space each, disproportionately cheap for the power level; King Cobra needs less space too but carries more awkward prerequisites to actually get into play.

Engineer & Federal Grants (sponsors)

Engineer supercharges the Build action, snowballing into placement bonuses and a fuller zoo faster — best suited to engine-heavy builds, and its value compounds with turns remaining, so it's strongest played early (it reliably grabs an early 5-appeal placement bonus, roughly on par with 1.67 conservation points). Federal Grants is a strong first sponsor specifically: an immediate cash injection plus a near-free endgame conservation point, and its science icon boosts other science-synergy cards it's played alongside.

Breeding Program & Release of Patents (project/science cards)

Breeding Program converts a play you were likely making anyway — getting a partner-zoo animal into play before the first break — into 2 conservation points and 2 reputation for free; the reputation push also helps hit the 5-rep card-flip and 8-rep extra-worker thresholds sooner. Release of Patents pairs with a double-science university or other science-icon cards to skip straight to a 2-conservation-point card flip — strong enough that it's usually worth playing early whenever it's available, even though the designer balanced it by giving other players at the table a small cash consolation (a multiplayer-only downside).

Veterinarian (sponsor)

A quick cash boost plus an easy endgame conservation point on its own, but its standout use is spending 4 strength to immediately complete a conservation project — especially valuable lategame for chaining a project support and another Association task inside the same action when turns are running out.

Horse (and cheap petting-zoo animals generally)

Don't dismiss cheap animals by face value — evaluate appeal-and-income per money and per build action, and three cheap petting-zoo animals stacked together lift both appeal and income substantially even with zero synergy between them. Horse specifically also grants reputation on top of standard petting-zoo value, pushing it into genuinely strong territory despite its low cost.

Side Entrance

Undervalued at a glance — build first on one side of your board, then drop Side Entrance on the other, and building locations open up almost anywhere. Easy to ramp toward a strong income level quickly, and pairs naturally with a building-heavy strategy chasing the 5-appeal endgame bonus.

Elephants — expensive, swingy, still worth it

African Bush Elephant and Asian Elephant (Asian ranked marginally ahead) are costly and their bonus draw is a coin flip — a useless scoring card some games, a free 3-4 conservation points others — but the expected value still lands around 1-2 conservation points per play, so value the card as if that number were printed on it rather than picturing the best case. As herbivores they also unlock flocking in other herbivores, and playing one early — before anyone's crossed 10 conservation points — keeps both starting endgame-scoring cards live for longer.

Bears as an alternate route to a second worker

Grizzly Bear, Eurasian Brown Bear, and New Zealand Fur Seal are all hard to play early, but their Full-Throated ability is powerful — if you weren't dealt an early Breeding Program or another clean path to a second Association worker, playing one of these bears is a legitimate alternate way to get there. Sun Bear and Raccoon are the easiest bears to bring out and grant an extra Association action instead — less powerful than Determination but still real early tempo, and bears generally carry bonus tags that feed endgame sponsor points too.

Situational specialists — worth it only when the pieces line up

Primatologist and the other animal-type experts (Herpetologist, Ornithologist, Expert in Predators, Expert in Herbivores) are best when they match a base conservation project, but pay out solid money even unmatched, and grant an icon of their animal type as a bonus. Small primates like Senegal Bush Baby and Cotton-top Tamarin carry steep prerequisites (partner zoos, double-science tags) but strong bang-for-buck once met, including early reputation boosts that can be the difference between grabbing an early card flip or worker versus neither. Proboscis Monkey is the extreme case — brutally hard to line up since so few primate cards exist to support it, but worth up to 10 appeal or 7 conservation points when it comes together.

📊Card pool shape (what to expect from the deck)

Birds and reptiles are the most common animal-type tags in the base card pool — strategies built around them have more supporting cards to draw into, making synergy and project requirements easier to hit consistently. Primates are the scarcest tag by a wide margin once dual-tag and duplicate-count cards are factored in; committing early to a primate-heavy plan is a real gamble since there just aren't many cards to draw into. Africa is the single most common continent tag, but the margin over the Americas and Asia is close to negligible — treat all three as strong, viable options rather than hunting for a single "best" continent. The overall tag spread is fairly even, though, so don't write off a rarer tag purely for its scarcity — less competition for it can be its own advantage.

Size-2 enclosures are the most frequently required size across the card pool (31 of 128 cards), ahead of size-1 and size-3 (24 each), with size-4 (20) and size-5 (19) trailing — size-2 is the safe default build since more of the deck can fill it, while committing to size-5 upfront is a narrower bet that only pays off for specific high-value targets you've already lined up. Card cost runs a mean of about 14.4 money with a fairly wide spread (min 4, max 36); holding roughly 13 money already affords half the animal deck, and roughly 17 affords three-quarters — useful budgeting checkpoints when you haven't decided your next play yet. Cost correlates strongly with appeal payout (so pricier cards reliably buy more appeal) but only moderately with conservation points and weakly with reputation — raw cost is a good predictor of appeal value specifically, a much noisier one for conservation, and a poor one for reputation, so align what you're paying for with which track you're actually chasing rather than assuming "expensive = good all-round." By pure appeal-per-money-spent (card cost plus enclosure cost combined), King Vulture, Stoat, and Nile Crocodile top the list — cheap, efficient appeal engines worth knowing even though that ranking ignores their conservation/reputation value entirely. The Petting Zoo is a narrow niche by contrast: only 10 pet-type cards exist total and they don't synergize with other card types, so treat petting-zoo investment as a small, self-contained play rather than a strategy to build around.

🏛️Universities

Provide research tags, a hand-limit increase, a card upgrade, and possibly reputation — but the hand-limit chase is a common trap (see Core Strategy). Default pick order, reasoning by "does this compound or is it conditional":

  1. Hand-size/reputation university first — a bigger hand pays off every subsequent turn once active, and more card options sooner gives your draw/discard/play cycle more potency for the rest of the game.
  2. Single science/reputation university second — moving the reputation track is worth more the earlier you do it, since it unlocks a cascade of further thresholds that compound the sooner you reach them.
  3. Pure double-science university last — two science icons are strong in isolation, but only cash in when you specifically need science for a play already in front of you; without a science-hungry situation on the table, it's the weakest default pick of the three.

Research tags are otherwise binary: they gate some of the strongest sponsors and a specific final-scoring card, but few other cards need them directly. Go all-in on research early, or ignore it — there's little efficient middle ground.

Whether to prioritize a university or a partner zoo with the same Association action is genuinely context-dependent — there's no fixed rule. Lean university when you specifically need the hand-size/reputation/research payoff outlined above; lean partner zoo when you need its continent icon for a card or project you're already holding.

Read a card's continent requirement carefully before assuming a partner zoo covers it. A plain continent icon can be satisfied by any source of that continent's symbol — another animal already in your zoo, an actual partner zoo, or a sponsor card showing that icon. The handshake icon is stricter: it requires an established partner zoo of the matching continent specifically, and nothing else substitutes — an animal card can carry a handshake requirement with no separate continent icon printed on it at all, and the handshake's background color doesn't visually match the continent it needs, so it's easy to misread at the table. The same distinction carries into conservation and sponsor cards: only Breeding Program-type conservation cards require a partner zoo (matching the released/bred animal's continent), while "release into the wild" cards never do; a sponsor card showing a bare handshake-plus-number only requires that many partner zoos total on your board, continent irrelevant.

Maps

Most map-strength data below comes from multiplayer (mostly 2-player) analysis — treat rankings as directional, not gospel, and lean hardest on the terrain/spacing principles, which transfer directly to solo. Where dedicated solo data exists, it's called out on its own, and it doesn't always agree with the multiplayer picture.

🧭What actually makes a map strong or weak

Ark Nova is high-variance — a huge card pool with no deck cycling inside a single game — so the best maps are the ones that reward the plays every game state calls for (build enclosures, play animals, run conservation projects) rather than ones that need one specific draw to pay off. Early tempo and income compound through the rest of the engine, so a same-sized advantage is worth more arriving on turn 3 than turn 15; a fast, flexible opening beats a map with a higher theoretical ceiling that depends on drawing into it.

Two terrain-level read-throughs worth applying to any map before you commit to a build plan on it: placement-bonus geography matters as much as bonus type — a strong bonus tucked in a corner far from your natural build path gets used late or not at all, while a mediocre bonus sitting right at your usual starting spot gets banked reliably every time — and rock/water adjacency drives enclosure flexibility. Terrain split into large, separated zones forces committing to one region early since large standard enclosures need consecutive open space; terrain that interleaves rock and water near the same bonuses lets you build either type without the map forcing your hand.

🥇Silverlake — the standout, by both community and data

Ranked clearly above the field by community tier lists and confirmed independently by statistical regression against real Board Game Arena game data (a model isolating each map's own scoring contribution after controlling for player skill and game length) — first-pass data put it +9.22 points above an average map, and an expanded 533-game follow-up still had it well clear of the rest of the field. The reasoning holds up from both angles: free early building plus two reputation gains reachable before the first break work under almost any opening hand, giving Silverlake more early flexibility than any other map and reducing how much a weak draw can hurt you. Its blocked building spaces are also the easiest in the game to route around — costing at most a temporary loss of a few money, recoverable once your Build action is upgraded.

The map's real signature, though, is its built-in Determination-style endgame bonus: back-to-back Association actions as a map feature, with no Determination-trait animal required, working in nearly every game (and boostable further with X-tokens even though the card cycles back to slot 1). Every other map needs you to actually draw a Determination animal to pull off a comparable double-move finish. Combined with two maxed-out conservation projects double-donated in the same closing sequence, this is the strongest reliable closing move in the map pool — once you're within roughly 30 appeal or 10 conservation points of crossing the finish line on Silverlake, stop building out and start constructing that 1-2 turn close instead of continuing to expand.

📈Where community opinion and the data disagree

Two maps are worth flagging because independent statistical analysis and community perception point in different directions — and in one case, the data itself moved as more games were added, which is its own lesson about trusting a single dataset too far.

Observation Tower — a genuine three-way disagreement

The first-pass regression (308 games) rated Observation Tower as a clearly good map (+4.40, ahead of Ice Cream Parlors), arguing it's underrated at the table: a baseline ~8 appeal easily stretches to 10-12 with a released animal or an aviary/reptile house, and its per-break sponsor-money conservation bonus is strong — its rock/water tile placement is the real weakness, but one that's plannable rather than crippling. A commenter in the same thread pushed back hard, calling it their least favorite map for exactly the same reasons the author downplayed: 8 appeal feels weak next to the alternatives, water splits the map awkwardly, and there are fewer usable rock spaces than the layout wants. When the dataset grew to 533 games, Observation Tower's own coefficient fell from clearly-good to roughly average (+0.19) — the author's own conclusion was that the original small sample had inflated its ranking. Net read: don't write it off as a bad map, but don't bank on the early "underrated gem" verdict either — treat it as an average, terrain-dependent map whose appeal ceiling rewards specifically working around its rock/water split.

Rescue Station — community loves it more than the numbers do

Community tier lists rank Rescue Station solidly A-tier — its dig/tuck bonus (pull from the card row regardless of reputation, tuck a card under the map, draw a replacement) sets up an easy round-1 conservation project or a couple of big late-game ones, strong in almost any build even if slightly less consistent than Silverlake. The statistical regression tells a different story: Rescue Station sat at -0.67 (average) in the first pass and only crept to +0.42 with the expanded dataset — nowhere near the community's near-Silverlake reputation. Part of the gap is likely a data artifact: Rescue Station was excluded from Board Game Arena's ranked arena rotation for a stretch, leaving it with less and lower-quality tracked data than other maps, and the regression author's own attempt to control for that didn't materially change its ranking. The more likely strategic explanation is that its bonus has a high ceiling conditional on your starting hand actually supporting a dig-and-tuck plan, but is unremarkable when your hand doesn't — informed players picking it when their hand fits it will outperform the blind aggregate average by a wide margin. Read it as high-ceiling-if-your-hand-fits rather than uniformly strong.

⚠️Consistently weak

Hollywood Hills and Geographical Zoo land at the bottom of both community tier lists and every pass of the statistical regression alike (Hollywood Hills around -4.81 to -6.09 depending on dataset, Geographical Zoo the single weakest map tested at -9.46/-8.42/-7.48 across passes) — the agreement across independent sources and growing sample sizes makes this the most reliable read in the entire map dataset. Hollywood Hills needs a specific cluster of good Sponsor draws (a dream line being Federal Grants, Zoo School, and Side Entrance together) to really pop, and even players who've logged real time on it report never finding a consistently strong default plan — general sentiment also holds that Sponsors are overvalued in Ark Nova generally, so building a whole map strategy around drawing several great ones compounds a shaky premise. Geographical Zoo's core tension is structural: it rewards spreading across habitat types (up to five separate 1-reputation bonuses, extra appeal, extra money) while most conservation projects reward specializing in one — the map's own incentive fights the scoring incentive, and chasing its bonuses makes you more draw-luck-dependent than on almost any other map.

🎯Maps that look fine multiplayer but cramp solo

Solo-specific data (which separately tracks win rate, average score, and how often a game breaks 30+ points, since a map can win reliably off a low safe average while having no real scoring ceiling) surfaces a pattern the multiplayer rankings above don't fully capture: some maps that read as fine or even good in 2-player data land in the bottom tiers solo because of spacing, not power level.

Park Restaurant is the clearest case — ranked A-tier by the community multiplayer tier list (income without needing animals played at all, several strong early build spots, a special-enclosure map bonus worth roughly $10) — but sits 16th of 18 maps solo, having never been reported breaking 50 points despite many plays. The map's central restaurant tile is genuinely strong but slow to reach without a long-reach sponsor, so its 5th-best-in-the-game money ranking is deceptive: it reflects unspent money sitting idle because the cramped layout has nowhere efficient to build, not an efficient economy. The same cramped spacing regularly locks out strong late-game large animals and a well-fit Aviary/Reptile House. Commercial Harbor tells a similar story: solo data ranks it 15th of 18 despite decent conservation-point output, because the gap between its rock and water zones is so narrow that only two specific routes let an Aviary and Reptile House both reach adjacency — real building has to route through tight chokepoints that make chaining large enclosures across consecutive rounds hard. It does have redeeming synergy (a good university-association bonus that lets you open with partner zoos and take the university later, plus an x2 placement bonus that together enable a lot of Association actions), so it's not dead weight if that specific line comes together — just don't expect its raw stats to reflect its true strength either direction without checking which resource (money or space) is actually your bottleneck.

Hollywood Hills' own solo data confirms the multiplayer read from a different angle: its map gimmick is largely useless solo (no opponents to leverage the interactive parts of Ark Nova's sponsor economy against), and while a later reprint improved its terrain balance and raised its average score and win rate substantially, that same reprint tucked one of the map's best late-game bonuses into an awkward corner and further starved the sponsor-focused side of the map — win consistency went up, but the already-low scoring ceiling got worse, not better. The general lesson across all three: check whether a map's power/ability can actually rescue a bad opening hand (useful) versus whether it's irrelevant once you already have a good one, and verify which resource — money or board space — is your real constraint before trusting a map's raw income or win-rate numbers at face value.

House Rules for Solo Replay

Ark Nova's base solo rules already lean low-luck compared to most euros — but a few spots still hide information or leave outcomes to a blind draw. If you're replaying a map to chase a better score, that residual randomness makes it hard to tell whether a higher number came from a better plan or a better shuffle. These rules strip out what's left, staying as close to the original game as possible.

Philosophy

Stay close to the original rules — deviate only where it removes luck without changing what the game is testing. Prefer revealing hidden information over blind draws, and draw-then-choose over pure chance, so a repeat play is a fair comparison of decisions, not a fairer dice roll.

🗂️Conservation project deck — flip it face-up

The project deck is the biggest source of hidden randomness in solo play, especially behind Assertion-style abilities (White/Indian Rhino) that draw and immediately let you play a project. Lay the whole deck out face-up (or flip it into an open row as space allows) instead of drawing blind. This doesn't add power — Assertion already lets you pick your best-fit project from what's drawn — it just lets you see the pool before deciding whether to trigger the ability at all, and removes the swing of the ability whiffing on a project you can't support yet.

🐘Random-payout animal abilities (Elephant and similar)

A handful of animal abilities draw a random scoring/bonus card as their payout (Elephant is the standout example flagged in Cards & Animals). Two options, in order of preference:

  • Draw-then-choose: draw 3, keep 1 (or whatever ratio feels close to the card's intended variance without eliminating it entirely). Keeps a live decision instead of a flat average.
  • Average-out: if you'd rather not add a mini-decision, just take the known average payout for that ability every time it triggers. Simpler, fully static, best for pure score-comparison replays.

Don't apply this to the deck as a whole (card market, sponsor market) — see below for why those are already fine as-is.

🎴Starting hand — pick your own

For a genuine skill/score comparison across replays, deal yourself a fully open hand instead of a random one: lay out significantly more cards than your hand size (e.g. 2-3× ) face-up and draft freely, or — for a fully static setup when you're specifically trying to beat a previous score on the same map — fix the exact same starting hand every attempt. The latter is the closest Ark Nova equivalent to Feast for Odin's "same board, beat your record" solo loop.

If you'd rather keep some deal-with-what-you-get tension, a lighter middle ground: deal 2 hands, keep the better one (a single mulligan) rather than full open drafting.

🗺️Map — fix it for the run you're comparing

Not a randomness fix (map choice isn't randomized to begin with) but the same replay-integrity logic applies: hold the map constant across attempts you're comparing. Switching maps between "attempts" mixes map strength into the score delta, which defeats the point of tracking improvement. Pick one map for a comparison run (Silverlake is the standout all-rounder per Maps, above) and stay on it until you're done chasing that particular score.

✅What's already fine as-is

Two systems that look random but don't need a house rule, because the game already exposes the information:

  • Card market & sponsor market — both deal into a face-up display you choose from (and Snap/upgraded actions widen or bypass the range entirely). The luck is already "revealed hidden info" by default; blind randomness only lives in what refills behind the display, which you can't see anyway in the paper original and isn't worth tracking against.
  • Turn order / blocking — solo has no opponents contesting actions or board space, so there's nothing to self-block or randomize in the first place. Nothing to house-rule here.
